Abstract

Abstract With the increasing stealth and severity of cyberattacks, the demand for advanced persistent threat (APT) detection systems that deeply understand complex system behaviors has grown substantially. However, existing provenance-based methods often struggle to balance prediction accuracy with inference latency across distributed, resource-constrained environments. To address this, we propose CE-GTC , a collaborative intelligence framework that synergizes large and small models across the cloud-edge continuum. Specifically, the system constructs provenance graphs to capture causal system interactions. In the cloud, a heavy Graph Transformer (the large model) performs computationally intensive representation learning—utilizing edge-feature injection and self-supervised objectives—to extract discriminative embeddings and compact normal behavior profiles. Meanwhile, edge devices host a lightweight clustering detector (the small model) for real-time, privacy-aware anomaly detection via Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)-weighted hybrid distance computation against multi-cluster centers. Experimental evaluations on the Unicorn Wget and StreamSpot datasets demonstrate that CE-GTC achieves superior performance, including an F1-score up to 0.974 and response latency under one second. This highlights the effectiveness of large-small model synergy for scalable, secure, and intelligent APT detection.

Body

Introduction Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs) represent one of the most formidable challenges in modern cybersecurity [ 1 , 2 ]. Unlike conventional attacks, APTs are characterized by their stealthy, multi-stage, and long-duration nature, often orchestrated by well-resourced adversaries targeting critical infrastructure and enterprise networks. These attacks typically infiltrate systems through initial access vectors, establish persistent footholds, and gradually exfiltrate sensitive data over extended periods, making them extremely difficult to detect using traditional signature-based intrusion detection systems. System-level provenance graphs have emerged as a promising representation for capturing the causal dependencies among system entities during execution [ 3 , 4 ]. By encoding system audit logs as directed graphs, provenance-based approaches can holistically model the complex behavioral patterns of APT campaigns. Recent works such as ThreaTrace [ 5 ], MAGIC [ 6 ], and KAIROS [ 7 ] have demonstrated the effectiveness of graph neural networks (GNNs) in learning meaningful representations from provenance graphs to detect anomalous activities indicative of APT intrusions. FLASH [ 8 ] further advanced this line by performing neighborhood-level attack detection via provenance graph representation learning, while LT-ProveGD [ 9 ] proposed a framework that encodes contextual information of dynamic provenance graphs for long-term feature extraction. Moreover, WATSON [ 10 ] introduced a semantics-aware abstraction method for provenance graphs that aggregates contextual behaviors from audit logs, and DEPCOMM [ 11 ] proposed a graph partitioning technique that decomposes large provenance graphs into communities for more interpretable and scalable APT detection. However, existing provenance-based APT detection systems face a fundamental tension between detection capability and computational efficiency. On one hand, powerful graph representation models such as Graph Transformers [ 12 , 13 ] can capture long-range dependencies and complex structural patterns through self-attention mechanisms. Yet, their quadratic computational complexity makes them prohibitively expensive for resource-constrained endpoint devices. Even foundational GNN architectures such as Graph Convolutional Networks [ 14 ] require non-trivial computation for large-scale provenance graphs. Furthermore, continuously transmitting massive volumes of system logs to the cloud for centralized analysis incurs significant bandwidth overhead and introduces severe privacy concerns. On the other hand, lightweight detection methods that can operate in real time on edge devices often sacrifice detection accuracy due to limited model expressiveness. In CE-GTC , sensitive raw audit fields such as file paths, command-line arguments, user names, and IP addresses are abstracted at the edge before the structured provenance graph is sent to the cloud for embedding extraction. Cloud-edge collaborative intelligence, where large models deployed in the cloud synergize with small models on edge devices, provides an ideal architecture for modern distributed security frameworks. The emerging paradigm of edge intelligence [ 15 ] further demonstrates the viability of deploying AI-driven analytics at network endpoints. In alignment with the growing paradigm of synergy between large and small models, we leverage the cloud’s abundant resources to deploy a heavy Graph Transformer (the large model) to extract complex threat intelligence, which is then condensed and distilled into lightweight clustering parameters to empower the edge detector (the small model). This enables real-time, privacy-aware threat detection by abstracting sensitive raw audit fields at the edge before cloud-side embedding extraction. To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose CE-GTC , a cloud-edge collaborative APT detection framework that synergistically combines the powerful representation learning capability of Graph Transformers in the cloud with lightweight clustering-based detection on edge devices. Specifically, our approach operates in two phases: (1) an offline cloud-side phase where a large Graph Transformer model is trained to generate high-quality embeddings of provenance graph patterns, and clustering parameters for normal behavior profiling are derived and packaged for distribution; and (2) an online edge-side phase where endpoint devices perform real-time anomaly detection using only the pre-computed clustering parameters (as condensed knowledge), with new events being sent to the cloud for embedding before local clustering inference. The key contributions of this paper are summarized as follows: We propose CE-GTC , a novel cloud-edge collaborative architecture for APT detection that achieves the synergy between a large Graph Transformer model (cloud side) and a lightweight clustering detector (edge side), enabling real-time threat detection with minimal computational overhead on endpoint devices. We design a provenance graph embedding pipeline based on Graph Transformer that effectively captures both structural and semantic features of system behaviors, producing discriminative representations suitable for downstream anomaly detection. We conduct extensive experiments on the widely used Wget and StreamSpot datasets to validate the effectiveness and efficiency of CE-GTC , demonstrating competitive detection accuracy with significantly reduced edge-side computational requirements. Related work Provenance-based APT detection Provenance graphs, constructed from system audit logs, record causal dependencies among system entities and have become a fundamental data structure for APT detection [ 3 , 4 ]. Early approaches relied on rule-based or tag propagation mechanisms to trace attack behaviors, but these methods struggle with zero-day attacks and scalability issues. Deep learning has catalyzed a new generation of provenance-based intrusion detection systems. ThreaTrace [ 5 ] pioneered GNN-based provenance graph analysis, while Unicorn [ 16 ] proposed a histogram-based approach for real-time detection. KAIROS [ 7 ] introduced a temporal graph network-based encoder-decoder that learns the temporal evolution of provenance structures. MAGIC [ 6 ] leveraged masked graph representation learning to capture multi-granularity anomaly patterns, and FLASH [ 8 ] performed neighborhood-level detection. WATSON [ 10 ] further advanced semantic abstraction by aggregating contextual behaviors from raw audit logs into meaningful provenance patterns. DEPCOMM [ 11 ] addressed scalability through community-based graph decomposition, enabling more interpretable attack investigation on large-scale provenance data. Concurrently, deploying intrusion detection systems on resource-constrained platforms has gained significant momentum. For instance, Hazman et al. [ 17 ] and Eljialy et al. [ 18 ] demonstrated the feasibility of applying deep learning and advanced feature selection methods for efficient anomaly detection in IoT and edge environments. Similarly, temporal representation of reconstruction errors has proven highly effective for network anomaly detection, as shown by Geng et al. [ 19 ]. Despite this progress, existing methods predominantly deploy the entire detection pipeline on a single platform. Our work addresses this limitation through a cloud-edge collaborative architecture that distributes computation according to each tier’s resource characteristics. Graph transformers and representation learning Graph Transformers have emerged as a powerful paradigm for graph representation learning, extending Transformer architectures to graph-structured data. Unlike traditional GNNs [ 14 ] that aggregate information through local neighborhood message passing, Graph Transformers employ self-attention to capture global dependencies, overcoming the over-smoothing problem. Notably, GraphGPS [ 20 ] proposed a general and scalable recipe for Graph Transformers by combining local message passing with global attention, achieving strong performance across diverse benchmarks. Recent advancements have highlighted the versatility of these architectures in complex analytical tasks. Dong et al. [ 21 ] demonstrated the effectiveness of graph pattern matching combined with rule-based inference for anomaly detection. The integration of Transformers has also been successfully extended to automated model design, as seen in GPT-NAS [ 22 ]. Furthermore, the theoretical foundations established by Graph Isomorphism Networks (GIN) [ 23 ] have provided principled guidance on the expressive power of GNN architectures, informing the design of more discriminative graph-level representations. In the context of lightweight processing, which is highly relevant to our edge deployment strategy, He et al. [ 24 ] proposed a selective deep clustering method guided by graph structures (G3DC), drastically reducing computational overhead. Furthermore, knowledge distillation techniques [ 25 ] enable the compression of complex Transformer models for efficient deployment. However, deploying such models across distributed systems introduces new vulnerabilities, such as backdoor attacks in fragment-sharing frameworks [ 26 ], emphasizing the need for robust architectural designs. Cloud-edge collaborative intelligence Cloud-edge collaborative computing has become a vital paradigm for balancing intensive computational capabilities with strict latency requirements, providing a foundational architecture for modern distributed systems.Zhou et al. [ 15 ] provided a comprehensive survey on edge intelligence, systematically categorizing the training and inference paradigms that enable AI deployment at the network edge, laying the theoretical groundwork for cloud-edge synergistic architectures. Efficient resource scheduling and data transmission are critical in this continuum; for instance, Xu et al. [ 27 ] proposed dynamic distributed DNN inference scheduling in edge-cloud networks (CADEC), while Gu et al. [ 28 ] developed efficient cloud data transmission mechanisms based on serverless computing compression. Furthermore, ensuring the security of data exchanged within these distributed setups is paramount, as addressed by Al-Yarimi et al.’s [ 29 ] blockchain-driven secure sharing framework for edge computing. Beyond basic resource allocation, the synergy between cloud and edge heavily relies on knowledge transfer and graph-based correlation across distributed nodes. Recent studies by Qi et al. [ 30 ] have successfully leveraged teacher LLM distillation to guide lightweight edge-side reasoning, providing a strong precedent for large-to-small model synergy. Additionally, simplified graph convolutional networks [ 31 ] and correlation graphs [ 32 ] have been utilized to process complex relational data while preserving privacy and ensuring compatibility. The integration of Transformer architectures with graph neural networks has also been validated in distributed spatiotemporal prediction tasks [ 33 ], and digital-twin technologies [ 34 ] have further expanded the capabilities of edge intelligence in dynamic environments. Moreover, federated learning frameworks such as FedML [ 35 ] have demonstrated efficient model training paradigms across distributed edge nodes, offering complementary strategies for privacy-preserving collaborative intelligence. Collectively, these advances in distributed graph processing, model distillation, and edge inference scheduling provide strong architectural justifications for our proposed framework. Yet, adapting this cloud-edge synergistic intelligence specifically to the unique topological constraints of provenance graphs for APT detection remains largely unexplored, which is the exact gap this paper addresses. Overview This section presents the proposed cloud-edge collaborative APT detection framework, CE-GTC (Fig. 1 ), CE-GTC operates under a two-phase paradigm that synergizes a large model deployed in the cloud with a small model operating on edge devices, embodying the principle of collaborative intelligence between large and small models. Fig. 1 The framework of CE-GTC In the offline cloud-side phase, the system performs three sequential tasks: (1) provenance graph construction , where raw system audit logs are transformed into structured graph representations with rich node and edge attributes; (2) Graph Transformer-based representation learning, where a multi-layer Graph Transformer encoder is trained via self-supervised objectives to produce discriminative graph-level embeddings; and (3) normal behavior profiling, where the embeddings of benign provenance graphs are clustered to establish a compact statistical profile characterizing normal system behavior. In the online edge-side phase, the cloud distributes the trained normal profile—comprising cluster centers, IDF weights, feature normalization parameters, and per-cluster anomaly thresholds—to edge devices as a lightweight parameter package. When a new provenance graph arrives at an edge node, it is first sent to the cloud for embedding extraction via the pre-trained Graph Transformer. The resulting embedding-based detection vector is then returned to the edge, where a simple distance-based comparison against the normal profile determines whether the graph represents anomalous (potentially APT-related) or benign behavior. Therefore, CE-GTC should be understood as an offline cloud-side modeling and online cloud-assisted inference framework, rather than a fully edge-local inference system.This design ensures that the computationally intensive representation learning remains in the resource-rich cloud, while the edge performs only lightweight arithmetic operations for real-time detection. In this process, sensitive raw audit fields such as file paths, command-line arguments, user names, and IP addresses are abstracted at the edge; only structured graph information, including node types, edge types, topology, and statistical features, is sent to the cloud for embedding extraction. Edge-side anomaly detection Once the cloud-side encoder is trained, CE-GTC constructs a lightweight normal behavior profile that can be distributed to edge devices for real-time detection, constituting the small model component of the framework. Feature weighting and multi-cluster profiling Beyond the learned graph embedding and structural statistics, the composite feature vector incorporates two types of reconstruction error signals that serve as additional anomaly indicators. The node reconstruction error for graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G$$\end{document} is computed by averaging the per-node mean squared error over all nodes in the graph: 7 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{recon}}(G) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_G|} \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}_G} \| \hat{\mathbf{x}}(v) - \mathbf{x}(v) \|^2$$\end{document} The graph statistics prediction error captures the dimension-wise squared discrepancy between the predicted and actual graph-level statistics: 8 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{stats}}(G) = \big( \hat{\mathbf{s}}(G) - \mathbf{s}(G) \big)^2 \in \mathbb{R}^{10 + K_v + K_e}$$\end{document} where the squaring is applied element-wise. Both error terms are expected to remain small for normal graphs that conform to the patterns learned during training, while anomalous provenance graphs typically produce elevated reconstruction and prediction errors. These error features thus provide complementary discriminative signals beyond the embedding itself. The full composite feature is then formed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{f}(G) = [\mathbf{g}(G) \parallel \mathbf{s}(G) \parallel \varepsilon_{\mathrm{recon}}(G) \parallel \varepsilon_{\mathrm{stats}}(G)]$$\end{document} . These features are standardized and weighted by Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) scores. For each feature dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$j$$\end{document} , the weight is computed as: 9 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{idf}_j = \log\left(\frac{N+1}{N \cdot \text{df}_j + 1}\right), \quad w_j = \frac{\text{idf}_j}{\sum_k \text{idf}_k}$$\end{document} where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{df}_j$$\end{document} is the proportion of training samples with positive activation in dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$j$$\end{document} . This mechanism amplifies dimensions activated in fewer training graphs—a crucial advantage for APT detection, as anomalous behaviors tend to trigger rarely-used system call dimensions. Real-world enterprise systems exhibit diverse normal operational modes—for example, a web server’s behavior during peak traffic hours differs substantially from its overnight batch-processing pattern, and a database node running scheduled backups produces provenance graphs that are structurally distinct from those generated during interactive query workloads. Representing all benign behaviors with a single centroid would therefore inflate the radius of the normal region and obscure genuine anomalies. To capture this heterogeneity, CE-GTC applies K-Means clustering to the IDF-weighted feature vectors of the training graphs. The clustering is performed in the weighted feature space, where each dimension is scaled by the square root of its IDF weight, so that rare but informative dimensions exert proportionally greater influence on cluster assignment. The optimal number of clusters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K^*$$\end{document} is determined automatically via the Silhouette coefficient. Specifically, the framework evaluates candidate values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K$$\end{document} from 2 up to a configurable maximum (set to 6 by default to avoid over-fragmentation) and selects the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K$$\end{document} that yields the highest average Silhouette score across all training samples. The Silhouette score for each sample measures the contrast between its mean intra-cluster distance and its mean nearest-cluster distance, with values close to 1 indicating well-separated clusters. When the training set is too small to support meaningful multi-cluster analysis, the framework gracefully falls back to a single-cluster configuration, ensuring robustness across varying data scales. After clustering, CE-GTC computes an adaptive anomaly threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau_k$$\end{document} independently for each cluster k. The threshold is derived from the distribution of distances between each training sample and its assigned cluster center, using a configurable strategy such as percentile-based cutoff, mean-plus-standard-deviation, or median absolute deviation. This per-cluster thresholding is essential because different operational modes may exhibit different natural spreads in the feature space: a tightly concentrated cluster of routine system events warrants a stricter threshold than a more dispersed cluster of heterogeneous but still benign activities. If any cluster contains fewer samples than a minimum size threshold, its per-cluster threshold is replaced by a global fallback threshold computed over all training distances. Lightweight detection at the edge The normal profile is serialized into a compact parameter package \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Pi$$\end{document} containing feature normalization parameters, IDF weights, cluster centers \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{c}_k$$\end{document} , and adaptive thresholds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau_k$$\end{document} . This results in a file typically on the order of kilobytes. At detection time, a new provenance graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_{\text{new}}$$\end{document} observed at an edge node is transmitted to the cloud for composite feature extraction. The resulting vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{f}(G_{\mathrm{new}})$$\end{document} is returned to the edge, where it is standardized and weighted to form \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\mathbf{f}}(G_{\text{new}})$$\end{document} . The graph is classified as anomalous if its distance to the nearest cluster center \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{c}_{k^*}$$\end{document} exceeds the corresponding threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau_{k^*}$$\end{document} : 10 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y(G_{\text{new}}) = \mathbb{I}[\text{dist}(\tilde{\mathbf{f}}(G_{\text{new}}), \mathbf{c}_{k^*}) > \tau_{k^*}]$$\end{document} In addition to the binary decision, CE-GTC computes a normalized anomaly score that quantifies how far the observation deviates from its nearest normal cluster relative to the learned tolerance: 11 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A(G_{\mathrm{new}}) = \frac{\mathrm{dist}\!\big(\tilde{\mathbf{f}}(G_{\mathrm{new}}),\, \mathbf{c}_{k^*}\big)}{\tau_{k^*}}$$\end{document} an anomaly score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A(G_{\mathrm{new}}) > 1$$\end{document} indicates anomalous behavior, while a score close to zero signifies high conformity to the normal profile. This continuous score enables operators to prioritize alerts by severity and facilitates downstream threshold tuning without retraining. The entire edge-side decision involves only vector subtraction, element-wise multiplication, and Euclidean distance computation. With an overall complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{O}(D \cdot K)$$\end{document} , it ensures real-time detection capability even on resource-constrained edge devices, completing the synergy between the cloud and the edge. Implementation CE-GTC is implemented in Python 3.8. The experimental environment mainly includes NumPy 1.24.4, PyTorch 2.4.0+cu121, PyTorch Geometric 2.6.1, NetworkX 3.1, and scikit-learn. PyTorch and PyTorch Geometric are used to train the Graph Transformer encoder, NetworkX is used for graph processing and statistical feature extraction, and scikit-learn is used for feature standardization, K-Means clustering, and Silhouette coefficient computation. For model configuration, the Graph Transformer encoder adopts TransformerConv as the basic graph attention layer. The hidden dimension is set to 96, the number of Graph Transformer layers is set to 3, the graph-level embedding dimension is set to 64, and the dropout rate is set to 0.1. The model is trained using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0008, a weight decay of 0.0001, and 20 training epochs. The weights of the node reconstruction loss, graph statistics prediction loss, and embedding compactness loss are set to 1.0, 0.8, and 0.05, respectively. During normal profile construction, all normalization parameters are fitted only on benign training samples and then kept fixed during testing. K-Means clustering is performed in the IDF-weighted feature space. The candidate range of K is set from 2 to 6, and the optimal number of clusters is selected automatically using the Silhouette coefficient. When the training set is too small to support meaningful multi-cluster profiling, the system falls back to a single-cluster profile. The anomaly threshold of each cluster is estimated from the distance distribution between training samples and their assigned cluster center. The default implementation uses a percentile-based threshold, with the percentile set to 80. During testing, the edge node sends the field-abstracted new provenance graph to the cloud for embedding extraction. After receiving the graph-level feature vector from the cloud, the edge node performs anomaly detection using the locally stored normalization parameters, IDF weights, cluster centers, and thresholds. All experiments are conducted under the same data split and parameter configuration to ensure comparability across different experimental settings. Evaluation Dataset This paper evaluates the effectiveness of CE-GTC on the Wget dataset and the StreamSpot dataset [ 36 ]. These datasets differ in scale, source environment, and semantic granularity. Therefore, they enable a relatively comprehensive evaluation of the robustness and generalization ability of CE-GTC . The Unicorn Wget dataset is derived from a controlled supply-chain attack simulation. It is an ideal benchmark for evaluating the detection accuracy of low-and-slow attacks. The Wget dataset contains 150 batches of simulated abnormal logs collected by CamFlow. Among them, 125 batches are normal, and 25 batches contain supply-chain attack records. The normal graphs contain 4,928,165 nodes and 19,182,782 edges, with a total size of about 64 GB. The abnormal graphs contain 714,102 nodes and 3,164,533 edges, with a total size of about 12 GB. The StreamSpot dataset is a public simulated dataset constructed by StreamSpot based on the SystemTap auditing system. It contains 600 batches of audit logs and covers six system-call monitoring scenarios. Each scenario generates 100 independent provenance graphs. The total data size is 2.13 GB. Among the six scenarios, five correspond to normal user activities, such as browsing YouTube and checking Gmail. One scenario corresponds to a drive-by download attack, in which a malicious URL is accessed and a Flash vulnerability is exploited to gain elevated privileges. The value of this dataset lies in its clean scenarios and clear contrast between normal and malicious behaviors. It is therefore well suited for testing whether a detection model can distinguish normal browsing behavior from malicious intrusion behavior, even when the two patterns may appear similar in some aspects (Table 1 ). MAGIC models normal behavior patterns through graph representation learning and can effectively perform deep feature extraction and structural abstraction. Performance analysis In terms of time performance, this study conducts a stage-wise analysis of the computational cost on the cloud side and the edge side based on the implementation workflow of CE-GTC . CE-GTC deploys graph Transformer encoding and model training on the cloud, while feature distance computation, normal pattern clustering, and anomaly decision are executed on the edge. This division is well justified. On the one hand, graph encoding and model training involve multiple TransformerConv layers, node feature reconstruction, and graph statistical feature prediction. These operations are computationally intensive and require substantial computing and storage resources. Therefore, they are more suitable for the cloud environment. On the other hand, the edge side only needs to perform distance computation on mapped test features, cluster assignment, and threshold comparison. This processing pipeline is short and fast, which makes it suitable for online detection in practical scenarios. In the experiments, the number of training epochs is set to 20. This setting is based on repeated experimental observations. When the number of epochs is further increased, the loss decreases much more slowly, while the additional training time and energy consumption continue to increase. Therefore, 20 epochs provide a reasonable trade-off between accuracy and efficiency. The two datasets exhibit clear differences in time cost between the cloud and edge sides (Fig. 2 ). On the Wget dataset, when training and testing are performed from scratch, cloud-side training and encoding take 1446.26 s, while edge-side detection only takes 0.60 s, resulting in a total time of 1477.92 s. When a trained model is reused, the cloud-side time is reduced to only 6.41 s, and the edge-side time is 0.28 s, so the total time decreases to 37.53 s. By contrast, the StreamSpot dataset is smaller in scale. When training from scratch, the cloud-side time is 154.41 s and the edge-side time is 1.05 s, with a total time of 171.71 s. When the trained model is reused, the cloud-side time is 3.60 s and the edge-side time is 0.91 s, and the total time further decreases to 17.67 s. Fig. 2 Time performance comparison of CE-GTC on Wget and StreamSpot Overall, the main time cost of CE-GTC is concentrated in the cloud-side training and graph encoding stage, while edge-side detection consistently maintains low latency. This result shows that the proposed method can preserve strong representation ability for complex graph behaviors while keeping the real-time detection burden at an acceptable level on the edge. Therefore, CE-GTC has good deployment feasibility and practical application value. Ablation experiment To verify the effectiveness of the distance metric module in CE-GTC , we conducted ablation experiments on two datasets, namely Wget and StreamSpot. Considering the critical role of distance computation in anomalous graph discrimination, we focused on comparing two distance modeling strategies: the first is a baseline model using only the IDF distance, while the second is a hybrid distance model that further incorporates the Euclidean distance on top of the IDF distance. The former mainly leverages IDF weighting to characterize the importance differences across feature dimensions, whereas the latter additionally introduces the Euclidean distance to capture global geometric discrepancies, thereby providing a more comprehensive characterization of the distributional deviation between graph representations. Through this comparison, the contribution of the hybrid distance strategy to anomaly detection performance can be systematically evaluated. The hybrid distance model consistently achieves superior detection performance on both datasets (Table 3 ). On the Wget dataset, when only the IDF distance is adopted, the model attains an Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-Score of 0.81, 0.78, 0.87, and 0.82, respectively. After further incorporating the Euclidean distance to construct a hybrid distance metric, these performance indicators improve to 0.933, 0.92, 0.956, and 0.936, respectively. These results indicate that, compared with the single IDF distance, the hybrid distance is more effective in characterizing the discrepancy between anomalous samples and normal patterns, thereby significantly enhancing the model’s capability to identify attack behaviors, with particularly notable gains in Accuracy and F1-Score. By introducing the Euclidean distance, the model not only preserves the discriminative capability of IDF with respect to important features, but also further measures the deviation between graph representations from a global perspective, thereby improving its sensitivity to complex anomalous patterns. Therefore, the hybrid distance design can effectively enhance the anomaly detection performance of CE-GTC and provide strong support for subsequent model construction. Conclusion This paper addressed the fundamental tension between detection capability and computational efficiency in provenance-based APT detection by proposing CE-GTC , a cloud-edge collaborative framework that synergizes a Graph Transformer encoder in the cloud with a lightweight clustering-based anomaly detector on edge devices. The cloud side leverages multi-layer graph attention with edge-feature injection and self-supervised training objectives to produce discriminative graph-level embeddings, while the edge side performs real-time anomaly detection using only a compact parameter package comprising IDF-weighted feature vectors, multi-cluster centers, and adaptive per-cluster thresholds, with an overall complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{O}(D \cdot K)$$\end{document} . Extensive experiments on the Unicorn Wget and StreamSpot datasets validated the effectiveness of CE-GTC . On the Wget dataset, CE-GTC achieved an accuracy of 0.933 and an F1-score of 0.936, outperforming Unicorn, FLASH, and RT-APT. On the StreamSpot dataset, CE-GTC attained a precision of 1.00 and an F1-score of 0.974, surpassing all compared methods including MAGIC. The stage-wise time analysis further confirmed that edge-side detection latency remains consistently below one second. These results demonstrate that the three key designs—Graph Transformer encoding for long-range dependency capture, IDF-based weighting for amplifying discriminative dimensions, and multi-cluster profiling for accommodating diverse normal behaviors—work synergistically to achieve both high detection accuracy and practical deployability. Despite these promising results, several limitations remain. The current framework depends on network connectivity for cloud-side embedding extraction, which may introduce latency in extremely high-throughput environments. Additionally, the evaluation was limited to two benchmark datasets, and the static normal profile cannot incrementally adapt to concept drift in benign behavior. Future work will explore knowledge distillation techniques to enable fully local edge inference, incorporate incremental learning mechanisms for dynamic profile updates, and extend the evaluation to larger-scale real-world datasets such as the DARPA Transparent Computing benchmarks to further validate the scalability and robustness of the framework.
