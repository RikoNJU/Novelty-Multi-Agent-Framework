Abstract

Abstract With the emergence of advanced large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, state-of-the-art social bots can now generate coherent text, dynamically adapt behavioral patterns, and mimic human user profiles with high fidelity. However, existing detection methods predominantly rely on static representations and unimodal decision-making schemes, thereby exhibiting significant limitations when confronting LLM-driven social bots. To address these limitations, we propose BotEvo, a novel bot detection framework grounded in behavioral evolution modeling and cross-modal fusion. BotEvo integrates a dynamic weighting scheme to quantify the contribution of individual posts, an implicit gating mechanism for deep fusion of text, behavioral, and sentiment features, and a two-stage threshold optimization strategy for adaptive, risk-stratified classification. Experimental evaluations on the Botsim and Twibot-20/SDAR datasets demonstrate that BotEvo attains F1 scores of 96.46% and 89.30%, respectively, consistently surpassing 14 state-of-the-art baselines.

Body

Introduction The rapid expansion of social media has fundamentally transformed information dissemination and public discourse. By early 2025, the total number of social media user accounts worldwide had surpassed 5.31 billion (We Are Social, Meltwater 56 ). Social bots, which are automated algorithms that produce content and engage with users, have attracted sustained academic scrutiny owing to their capacity to distort online public opinion (Grimme et al. 25 ; Ferrara et al. 23 ; Shen et al. 52 ; Davis et al. 10 ; Ariza 4 ). Over the past few years, progress in large language models (LLMs), represented by platforms like GPT-4 and Claude, has markedly improved the quality and fluency of machine-generated content. Nevertheless, these capabilities have also been exploited by malicious actors to deploy social bots that are increasingly human-like in behavior. The proliferation of such advanced bots exacerbates the risks of misinformation dissemination (Anwar et al. 3 ) and opinion engineering (Radivojevic et al. 47 ), posing critical threats to the integrity and authenticity of public discourse (Myers et al. 43 ; Yang and Menczer 60 ; Qiao et al. 46 ; Li et al. 36 ). Traditional models apply a single fixed threshold (red line) to classify all users, yet the risk distributions across different user groups differ substantially: for high-risk groups, a lenient threshold may cause missed detections, whereas for ordinary groups, a stringent threshold may trigger false positives. The adaptive threshold (green line) proposed in this work can be dynamically tuned based on the risk distribution of each group, which improves classification performance Traditional bot detection approaches can be divided into three primary paradigms: feature-based, text-based, and graph-based methods (Aljabri et al. 2 ; He et al. 28 ; Liu et al. 38 ). Feature-based methods extract behavioral, content, and network-level features via manual or automated procedures for classification (Kudugunta and Ferrara 34 ; Yang et al. 59 ), but their effectiveness is frequently compromised when confronting bots that closely imitate human behavior or employ adversarial evasion strategies (Li et al. 36 ; De Nicola et al. 11 ). Text-based methods apply Natural Language Processing (NLP) to analyze semantic and sentiment patterns in user-generated content (Wei and Nguyen 57 ; Dukić et al. 16 ; Heidari and Jones 26 ), yet their discriminative power diminishes when content is refined or synthesized by large-scale generative models, such as through paraphrasing or stylistic imitation (Cresci 8 ; Ferrara 22 ). Graph-based methods model social interactions as graph structures and employ Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to detect anomalous coordinated behaviors (Feng et al. 18 ; Lei et al. 35 ; Feng et al. 17 ; Ye et al. 64 ; Feng et al. 20 ; Hu et al. 30 ; Li et al. 37 ), serving as a vital means for detecting organized bot campaigns (Huo et al. 31 ; Reiche et al. 48 ). However, these approaches predominantly depend on static feature engineering (Yang et al. 62 ) and struggle to counteract the contextual understanding capabilities and sophisticated linguistic features exhibited by LLM-driven bots (Di Paolo et al. 13 ; Kim et al. 32 ; Ng and Carley 44 ; Sayyadiharikandeh et al. 50 ). As LLM-generated content has shown persuasive power comparable to or even exceeding human writing (especially in socio-political discussions Carrasco-Farre 7 ; Sun et al. 53 ; Herbold et al. 27 ), detection methods specifically targeting LLM-driven bots have emerged as a central focus of contemporary research. These approaches aim to reveal subtle anomalies across multiple behavioral dimensions. For example, LLM-BotGuard (Duan et al. 14 ) leverages the ability of large language models to capture complex linguistic patterns, while incorporating graph neural networks to analyze the relational and structural patterns used by LLM-driven bots. Likewise, BotDMM (Duan et al. 15 ) adopts a dual-channel multimodal learning framework to identify LLM-driven bots on social media, focusing specifically on both the linguistic fluency of LLM-generated text and the dynamic changes in social interaction patterns over time. Furthermore, recent efforts have begun to incorporate richer behavioral indicators beyond basic follower-following metrics to improve detection accuracy (Reiche et al. 48 ). Collectively, these studies represent the forefront of contemporary bot detection methodologies. Despite these advances, existing approaches remain subject to three critical limitations. First, although multimodal fusion is increasingly adopted, current models frequently fail to capture the deep inter-modal correlations and behavioral evolution patterns inherent in users’ cross-modal behaviors. Second, most detection systems employ fixed, uniform decision thresholds, lacking adaptability across diverse user populations (Feng et al. 21 ; Wang et al. 55 ), potentially leading to under-detection of high-risk accounts and misidentification of benign users (as illustrated in Fig. 1 ). Third, prevailing deep learning models suffer from the “black box” problem (Peng et al. 45 ), which obscures the decision rationale and key discriminative features, thereby impeding in-depth analysis of LLM-driven bot behavior patterns. To address these challenges, we propose BotEvo, a novel framework for LLM-driven social bot detection grounded in behavioral evolution modeling and multimodal feature fusion. The framework first extracts post-level features through a data preprocessing module, and then computes a set of post weights for each user. Next, it builds multi-view user embeddings by capturing both static attributes and temporal behavioral patterns concurrently, producing user-level representations. Finally, it captures cross-modal correlations via an implicit gating-based fusion approach, and incorporates a two-stage threshold optimization strategy to balance detection performance across diverse user categories. The principal contributions of this work are as follows: We introduce BotEvo, an LLM-driven bot detection framework that incorporates temporal-aware feature aggregation. The core objective is binary classification (human vs. LLM-driven bot); a multi-class extension ( C = 3 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C\!=\!3$$\end{document} ) is provided as a supplementary formulation for datasets containing heterogeneous bot types. This framework effectively models the behavioral evolution of user activities through effective fusion of heterogeneous information (textual content, behavioral traces, and sentiment signals) and adaptive decision thresholds customized to different user risk profiles. This design significantly reduces both false negative and false positive rates. We identify and characterize the discriminative behavioral signatures of LLM-driven bots, with a particular focus on sentiment consistency and temporal behavioral regularity. Extensive experiments on the Botsim and Twibot-20/SDAR benchmarks demonstrate that BotEvo attains F1 scores of 96.46% and 89.30%, respectively, consistently outperforming 14 state-of-the-art baselines by substantial margins. We present an efficient, graph-agnostic detection paradigm. Related work Social bots employ automated technologies to mimic human-like behavior, enabling covert operations, opinion manipulation, and purposeful influence (Feng et al. 19 ). This section reviews detection advances from traditional approaches to emerging LLM-driven bot detection techniques. Social bot detection Traditional approaches to social bot detection can be categorized into three primary paradigms: feature-based, text-based, and graph-based methods. Feature-based methods, the core paradigm of early research, extract manually designed features from account metadata, behavioral patterns, and content attributes for classification. For example, Gilani et al. ( 24 ) fused multi-dimensional features to improve classification accuracy, and Yang et al. ( 61 ) developed Botometer, which integrates thousands of features and has become a representative tool of this paradigm. However, Cresci et al. ( 9 ) revealed a fundamental limitation: evolutionary algorithms can artificially manipulate bot feature distributions, causing static-rule-based systems to fail. Text-based methods seek breakthroughs from the deep semantic patterns of bot-generated content. With breakthroughs in NLP technology, researchers began to use deep neural networks (such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) Kudugunta and Ferrara 34 ) and pre-trained language models (such as Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) Heidari and Jones 26 ; Saravani et al. 49 ) to model post sequences to capture subtle anomalies in bot text in grammar, syntax, or sentiment. Such methods have achieved high accuracy on specific datasets (Deshmukh et al. 12 ). However, recent research points out that the latest bots can generate highly anthropomorphic or even plagiarized human text (Liu et al. 38 ), making the detection boundary increasingly blurred when relying solely on text content analysis, forcing researchers to turn their attention to social relationships and group behavior patterns that can better reflect the essence of bots. Graph-based methods identify bots by modeling interaction relationships such as following, retweeting, and mentioning between users, becoming the current mainstream direction. Its development has gone through a deepening from simple graph models to complex graph neural networks. Early work (Ferrara et al. 23 ) revealed that next-generation social bots disguise themselves by forming tight social graph structures. Subsequently, Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) (Kipf and Welling 33 ) were introduced to this task (Ali Alhosseini et al. 1 ), achieving automatic learning of node representations. To cope with the inherent heterogeneity of social networks, heterogeneous graph-based methods (Feng et al. 18 , 20 ; Cai et al. 6 ; Yang et al. 63 ) significantly improved multi-source information fusion and coordinated behavior detection capabilities through type-aware message passing mechanisms. Although graph-based methods have made significant progress, their performance heavily depends on high-quality, complete explicit social topology structures (Peng et al. 45 ). LLM-Driven bot detection With the rapid advancement of large language model technologies, exemplified by ChatGPT and GPT-4, sophisticated text generation and conversational capabilities have been increasingly leveraged to develop next-generation social bots (Feng et al. 21 ). Significantly enhanced human-like behavior is exhibited by these LLM-driven bots, posing unprecedented challenges to existing detection mechanisms. Based on the level of bot sophistication addressed, current research in bot detection can be categorized into three evolving paradigms: static attribute detection, dynamic behavior detection, and social context consistency detection. Static attribute detection focuses on identifying surface-level textual features to address early-stage content anthropomorphization in bot-generated text (Li et al. 36 ). This paradigm assumes that measurable statistical deviations in fixed linguistic characteristics, such as lexical distribution and syntactic structure, are exhibited by text produced by large language models. Discriminative signals have been extracted from metrics including text perplexity (Xu et al. 58 ), structured narrative patterns (Feng et al. 21 ), and semantic complexity and diversity (Li et al. 36 ). For instance, a systematic analysis of distinguishing features between human-authored and AI-generated content using ChatGPT outputs was conducted by Mindner et al. ( 42 ). Valuable insights into effective feature dimensions for detecting LLM-driven bots are provided by this work. While these methods are effective for early-generation bots, their performance deteriorates substantially when advanced bots optimized through prompt engineering and iterative refinement are encountered. Dynamic behavior detection aims to identify anomalies in the temporal patterns of user activities, addressing the intermediate challenge posed by evolving bot strategies (Li et al. 36 ). This paradigm shifts focus from static content analysis to the modeling of behavioral evolution over time. Non-human activity rhythms and adaptive behavioral adjustments are sought to be uncovered through the analysis of account-level behavioral sequences. The LLM-BotGuard framework was proposed by Duan et al. ( 14 ), which captures behavioral dynamics by extracting time-series behavioral features and quantifying their variability using entropy-based measures. However, sufficient temporal resolution is still lacked by existing approaches, making it difficult to accurately characterize how bot behaviors adapt in response to environmental stimuli or platform dynamics. Social context consistency detection targets coordinated group behaviors that deviate from naturally emerging interaction patterns in large-scale networks (Li et al. 36 ). The core assumption is that while individual bots may generate highly plausible content, their collective interactions often display structural or temporal regularities inconsistent with those observed among genuine users (Zhou et al. 66 ; Duan et al. 14 ). An empirical study on the Chirper.ai platform was presented by Zhu et al. ( 68 ), offering preliminary evidence supporting this line of research. Nevertheless, complete social topology information is typically required by these methods, limiting their practical utility in real-world settings where network data are often incomplete. Despite representing recent progress in detection technology, critical limitations are still faced by frameworks such as LLM-BotGuard and BotDMM. Although type-specific expert modules are incorporated into LLM-BotGuard, its behavioral modeling relies primarily on static feature representations. The temporal progression of user behavior cannot be fully captured by this design. Moreover, performance degradation is caused by its dependence on explicit network graph structures when topological information is sparse. A dual-channel architecture is adopted by BotDMM to process content and structural features separately. However, simple vector concatenation is employed by its feature fusion mechanism. Conditional dependencies across modalities cannot be modeled through this approach. Furthermore, overall feature separability is prioritized by its contrastive learning objective, without differentiation between high-risk and low-risk user groups. Suboptimal decision boundaries in heterogeneous populations may be caused by this design. In summary, state-of-the-art methods for LLM-driven bot detection confront two fundamental challenges: (1) insufficient granularity in modeling temporal behavioral dynamics, which limits the ability to detect strategic adaptations over time; and (2) simplistic fusion strategies (predominantly linear combinations) that fail to capture inter-modal interactions. The BotEvo framework proposed in this work addresses these shortcomings through three principled design choices. First, it uses a dynamic weighting mechanism to model behavioral evolution via multi-factor temporal adaptation. Unlike the static representations in LLM-BotGuard, this mechanism can effectively adapt to temporal patterns in user activity. Second, it enables deep cross-modal integration via an implicit gating fusion strategy that learns conditional dependencies between modalities, surpassing the simple concatenation used in BotDMM. Finally, it operates independently of complete social graph structures, eliminating the topological dependency shared by GCN, GAT, and BotRGCN-family methods, thereby enhancing robustness and scalability in real-world deployment scenarios. Preliminaries Problem definition Multimodal behavioral data are generated by users’ social media activities, encompassing textual content, temporal patterns, and sentiment expressions. Each post serves as a fundamental unit that captures a user’s linguistic output, action type, and implicit sentiment at a specific point in time. Collectively, these heterogeneous, time-stamped data points form a distinctive behavioral profile for users in the digital environment. In this work, the detection of LLM-driven bots is formalized as a user-level M-classification task. Methodology This section proposes the BotEvo framework, which is centered on behavioral evolution modeling and implicit fusion of multimodal features. BotEvo begins by decomposing raw user data into three types of post-level features through a data preprocessing module, then applies a dynamic weighting mechanism that incorporates multi-factor constraints to generate user-specific feature weight sets. Next, using these weighted features as input, the collaborative representation module constructs complementary static attribute embeddings and dynamic behavior embeddings via sub-modules, thereby forming comprehensive multi-view user representations. To address the challenge of fusing heterogeneous representations, BotEvo adopts an implicit gating fusion strategy, dynamically associating text logic features with behavioral features through an adaptive weight allocation mechanism to achieve complementary enhancement of cross-modal information. Finally, BotEvo feeds the fused unified representation into a classifier and employs a two-stage threshold optimization mechanism to balance detection accuracy across categories. Experiment Experiment setup Datasets To evaluate the model performance across diverse bot types and scenarios, this experiment selects two representative datasets: Botsim-24 (Qiao et al. 46 ) and a mixed dataset combining Twibot-20 (Feng et al. 19 ) with SDAR (Li et al. 36 ). Botsim-24 integrates real Reddit accounts with LLM-driven bots (Qiao et al. 46 ). A total of 2907 accounts are included in this dataset. Among them, 1907 are human accounts and 1000 are bot accounts. The Twibot-20/SDAR mixed dataset consists of labeled samples from two sources. It includes 11826 labeled samples from Twibot-20 (Feng et al. 19 ). It also includes 3000 LLM-generated samples from SDAR (Li et al. 36 ). These 3000 samples are labeled as LLM-driven bots. The mixed dataset is constructed following the methodology of BotDMM (Duan et al. 15 ). It is worth noting that the Twibot-20/SDAR mixed dataset encompasses three distinct types of users: LLM-driven bots, traditional bots, and humans. Consequently, the related experiments conducted on this dataset are formulated as three-way classification tasks. For both datasets, the data is split into training, validation, and test sets. The split ratio is 70 percent for training, 20 percent for validation, and 10 percent for testing. Stratified sampling is adopted to ensure consistent class distribution across each subset. Evaluation metrics and implementation details Evaluation metrics To evaluate model performance, we employ Accuracy (Acc), F1 score (F1), and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC). These metrics collectively assess classification accuracy, robustness to class imbalance, and the correlation between predicted and true labels. Specifically, the combination of F1 and MCC provides a compact yet informative evaluation of classification quality under class imbalance. This choice is particularly suitable for bot detection, where controlling false negatives is practically important because missed bot accounts may lead to disproportionate downstream risks in real-world deployment.
