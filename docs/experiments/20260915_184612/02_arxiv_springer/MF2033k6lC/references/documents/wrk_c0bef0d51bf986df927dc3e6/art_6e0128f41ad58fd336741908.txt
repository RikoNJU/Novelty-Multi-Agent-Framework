Abstract

Abstract Temporal networks have gained significant prominence in the past decade for modelling dynamic interactions within complex systems. A key challenge in this domain is Temporal Link Prediction (TLP), which aims to forecast future connections by analysing historical network structures across a range of applications. While existing surveys have addressed specific aspects of TLP, they typically lack a comprehensive framework that distinguishes between representation and inference methods, the two key ingredients of TLP. This survey bridges this gap by introducing a novel taxonomy that explicitly examines representation and inference in existing methods, providing a novel classification of approaches for TLP. We analyse how different representation techniques capture temporal and structural dynamics, examining their compatibility with various inference methods for link prediction tasks. Our taxonomy not only clarifies the methodological landscape but also reveals promising unexplored combinations of existing techniques. This taxonomy provides a systematic foundation for tackling emerging challenges in TLP, including model explainability and scalable architectures for complex temporal networks.

Introduction A temporal network is a network whose structure and properties evolve over time. In the past decade, temporal networks have been used to model interactions between the components of complex systems with dynamic structures and characteristics. A temporal network consists of two key elements: a set of nodes representing components of a system and a set of links indicating interactions between pairs of nodes. In temporal networks, links between nodes may appear or disappear over time, and new nodes may also join. Broadly, temporal networks are represented in two paradigms: snapshot-based and continuous-time (event-based). Snapshot-based networks discretise time into intervals, yielding a sequence of static graphs, which is intuitive but may lose fine-grained temporal information. In contrast, continuous-time networks preserve the exact timestamps of interactions (events), enabling models to capture dynamics at arbitrary resolutions [ 1 , 2 ]. This distinction forms the foundation for most temporal representation learning approaches and is particularly relevant to temporal link prediction (TLP). Identifying the likelihood of connecting between nodes to predict future links in a network (link prediction) is a fundamental problem that can be applied to various domains such as social networks [ 3 ], telecommunication networks [ 4 ], traffic forecasting [ 5 , 6 ] or knowledge graphs [ 7 , 8 ]. Link prediction was first discussed in the context of static networks [ 9 ] where the aim has been to predict future links based on a network’s current state. In this paper this is referred to as traditional link prediction. However, TLP uses a network’s historical states to predict the likelihood of future links. Incorporating historical dynamics into the TLP problem introduces additional complexity [ 10 , 11 ]. In social networks for instance, such as the one shown in Fig. 1 , the TLP task is to predict the likelihood of a future link between User 1 and User 3 based on the observed temporal interactions among users. Fig. 1 Temporal link prediction in a social network Recently, numerous methods have been proposed to address the TLP problem, each method focusing on different network characteristics. Solving TLP requires two key ingredients: first, a representation unit , which models how a temporal network can be captured to reflect its traits and historical dynamics; and second, an inference unit , which determines the likelihood of future links based on the learned representation. Since all TLP models necessarily need both units, this review is confined to approaches concerning either representation or inference. Traditional surveys often categorise TLP models into broad methodological families such as “learning-based” [ 11 ]. This perspective provides a useful overview of existing approaches, but tends to emphasise overall model families rather than disentangling specific design choices. For instance, both TGN [ 1 ] and EvolveGCN [ 12 ] are typically labelled as “learning-based,” yet their underlying assumptions differ markedly: TGN employs a continuous-time message passing representation units with attention-based units making it effective for modelling of rapidly evolving interactions, whereas EvolveGCN adopts a sequence of discrete snapshots with RNN-based updates of GCN parameters, which is more suitable for gradually evolving networks. In the paper, TLP models are classified according to whether their primary contribution is to representation or inference; the defining attributes in each unit form the basis for the taxonomy adopted here. By separating representation and inference units, our taxonomy highlights such distinctions more explicitly, thereby clarifying how models align with different data characteristics and task requirements, and revealing unexplored combinations. The survey begins by examining various representation methods and discusses their merits and challenges. It then classifies and evaluates the inference approaches that have been explored in the literature. Guided by this methodological perspective, the discussion is subsequently divided into two sections “ Review of Representation Units for TLP ” and “ A Review of Inference Units for TLP ” dedicated to each unit, respectively. The survey highlights potential research opportunities through two primary avenues: (i) unexplored combinations of existing representation and inference units and (ii) the development of novel unit designs that could introduce new computational paradigms for either representation or inference. These opportunities could lead to more effective TLP approaches. Various surveys have been conducted to review TLP studies. Some surveys concentrate on specific network structures, such as homogeneous networks [ 13 , 14 ], or directed networks [ 15 ]. Other surveys focus on the application of TLP across various domains, encompassing temporal knowledge graphs [ 16 ] and social networks [ 3 , 17 , 18 ]. Our survey sets itself apart in several aspects: The survey introduces a novel taxonomy that distinguishes between the representation and inference units. Unlike existing surveys [ 13 , 14 – 15 ] which classify models under a single hierarchical framework, our approach separates these components to provide deeper insights into the models’ applicability and performance variations whilst also exploring diverse network characteristics pertinent to the TLP problem. The survey provides a comprehensive review of the representation unit. While previous surveys [ 3 , 17 ] provided some discussions of these models, we offer a systematic classification and analyse their ability to capture temporal and structural dynamics, thereby addressing a significant gap in the literature. The survey identifies current challenges and outlines potential directions for future research in representations, network characteristics, and TLP methodologies, offering valuable guidance for subsequent investigations in this field. Methods included in this survey come from papers identified using Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar and keywords on temporal networks, network representation and link prediction. Only articles written in English in the period 2000–2025 that propose relevant algorithms or methods were considered. The rest of this paper is organised as shown in Fig. 2 . “ Background ” presents the background on temporal graph representation and the problem statements. Following this, “ Taxonomy ” introduces the taxonomy framework. “ Review of Representation Units for TLP ” and “ A Review of Inference Units for TLP ” provide an overview of TLP methods, classifying them based on their representation and inference units. “ Variations of the TLP Problem ” and “ Future Research Directions ” explore variations of the TLP problem and outline potential directions for future research. Finally, “ Conclusion ” presents the survey’s conclusion. Representation Methods Temporal graphs can be described using two distinct approaches for the time dimension: Discrete-time Dynamic Graphs (DTDG): A DTDG simplifies a temporal network into a sequence of timestamped snapshots, converting the continuous temporal space into discrete timestamps. This allows dynamic graphs to be represented in a static, analysable format, though some information may be lost between snapshots during discretisation. Continuous-time Dynamic Graphs (CTDG): This method abstains from discretising the time dimension. Instead, it characterises the presence of all elements independently within a continuous temporal space, preserving the inherent temporal continuity of temporal networks. Summary This section introduced the foundations of temporal networks and the problem formulation of TLP. Temporal networks are modelled as temporal graphs, where the evolution of nodes, edges, and attributes can be described in either discrete-time or continuous-time paradigms, each offering different trade-offs between efficiency and information preservation. TLP extends traditional link prediction by requiring models to capture both spatial and temporal dynamics for forecasting future interactions. We further distinguished between transductive and inductive inference tasks, highlighting their respective assumptions and generalisation scopes. Overall, this background sets the stage for understanding the methodological developments reviewed in the following sections. In particular, the distinction between discrete-time and continuous-time representations, as well as between transductive and inductive inference tasks, provides the foundation for the taxonomy that follows. Taxonomy The Framework of the Taxonomy This survey proposes a taxonomy framework for TLP. It contends that all TLP methods comprise two sub-tasks: representing temporal networks and making predictions, which are processed by two distinct components: the Representation Unit and the Inference Unit. The representation unit captures the network structure and dynamics, whilst the inference unit leverages these representations to forecast future links. As depicted in Fig. 5 a, nodes, edges, and attributes are processed by a representation unit to produce inputs that capture dynamic patterns. Subsequently, the inference units process features or embeddings derived from the representation unit to infer future links. In [ 26 ], transductive and inductive tasks relate to inference units but not to graph representation. Inference units are the methods employed to process graph representations derived from representation units and learn dynamic patterns of temporal networks for TLP. Relationship of Representation Unit and Inference Unit As illustrated in Fig. 5 a, data are first transformed by the Representation Unit into features or embeddings that capture structural and temporal dynamics. These representations are then processed by the Inference Unit to produce link predictions, often in conjunction with auxiliary components such as candidate generation or negative sampling [ 1 , 81 ]. A TLP model, therefore, relies on the alignment between the expressive capacity of its Representation Unit and the predictive capability of its Inference Unit. For example, simple feature extraction in the Representation Unit, such as similarity or centrality measures, is typically paired with lightweight inference techniques such as time series models or distance-based scoring. In contrast, latent states derived from graph neural network encoders or continuous-time event-driven models usually require parametric inference modules, including multilayer perceptrons, recurrent decoders, or attention-based predictors. It is also important to note that many approaches to Representation Units and Inference Units are rooted in neural network architectures. Graph neural networks, recurrent networks, and attention mechanisms are different forms of neural computation that can be employed in either stage. Consequently, TLP models often integrate these components in diverse ways, combining representation and inference modules into flexible and powerful end-to-end architectures. This neural foundation provides both strong approximation ability and architectural adaptability, allowing researchers to design models that are tailored to specific temporal settings while retaining the capacity to generalise across a wide range of prediction tasks. Temporal Graph Benchmarks In recent years, significant progress has been made toward standardising evaluation in temporal graph link prediction, most notably through the Temporal Graph Benchmark (TGB) [ 82 ] and its extension TGB 2.0 [ 83 ] on multi-relational and heterogeneous temporal graphs. These benchmarks provide datasets, unified preprocessing pipelines, and public leaderboards, 1 enabling systematic comparisons among a wide range of methods. TGB includes datasets from diverse domains. For example, a dataset on Wikipedia co-editing interactions focuses on predicting which page a user will edit at a given time [ 84 ]. Another dataset on Amazon product reviews considers the task of predicting the next product a user will review [ 85 ]. A financial dataset based on stablecoin ERC20 transactions requires forecasting the destination address of a transfer, including cases during the Terra Luna collapse [ 86 ]. A social media dataset constructed from Reddit reply dynamics predicts whether one user will respond to another [ 87 ]. Finally, a dataset on international flights predicts whether a flight will occur between two airports on a specified day [ 88 ]. Collectively, these datasets span domains such as collaborative editing, e-commerce, finance, social media, and transportation, highlighting the broad range of real-world applications for TLP. Both TGB and TGB 2.0 report systematic comparisons of TLP methods, ranging from classical heuristics and static baselines to state-of-the-art temporal graph neural networks such as JODIE, TGN, and CAW. The leaderboard results highlight that while sophisticated temporal models often achieve strong predictive performance, simple memory-based heuristics also remain competitive, especially on large-scale datasets. Summary Building on the distinctions introduced in the previous section, this taxonomy frames TLP methods around two complementary units: the Representation Unit and the Inference Unit. The Representation Unit reflects the discrete-time vs continuous-time modelling paradigm, focusing on how temporal networks are represented and encoded. The Inference Unit corresponds to the transductive vs inductive inference setting, determining how predictions are carried out given the learned representations. By combining these two dimensions, we categorise existing methods into four principal groups, providing a coherent structure for understanding these approaches. This design not only ensures consistency with the conceptual foundations of temporal networks but also highlights open challenges, such as bridging hybrid Representation and Inference Units, balancing representational fidelity and efficiency, and enabling models to generalise across diverse TLP scenarios. Review of Representation Units for TLP Discrete-time Dynamic Graphs Methods Snapshots The Snapshots approach is a fundamental method employed in the domain of discrete-time dynamic graphs for TLP. This technique directly inputs network temporal adjacency matrices into the inference unit. The snapshots are treated as a sequence of static networks and are structurally represented by adjacency matrices. This method is effective for tasks that resemble time-series inference. The direct use of snapshots simplifies the representation and processing of dynamic graphs, making it an efficient choice for many application scenarios. Matrix Factorisation (MF) MF, also known as matrix decomposition, decomposes historical adjacency matrices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf{A}}^\tau _{\tau - \Delta ^{\prime }}$$\end{document} or their transformations to extract latent features of temporal networks. These features can then be combined with other inference units or make direct inferences of future snapshots. Several matrix factorisation techniques have been applied to TLP with considerable success. These include Eigenvalue Decomposition (ED), Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), Tensor Factorisation (TF), and Non-negative Matrix Factorisation (NMF), all of which are frequently used as task-dependent dynamic network embedding methods for TLP. There are static and dynamic approaches for the random walk in discrete-time dynamic graphs for TLP. Static random walk methods model each snapshot independently to generate embeddings, which are then used by the representation units for TLP. Well-known algorithms that apply this approach include DeepWalk [ 40 ] and Node2Vec [ 41 , 107 ]. Static Methods DeepWalk [ 40 ] and Node2Vec [ 41 ] are influential graph analysis methods that utilise random walks to learn node embeddings, drawing inspiration from Skip-gram model [ 108 ]. DeepWalk generates node sequences through random walks and employs the Skip-gram model to capture higher-order proximity between nodes, effectively preserving local connectivity patterns. In contrast, Node2Vec extends DeepWalk’s methodology by introducing biased random walks that balance breadth-first and depth-first search [ 109 , 110 ], enabling the model to capture both local and global topological information. This makes Node2Vec particularly adept at exploring complex network structures. [ 107 ] first applied Node2Vec to temporal networks by using Node2Vec on each snapshot independently to generate an embedding sequence for each node. Then, they utilised traditional classifiers as inference units to make TLP, including Logistic Regression [ 111 ], Random Forests [ 112 ], and Gradient Boosted Regression Trees [ 113 ]. However, these static methods have issues with time-consuming computations and consistency. In summary, Node2Vec offers a more flexible approach than DeepWalk by allowing for adjustable exploration of a node’s neighbourhood. However, both methods face scalability issues and computational challenges when applied to temporal networks, necessitating considerations of efficiency and dynamic consistency in their application. Dynamic Methods [ 43 ] proposed SGNE, extending the Skip-gram algorithm based on DynNode2Vec [ 42 ]. While both methods adapt Skip-gram to capture temporal dynamics in discrete-time representation, SGNE introduces two key innovations: a decomposable loss function for learning representations of new nodes, and a selection mechanism that identifies the most influential nodes affected by network evolution. The focus on influential nodes potentially enables SGNE to achieve higher accuracy in TLP, particularly in networks where specific nodes drive structural changes.
