Abstract

Abstract This work introduces a data-centric framework for answering analytical questions using network models, transcending domain-specific modeling conventions. The unified network modeling framework (UNMF) provides an interdisciplinary strategy for principled modeling and analysis of complex networks, including multilayer and temporal networks that arise in sustainability-driven applications. UNMF connects dynamic analysis, pattern discovery, and network-grounded integration of heterogeneous sources (structured and unstructured). We present guided instantiations of UNMF in urban development, mobility, ecosystems, and social-network settings to show how explicit modeling choices can be documented, compared, and assessed within a shared evaluative framework. In doing so, we formalize and systematize Networked Data Science as a field at the intersection of network science and data science, and we define its scope and applications. This work contributes to network science by providing an auditable design-and-evaluation procedure for studying complex, evolving systems and for making representation choices more explicit, inspectable, and reusable across domains.

Body

Introduction Networks are ubiquitous in our modern world, arising from social interactions to transportation systems, from financial markets to regulatory dynamics in biological systems. Understanding the complex patterns that emerge from these networks has become an important topic of research in various disciplines, including computer science, physics, sociology, urban planning, and biomedicine. The study of networks has led to remarkable insights and advancements in various fields, such as the discovery of the small-world phenomenon [ 1 , 2 ], the identification of communities [ 3 ], or the development of effective strategies for controlling epidemics [ 4 ]. However, despite the wealth of research in the field, there is still a lack of a general framework for the principled modeling and analysis of complex networks [ 5 ]. Many existing methods are tailored to specific domains and lack the generality needed to capture the commonalities across different networks, hindering progress in interdisciplinary research. For instance, network analysis in epidemiology often utilizes agent-based models to simulate disease spread [ 6 ], while social networks analysis might employ graph theory to understand community formation and influence dynamics [ 7 ]. Similarly, ecological networks rely on niche models to study species interactions [ 8 ]. The domain-specific nature of these methods, such as agent-based models in epidemiology (which focus on individual behavior) [ 6 ] or graph theoretical approaches in social sciences (which emphasize relationships and node centrality) [ 7 ], means that they are often not directly transferable to other fields. This specificity limits their utility in addressing broader, interdisciplinary challenges, which often share underlying structural or dynamical features. For example, methods developed for social networks might provide insights into ecological food webs by revealing how changes in one part of a network can influence its overall stability [ 8 ]. Introducing a unified framework for analyzing complex networks would bring several advantages. It would facilitate the cross-pollination of ideas and techniques across different domains, enabling researchers to adopt the most effective analytical tools irrespective of their field of origin. Such a framework could better address societal challenges in complex systems, especially those driven by sustainability goals. For example, integrating network models from ecology, urban planning, and transportation could lead to more robust strategies for developing sustainable cities [ 9 ]. A general framework would also streamline the adaptation of models to new problems, accelerating innovation and enhancing our ability to predict and mitigate issues in complex systems [ 10 ]. By establishing a universal set of principles for network analysis, researchers could more effectively collaborate on transdisciplinary problems, leading to holistic solutions that are informed by the interconnected nature of global challenges. This approach would not only optimize resource allocation but also promote a deeper understanding of complex dependencies and interactions, crucial for addressing the multifaceted aspects of sustainability and resilience in societal systems [ 10 ]. Tackling this challenge is an important step toward meeting the need to manage complexity across domains [ 11 ]. One motivating example is the use of network models as a substrate for rich digital twins that capture self-evolving urban dynamics in smart-city and sustainability research. Principled network modeling is also relevant to address research gaps in knowledge acquisition from unstructured data [ 12 , 13 – 14 ], by promoting consistent representations for heterogeneous sources (e.g., text from social media, web pages, or documents). Standardized network modeling further supports the integration of unstructured data with existing structured sources. For instance, in transportation analysis, social media signals can be integrated with traffic flows and infrastructure data to identify mobility patterns and inform planning. Beyond integration, modeling standardization enables more precise, quantitative evaluations of urban planning principles [ 11 ]. This is particularly relevant for sustainable mobility, where intergenerational challenges demand temporal analyses over long horizons and multilayer spatial representations. Finally, integrating complex-network models with pattern discovery supports reasoning with contextual information about cities [ 15 ], extending classic context-free machine learning and data-mining approaches toward sustainability-driven decision support. Our central hypothesis is that standardized modeling principles facilitate the analysis of multilayer and dynamic networks derived from heterogeneous data [ 16 ]. While dynamic networks capture changes in entities (nodes) and interactions (edges) over time, multilayer networks integrate multiple modalities via layer-specific and cross-layer relations. This combination enables researchers and practitioners to study dependencies across layers and to assess how changes in one layer propagate to others. In transportation systems, for example, integrating unstructured signals with traffic data can yield a more comprehensive view of system behavior and resilience. More specifically, the contribution of UNMF is to provide an auditable design-and-evaluation procedure for constructing and comparing network encodings from heterogeneous data. Its primary value lies in making representation choices explicit, inspectable, and reusable across tasks and domains, rather than in claiming that a single encoding strategy has already been shown to outperform all alternatives quantitatively. Challenges and research gaps Current research in network-oriented integration of structured and unstructured data has made significant progress in recent years [ 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 – 21 ]. However, several gaps still exist, motivating the need for further investigation and unified views. These gaps motivate a comprehensive approach such as the proposed unified network modeling framework (UNMF), which targets three recurring challenges when integrating heterogeneous data sources and building interpretable, reproducible models for decision support: Complex models for multi-context heterogeneous data: Classic data science models often fail to capture the intricate dynamics and interdependencies of social, environmental, biological and urban systems, such as the interactions between land use, transportation, and social behavior [ 22 , 23 – 24 ]. These models need to reflect the complex relationships and feedback loops between various urban subsystems to better support empirical validation and policy-making [ 25 , 26 ]. The study of chimera states in multilayer networks, for instance, uniquely illustrates the coexistence of coherent (synchronized) and incoherent (desynchronized) behaviors within the same network, which are types of complex views that can only be understood through network models [ 27 ]. The complexity of these systems also poses significant challenges in terms of interpretability and explainability, which are critical for ensuring that insights derived from models are actionable and understandable for stakeholders [ 28 , 29 ]. Considering the guiding case, urban environments generate vast datasets from myriad sensors, administrative sources, and citizen-generated data, often compartmentalized and siloed, thus obstructing holistic analysis and integration [ 30 , 31 ]. For example, traffic management systems struggle to integrate real-time data from various sensors with historical traffic data, which complicates responsive traffic control and urban planning. Additionally, the integration of temporal dynamics introduces further complexity, affecting how data from different times are synchronized and analyzed [ 25 ]. Statistics and patterns in expressive network models: Retrieving actionable statistics and recurring patterns within complex networks, such as those found in social interactions or traffic flows, can significantly enhance our understanding of multi-context highly complex issues [ 32 ]. The structuring and representation of data greatly influence the outcomes of analyses. For instance, choosing between a graph-based or a traditional tabular data structure, or what is a node or an edge on a system, can affect the detection and interpretation of patterns within the data [ 33 , 34 ], yet in data-intensive contexts a roadmap of how these can be consistently modeled has not been seen. By exploring different data representation strategies within the modeling stage, we seek to uncover their effects on the discovery of patterns and the understanding of urban decision-making. Network-based analytical techniques that can handle the scale and complexity of multi-context data are crucial for identifying actionable insights that are otherwise not understandable without high-level views [ 32 ]. This formalization is essential for developing standardized methodologies that improve comparability and reproducibility across studies. Bridging data science and network science: Establishing a connection between the localized views provided by data science and the broader perspectives offered by network science can lead to a more comprehensive understanding of multi-context dynamics. This integration helps in understanding how we may use ideas from both fields to further analysis of big data that is so common in our times, bridging insights obtained from pattern analysis with established network science methodologies. By combining these perspectives, we can gain a comprehensive understanding of urban dynamics and the role of transportation networks in shaping individual decision-making processes [ 29 ], as well as understanding the dynamics of knowledge graphs for instance. This associative study will allow us to bridge insights obtained from pattern analysis with established network science methodologies. By combining these perspectives, we can gain a comprehensive understanding of urban dynamics and the role of transportation networks in shaping individual decision-making processes, as well as understanding the dynamics of knowledge graphs for instance. To address these gaps, we propose a unified view that integrates diverse temporal data sources and combines pattern-centric and network-centric analyses in a single, auditable workflow. Research questions Considering the above identified research challenges and gaps, this research proposes a novel framework for the network-grounded study of complex systems, encompassing network modeling principles from complex data (e.g., massive, multimodal, spatiotemporal), together with the necessary analytical facilities, to study complex systems. In this sense, the main question we aim to answer along this work is: How to model data into networks conducive to the understanding of actionable dynamics? Actionable dynamics are those either contributing to the acquisition of novel knowledge or successful design of operational and strategic policies. Specifically: Are there guiding principles for problem-oriented modeling and analysis of dynamic and multilayer networks? Which statistics and patterns in dynamic and multilayer networks best support the acquisition of actionable information? How does the instantiation of these network modeling principles contribute to addressing societal challenges across domains? In particular, we aim to identify common features and principles that underlie different types of networks, such as scale-free degree distributions, small-world properties, and modular structures, and develop a unified language for characterizing and modeling networks. Research objectives This research aims to be a milestone in the unification of Network Science and Data Science. The major objectives we aim to achieve to answer the previous research questions are: To identify common features that underlie different types of networks, such as scale-free degree distributions, small-world properties, and modular structures. To develop a unified model for the modeling and analysis of networks based on these common features and principles that is extensible to dynamic and multilayer network modeling. To apply the proposed unified framework to societal challenges in varied contexts, such as transportation optimization, knowledge-graph construction, and urban planning. Multilayer networks are used to model a variety of scenarios, encompassing multimodal transport networks, heterogeneous sensor networks, and multivariate relationships between concepts. Statistics and algorithms have been proposed to capture the dynamics of these networks [ 36 , 37 ]. The dynamics of the target systems can, in general, be represented in temporal networks. Generally, the principle of locality guides the determination of global behavior and the extraction of novel knowledge. Therefore, in this work, current statistical and machine learning principles applied to these structures will be extended using local stances. The usage of patterns mining and other techniques to understand dynamic processes using topological information has been the subject of focus, such as using percolation and cascading effects to study network robustness, agent-based modeling, and numerical simulations [ 38 ]. Nevertheless, we aim to use a data-driven lens to understand real user dynamics. This approach focuses on contribution to a data-driven smart city by aiding Strategic, Sustainable, Infrastructure planning, Economic forecasting, and Community economic development [ 39 , 40 ]. The usage of pattern-centric approaches could be used to associate similar progression of relationships between annotated entities using embeddings in RDF graphs. 1 These graphs can integrate data from different datasets [ 41 , 42 ]. In the urban application domain, the modeling of city transport networks has been used to understand fractal behavior at a topological level. This is a term that refers to structures that are self-similar at certain scales and have been seen in a wide variety of systems [ 43 ]. The fractal dimension of city borders, in particular, may be quantified. For example, the fractal dimension of the Paris subway system was determined to be 2 in the city, and 0.5 in the outskirts [ 44 ]. Other phenomena such as city scaling have been explored by Bettencourt and Geoffrey [ 45 ] where they state that cities are scaled versions of one another; nevertheless, it is of interest to understand if this phenomenon is true in what pertains to city dynamics. Network statistics Graphs offer a versatile data structure to specify networks. They are composed of nodes, also known as vertices ( V ), and edges ( E ) that connect them. These edges may have multivariate and univariate annotations. They have extensive applications in modeling complex systems, such as social networks, web pages, and transportation networks [ 46 ]. Graphs can be used for a variety of operations, including traversal, shortest path, connected components, minimum spanning tree, topological sorting, and more. Graph traversal algorithms involve visiting a precise set of nodes in a specific order, such as depth-first search (DFS) and breadth-first search (BFS) [ 47 , 48 ]. Algorithms, such as Dijkstra’s [ 49 ] and Bellman–Ford [ 50 ], can determine the shortest path between two nodes. Connected components are groups of nodes that are linked to each other, and minimum spanning tree finds a subset of edges that connects all nodes in the graph with the fewest edges [ 51 ]. Topological sorting arranges the nodes of a directed graph in a linear order such that all edges go from earlier to later elements [ 48 ]. In 1959, Dijkstra proposed the famous Dijkstra’s algorithm for finding the shortest path in a weighted graph [ 49 ]. Tarjan proposed the first linear-time algorithm for finding strongly connected components of a directed graph in 1972 [ 47 ]. The field of graph analysis has evolved significantly over time, with researchers developing a wide range of techniques and algorithms to better understand the structure and properties of graphs. Graph matching, for instance, involves comparing two graphs to measure their similarity using algorithms such as the Graph Edit Distance (GED) and Maximum Common Subgraph (MCS) [ 74 , 75 ]. Random graph models, on the other hand, enable the identification of patterns in a graph that are unlikely to have occurred by chance [ 76 ]. Other techniques for graph analysis include filtering, transformation, aggregation, clustering, shortest path, link prediction, Page Rank, spectral clustering, subgraph extraction, maximum flow, minimum cut, transitivity, assortativity, modularity, graph compression, Graph Laplacian, and Graph Fourier Transform [ 77 , 78 , 79 , 80 – 81 ]. For example, centrality measures can help identify the most important nodes in a network, while community detection can help identify groups of nodes that are tightly connected to each other. Path-based measures can be used to understand how information flows through a network, while network flows can be used to optimize the use of resources in a network. Random graph models can be used to generate synthetic networks that have similar properties to real-world networks, while dynamic network analysis can be used to study how networks change over time. System-level methods can be used to identify the overall structure of a network and to understand how different parts of the network are connected to each other. By using these different methods in combination, network analysts can gain a deep understanding of the structure and behavior of complex networks. Moreover, recent advances in machine learning have led to the development of several graph-oriented tasks, such as graph convolution, attention, and embedding, which enable the extraction of features, projection into a lower-dimensional space, and attention to different parts of the graph based on specific criteria [ 82 , 83 – 84 ]. In this context, graph neural networks (GNNs), including graph convolutional networks (GCN), graph autoencoders, and graph generative models have also been used to analyze and interpret graph-structured data [ 84 , 85 – 86 ]. Other related tasks include Graph Reinforcement Learning, which involves using reinforcement learning to navigate through the graph and make decisions based on the graph structure, and Graph Explainability, which focuses on explaining the decisions made by a model learnt from graph-structured data [ 87 , 88 ]. Positioning of a unified network modeling framework among unifying models What existing unifiers do Prior work offers three complementary ways to unify network modeling: Representational unifiers, multilayer and multiplex and tensor-based formalisms that collect multiple relation types, contexts, and time into a single mathematical object [ 89 , 90 , 91 – 92 ]. Multislice methods provide consistent community detection across layers and time [ 93 ]. These works standardize how to encode structure once design choices (layers, temporal windows, weights, signs, directions) are fixed. Type and semantics unifiers, heterogeneous information networks (HIN) with meta-path semantics that make typed entities and relations first-class and enable path-based similarity and analysis [ 94 , 95 ]. These works standardize how to carry and exploit schema-level meaning once a schema and a meta-path set are chosen. Generative and statistical unifiers, exponential-family models of network formation and evolution (exponential random graph models, ERGM; temporal ERGM, TERGM) that specify pr obabilistic laws for dependence structures via sufficient statistics [ 96 , 97 ]. These works standardize how to make inferences given a chosen representation and statistic set. Where they stop The approaches above are powerful but largely begin after key design decisions have been made: which entities become nodes; which interactions become edges; which layers and temporal granularity to use; how to handle direction, signs, and annotations; and which thresholds and weights to apply. They also do not, by themselves, require recording the rationale behind those choices or evaluating alternative encodings against a common portfolio of criteria. Graph data models such as the Resource Description Framework (RDF) and the Labeled Property Graph (LPG) further standardize storage and querying [ 98 ], but they do not prescribe how to select a modeling design for a given task. Fig. 1 Taxonomy of network analysis methods Dynamic network analysis The study of dynamic networks, which capture the time-dependent interactions between elements in a network, has gained significant attention in recent years. Different techniques and methods have been developed to analyze and understand the temporal dynamics of networks, particularly for transportation networks. Temporal motifs, patterns of edge interactions that occur at specific time intervals, have been used to understand traffic flow patterns and identify recurring congestion patterns [ 99 ]. Burst detection, which identifies time periods of high network activity, has been applied to optimize transportation infrastructure and resources to accommodate peak demand [ 100 ]. When interactions capture spatial dependencies, dynamic networks are also referred as spatiotemporal networks. Time-varying community detection techniques have been used to identify areas of the city that are highly connected and well-served by transportation infrastructure, as well as areas that may be underserved or disconnected [ 101 ]. Temporal link prediction, which predicts future link formation based on past network dynamics, has been used to predict changes in transportation demand and identify opportunities for improving transportation infrastructure and services [ 102 ]. Temporal centrality measures, including trend and variability statistics of classic indices along time, have been used to identify key transportation hubs and nodes in a city, as well as to understand how these hubs change over time [ 103 , 104 – 105 ]. Temporal k-core analysis has been used to understand the resilience and stability of transportation networks, as well as to identify essential nodes and hubs for maintaining network connectivity [ 106 ]. In spite of the role of temporal network alignment and graphlet correlation for identifying common patterns and features in transportation networks across their applications in different cities or time periods, as well as to understand their resilience and stability are limited. Temporal network entropy and sparsity have been used to understand the predictability, stability, efficiency, and robustness of transportation networks, as well as to identify periods of high variability or change [ 46 , 107 ]. Temporal network controllability has been used to identify key nodes and hubs essential for maintaining network connectivity and efficiency, as well as to optimize transportation infrastructure and resources to achieve desired outcomes [ 108 ]. Temporal network alignment refers to the process of comparing and aligning multiple dynamic networks that evolve over time, enabling prediction and comparison [ 109 ]. While this technique has been extensively applied in various domains, such as social networks and biological systems, its application in the context of transport and knowledge systems remains unexplored in the existing literature. Thus, there is a notable gap in studies focusing on temporal network alignment specifically in the domains of transport and knowledge systems. On another perspective, generative models have been used to predict changes in transportation demand and validate theories about the underlying mechanisms that drive the behavior of transportation networks, as well as to design and optimize transportation infrastructure and services [ 110 ]. Temporal network null models and reconstruction have been used to test hypotheses about the statistical significance of observed network properties, fill in gaps in transportation data, and identify opportunities for improving transportation infrastructure and services [ 111 ]. The study of temporal networks holds significant potential in understanding the temporal dynamics of transportation systems for reaching sustainability goals that require further modeling for tackling intergenerational issues. These techniques and methods can help stakeholders and decision-makers make informed decisions about transportation planning and management, as well as provide insights into how transportation networks change over time and across different contexts. On the other hand, in the realm of knowledge systems and information retrieval, the importance of networks cannot be overstated. Researchers have delved into the intricacies of temporal information retrieval, recognizing the challenges and techniques involved in extracting temporal knowledge from vast document collections. Studies in Temporal Information Retrieval [ 112 ] have shed light on the temporal aspects of documents and queries. Additionally, the exploration of temporal networks [ 113 ] has clarified how dynamic network structures can be studied across domains, including social networks and transportation systems. Furthermore, Vega and Magnani [ 5 ] introduce a model for studying temporal text networks, which consist of individuals connected by text exchanges that occur at specific times. Their contribution is especially relevant here as a representational point of departure: it shows how temporal and textual relations can be combined within a common formal model and how existing analysis methods from different areas can be brought to bear on such data. Relative to the present manuscript, however, that work does not in itself specify an auditable design-and-evaluation procedure for comparing alternative encodings under a shared set of criteria, nor does it foreground the cross-domain documentation of iterative design revisions. By focusing initially on statistically significant patterns, researchers gain insights into the underlying structures and dynamics of the dataset [ 131 ]. These patterns offer valuable information about meaningful relationships and associations in the network. Once these regularities have been thoroughly investigated, attention can be directed toward exploring anomalous patterns [ 132 ]. Anomalous patterns represent deviations from the expected behavior, offering a deeper understanding of networked data and the potential identification of novel or outlier phenomena. Clustering is another technique that can be used for creating edges between data points. This technique involves grouping similar data points together, and then creating edges between data points in the same group. Clustering can be based on proximity, correlation, or random walk [ 133 ]. Clustering aims to partition a set of data points into groups or clusters such that the data points within each cluster are more similar to each other than to those in other clusters. Biclustering offers a powerful approach to uncover patterns in networks [ 134 ] by simultaneously identifying cohesive subgroups of nodes and their corresponding subsets of edges from data, enabling the discovery of functional modules and community structures [ 135 ]. This method allows for a comprehensive understanding of the intricate relationships and organization within complex networks, facilitating insights into their structure and dynamics. Biclustering involves the simultaneous clustering of both rows (objects) and columns (features) of a matrix, aiming to discover subsets of objects that exhibit similar behavior across subsets of features. The dynamics within a system are described using a matrix representing network data using weights; the temporal dimension is added by representing a matrix of the system state per each time step [ 136 ]. The system dynamics can be seen as the progression of the weights or relationships along time. Statistical tests are widely used for hypothesis testing and to identify significant differences between groups or samples. In the context of network analysis, these methods can be employed to analyze dynamic network data, which involves examining changes in network structure over time. A variety of statistical tests can be used for this purpose, including the Chi-squared test, Student’s t-test, ANOVA, Friedman test, Mann–Whitney U test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Kruskal–Wallis test, Levene’s test, Bartlett’s test, Anderson–Darling test, Pearson’s correlation coefficient, and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. For example, consider the Chi-squared test, which is used to determine whether there is a significant difference between the expected and observed frequency distribution. In the context of network analysis, the formula for the Chi-squared test can be used to calculate the sum of squared differences between observed and expected frequencies of specific network patterns or features. Similarly, the t-test and ANOVA can be used to identify significant differences between the mean of relevant statistical estimates collected from two or more network samples. The Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test can be used to determine differences in medians of two or more network samples, while Levene’s and Bartlett’s tests can identify significant differences in variances among these samples. The Anderson–Darling and Lilliefors tests can be used to check if a sample of data follows a specific distribution. Thus, incorporating network structure in the statistical tests used for pattern detection can help researchers identify changes in network characteristics over time, and subsequently develop models for network evolution. For instance, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test allows us to compare the observed degree distribution of a network with an expected distribution, providing a measure of goodness of fit. The modularity statistic serves as a powerful tool for assessing the quality of identified communities within a network. To investigate variations in node importance, statistical tests like the t-test or permutation tests can be utilized to compare centrality measures (e.g., degree centrality, betweenness centrality) between different groups or time points. Moreover, network connectivity can be examined using the Kruskal–Wallis test or Fisher’s exact test to detect significant differences in the presence or absence of edges across distinct groups or conditions. Additionally, the Pearson correlation coefficient or Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient offers means to quantify the correlation between node attributes, such as degree, and shed light on network assortativity. These statistical approaches enhance our understanding of network structures and dynamics, empowering researchers to make meaningful inferences and unravel intricate patterns within complex networks.
