Abstract

Abstract Finding the shortest path between two vertices in a graph is essential in various applications, including logistics, social networks, citation graphs, and others. Given the need for repeated pathfinding in large graphs, accelerating this process is crucial. This article introduces a novel method that leverages the inherent clustering of graphs, enabling a quick elimination of suboptimal routes and significantly enhancing efficiency with minimal accuracy losses. Our approach builds upon traditional algorithms, such as the bidirectional Dijkstra, and explores hierarchical techniques. Extensive testing on over 750 real-world city graphs – including those of Beijing, Moscow, Paris, Barcelona, and New York – demonstrates its effectiveness. This article provides a comprehensive overview of the proposed method and its performance statistics.

Introduction The purpose of this paper is to introduce a novel method for accelerating the solution of the shortest path problem between two vertices in a graph. This problem focuses on finding a route that minimizes the sum of the edge weights connecting vertices. The determination of the shortest path is applicable across diverse areas where information is represented as graphs of any nature. Graphs are a versatile and convenient way to analyze different types of data: biological, social, technological, and information networks. In each of these domains, understanding the proximity between vertices is essential. For instance, in biology, this might involve pathfinding approaches in metabolic pathways, where the task considers a set of possible reactions and their associated compounds, appearing in a particular living organism as a directed graph (metabolic network) [ 1 ]. In the scientific community, this might be the task of identifying key patents within a citation graph by estimating the distance between vertices. Finally, in social networks or transport graphs, the task of finding the shortest path emerges naturally. In summary, the problem of finding the shortest path in an abstract graph is fundamental and widespread, making the development of new approaches to its solution highly relevant. In graph theory, the shortest path problem is considered one of the most significant classical problems. A variety of algorithms have been developed to resolve it, with Dijkstra’s algorithm being one of the most well-known and widely used. This algorithm, invented by Edsger Dijkstra in 1959, has a time complexity of M log N \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$M \log{N}$\end{document} , where N is a number of vertices and M is a number of edges in the graph. Despite the effectiveness, this complexity can pose challenges when working with graphs that contain large number of vertices, or when the shortest path problem must be solved repeatedly. Nevertheless, numerous faster methods for solving the shortest path problem have been proposed to address these challenges, as discussed in [ 2 ]. Among these, the authors of the article [ 2 ] consider methods based on preprocessing the original graph through clustering as particularly promising. Clustering, or community structure, is a significant feature of graphs that represent real systems. In these graphs, vertices are organized into clusters, characterized by a high density of edges within the same cluster and a low density of edges between different clusters. These clusters, or communities, can be considered as relatively independent compartments of a graph. Thereby the key objective of proposed method is to initially select the community, through which the route will be directed, and then search for routes within that community, rather than across the entire graph. This approach naturally eliminates a significant number of route options that a priori cannot be optimal, thereby reducing the time required to find the path and not provoking large deviations from the optimal route. In this work, we provide a detailed theoretical foundation for the implementation of a hierarchical approach, based on existing methods such as clustering and pathfinding algorithms. We also performed theoretical estimations, providing a mathematical description of the developed method to estimate the acceleration in computing time, including acceleration approximations at both tails of the acceleration plot. In addition to computing time acceleration, we also estimated the time complexity of the preprocessing phase. We presented case studies demonstrating the application and evaluation of the developed method on both real and synthetic data. It should be noted that the sparsity of real graphs ranged from 1.1 to 1.7, with an average error of up to 15% in cities, while acceleration increased proportionally with graph size, ranging from 5 to 30 times. Concerning synthetic graphs, we observed that completely random synthetic graphs show high error values when using the developed method, while synthetic geometric graphs have an average error up to 15-20%, which is related to the fact that some graphs are less clusterable, hence increasing the propensity of the whole algorithm to give higher errors. In summary, the primary contribution of this paper is the development of a method that significantly accelerates the solution process, with the accompanying mathematical description enabling the estimation of the lower bound of acceleration and the necessary hyperparameters in advance, eliminating the need for manual selection. Related works Graph preprocessing to solve the single pair shortest path problem is a widely used technique aimed at simplifying the graph and accelerating computations. Various methods exist to simplify the graph with minimal information loss, including Highway Hierarchies [ 3 , 4 ] and Contraction Hierarchies [ 5 – 7 ] related algorithms, partitioning or coarse-graining of graphs, and graph compression. These techniques generally reduce the number of pathfinding options by eliminating the least relevant alternatives. It is worth mentioning that graph simplification methods are often highly effective when combined with other optimization techniques, such as precomputed calculations or bidirectional search [ 8 , 9 ] as well as community detection in the hyperbolic space [ 10 ]. One of the pathfinding methods that uses precomputed calculations for graphs is contraction hierarchies (CH). The key example of hierarchical approaches is the contraction hierarchies method, suggested by R. Geisberg [ 5 ], which is based on iterative contraction of less significant nodes into shortcuts. The priority function for node order is based on the square root of the Voronoi region. This method significantly reduces the number of nodes considered during pathfinding, thereby accelerating computational efficiency. D. Ouyang et al. proposed a pre-treatment method that overcomes the disadvantages of hierarchy-based and hop-based solutions [ 11 – 14 ]. They introduce a hierarchical 2-hop index [ 15 ], assigning each vertex a 2-hop label and an hierarchy level. For shortest distance queries, only a subset of vertices, determined by the hierarchy, is used, thereby improving efficiency for both long-distance and short-distance queries. Another example of preprocessing methods is graph partitioning algorithms. Graph partitioning is a technique that divides the graph into several segments or layers, while the nodes of these segments are mutually exclusive based on the predefined rules [ 16 , 17 ]. Partitioning approach could be spatially-based, such as kd-Tree-like or grid-based (rectangular), or based on the properties of the graph structure. A key aspect of this technique is the correct division of the graph into an appropriate hierarchy. It means that not all graph topologies will yield the same performance improvements when using multi-layer graph partitioning. For example, B. Tesfaye et al. [ 18 ] describe two approaches to the graph partitioning problem: community detection and edge-cut partitioning. Louvain and Leiden are community selection approximation algorithms aimed at graph modularity optimization. Other examples of spatially-based approaches include more recent ones, which use graph neural network-based partitioning methods to predict node-partition assignment probabilities, and ClusterNet, which incorporates differentiable k-means clustering [ 19 ]. Finally, the last group of methods, graph compression techniques, are aimed at reducing the size of large graphs while preserving essential properties relevant to specific types of queries. The primary goal is to maintain the ability to accurately answer queries on the compressed graph as if they were executed on the original, uncompressed graph. One notable approach in this area is the Graph Compression method introduced by Wenfei Fan et al. [ 20 ]. In a more recent study, Karasikov et al. [ 21 ] propose the Multi-binary relation wavelet tree (Multi-BRWT) method, which compresses graph label data by constructing a hierarchical structure of wavelet trees. Each node in this structure stores an index vector representing the presence of labels in the graph. Clustering techniques are widely used in transportation problems, with examples applied to the research on the delay propagation in railway networks [ 22 ], where graph summarization techniques have been used. When applying these algorithms to real-world networks, it is crucial that each node is assigned to only one cluster [ 23 ]. In such cases, spatially-based graph clustering methods, like k-means, can be applied. However, this approach might not fully reflect the structure of the considered graph. The different properties of spatial networks, in terms of clustering and topology analysis, have been extensively studied in recent decades [ 24 ]. A related approach to CH by A. Pinandito et al. [ 25 ] integrates the Douglas- Peucker algorithm to reduce the number of points considered during the pathfinding process. This integration significantly decreases computational load while maintaining the accuracy and visual clarity of the path. The results demonstrate improved efficiency, making the method suitable for real-time applications in public transportation systems. In terms of acceleration, the authors achieved a 5- to 15-times acceleration on real-world graphs with only a 1-2 % error margin. Similarly, N. M. Wardhana et al. [ 26 ] used waypoint clustering, resulting a 2.5% error rate and an 8-time acceleration factor on a real road network. In another study incorporating CH method [ 27 ], tests on a smaller dataset showed no more than a 5% error with a 4-to 10-fold acceleration on a static graph. Despite the popularity of the methods mentioned above, there has been a lack of studies providing theoretical estimates. For example, no method currently exists to predict the resulting acceleration accurately. Additionally, the relationship between calculation error and the characteristics of the underlying graph has not been thoroughly investigated. Figure 12 Distribution of CO 2 savings by a factor of N compared to a simple solution using Dijkstra’s algorithm Conclusions This work suggests a hierarchical approach to graph preprocessing aimed at accelerating subsequent pathfinding queries. The classical Dijkstra’s algorithm is used as a case study, and theoretical estimates of the acceleration achievable through this method are provided. The theoretical analysis of the hierarchical pathfinding method assumes that cluster centers are uniformly distributed across the graph. This assumption facilitates the estimation of the average number of clusters a path traverses. Notably, the theoretical estimate is particularly sensitive to the average path length within clusters. For instance, for cities transport graphs, varying this estimate for individual cities, one can achieve a more accuracy estimate of acceleration. Consequently, the theoretical estimate of acceleration is generally lower than the actual observed acceleration. Practical acceleration has been demonstrated using both road graphs of real cities and abstract graphs. Results for real cities with vertex counts exceeding 10,000 show an average acceleration of approximately 13 times, which increases with the number of vertices. For synthetic graphs, the speedup provided by the hierarchical approach depends on several parameters, including graph density. Overall, acceleration primarily depends on the number of clusters identified. A large number of clusters slows down path searches in the centroid graph while speeding up searches in the source graph. Conversely, a smaller number of clusters accelerates searches in the centroid graph but requires iterating over many vertices during path search in the source graph. The average error of this approach is about 15% for real cities and is independent of the number of vertices. As demonstrated in the Results section, this error is mainly due to graph topology, as changes in the relative positions of vertices (e.g., inter-vertex distances) significantly affect the error, while key graph hyperparameters, such as density and vertex count, remain unchanged. The primary mechanism driving the error is the divergence in paths between the boundary points of a cluster (which have outgoing edges to other clusters) and the centroid point within the cluster. An example of this behavior is illustrated in Sect. 4.2 , where increasing the graph’s density — and consequently, the number of boundary points within each cluster—has a significant impact on the error. This method has specific features and limitations. The proposed theoretical estimates for pathfinding algorithms assume uniform distribution of cluster centers across the graph. In the future, these theoretical results could be generalized to other classes of graphs.
