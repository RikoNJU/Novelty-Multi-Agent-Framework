LACE: Layer-Wise Compression for Dynamic Frame Rate Codecs

Thanapat Trachu, Samuele Cornell, William Chen, Shinji Watanabe

Affiliation: Language Technologies Institute, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, USA

{ttrachu, scornell, wc4, swatanab}@andrew.cmu.edu

Abstract

Neural audio codecs are a key component in speech language modeling. However, their high frame rates lead to long sequence lengths, increasing computational costs. Dynamic frame rate codecs mitigate this by reducing the effective frame rate using a compression step to merge multiple frames together. However, most prior methods either operate on single-codebook codecs or apply a single compression step before multi-layer quantization. This forces all quantization layers to share the same segmentation boundaries, despite the residual embeddings at different quantization layers exhibiting different rates of change over time.
We propose LACE (Layer-Adaptive Codec Encoding), a dynamic frame rate codec that applies an independent compression step at each quantization layer, enabling layer-specific segmentation boundaries. To use LACE tokens in downstream text-to-speech (TTS), we further introduce union alignment and boundary anchor mechanisms to make durations consistent across layers while preserving compression benefits. Experiments on LibriTTS show that LACE offers a better rate-quality tradeoff than prior dynamic frame rate methods on the reconstruction task and improves TTS inference efficiency while maintaining competitive synthesis quality. Our code is released as part of the ESPnet3 codec recipe.

Index Terms: Audio Codec, Text-to-Speech, Dynamic Frame Rate Codec, Compression

I Introduction

Neural audio codecs have emerged as an important component for speech language modeling. When used as speech tokenizers, they convert continuous speech into discrete codes, allowing speech generation [1, 2, 3] and speech language models [4, 5, 6] to be formulated as next-token prediction problems similar to those used in large language models. However, general neural audio codecs [7, 8, 9] typically operate at high frame rates, producing long sequences of discrete codes.

Long sequences of discrete codes introduce two limitations. First, long sequences increase the computational cost, as the complexity of transformer-based models tends to scale with sequence length [10]. Second, the length mismatch between speech and text tokens can degrade the performance of speech language models, since substantially longer speech sequences make syntactic and semantic modeling more difficult, as shown in [11].

These limitations motivate dynamic frame rate codecs [12, 13, 14, 15], which represent speech as discrete codes paired with durations. A compression model produces segmentation boundaries, which are frame positions where the frame-level embeddings are partitioned. These boundaries divide the embeddings into variable-length segments, and each segment is represented by one code and its duration. Since each code now corresponds to a segment rather than a single frame, the frame rate is measured as the number of segments per second instead of the number of frames per second. We refer to this as the effective frame rate. For example, if one second of audio with 75 frame-level embeddings is compressed into 25 segments, the effective frame rate is 25 Hz, instead of 75 Hz. By assigning longer durations to regions with lower information density, dynamic frame rate codecs use fewer codes to represent speech than general codecs.

Most existing dynamic frame rate codecs either operate on single-codebook codec models [15, 13], or apply a single compression step before multi-layer quantization [14, 16, 17]. In both cases, all quantization layers are constrained to share the same segmentation boundaries. This constraint may be suboptimal for multi-codebook codecs due to the hierarchical nature of their quantization process. In residual vector quantization (RVQ) [18], the first layer quantizes the input directly, while each subsequent layer quantizes the residual embeddings from the previous layer. Consequently, earlier layers capture the main signal structure, whereas deeper layers encode finer details. As a result, representations at different layers may change at different rates over time, suggesting that each layer requires its own segmentation boundaries. This intuition is supported by our analysis in Section V-C1, which shows that deeper-layer residual embeddings change more rapidly than earlier-layer residual embeddings. This is further supported by the findings of SNAC [19], which uses different frame rates across quantization layers, although it is not a dynamic frame rate codec because its frame rates are fixed.

Motivated by these findings, we propose LACE (Layer-Adaptive Codec Encoding), a dynamic frame rate codec that independently applies a compression step at each quantization layer. This allows each layer to choose its own segmentation boundaries.
To adapt LACE to the common speech language model framework, we propose union alignment, which constructs shared segmentation boundaries by taking the union across layers. Since union alignment can increase the effective frame rate, we further propose boundary anchor, which constrains deeper layers to reuse boundaries introduced by earlier layers. Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose LACE, a dynamic frame rate codec that applies an independent compression step at each quantization layer.

•

We propose union alignment and boundary anchor to resolve duration inconsistency across layers, enabling LACE tokens to be used in downstream TTS training.

•

We provide a theoretical analysis showing that LACE achieves a lower upper bound on the expected quantization error than single compression.

•

Experiments on LibriTTS [20] show that LACE offers a better rate-quality tradeoff than prior dynamic frame rate methods on the reconstruction task and improves TTS inference efficiency while maintaining competitive synthesis quality.

II Background

II-A Neural Audio Codec Preliminaries

A neural audio codec consists of an encoder, a quantization module, and a decoder. The encoder maps the raw waveform into frame-level embeddings 𝐇∈ℝT×D\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D}, where TT is the number of frames and DD is the embedding dimension. The quantization module discretizes 𝐇\mathbf{H} into frame-level codes 𝐙∈[1,V]T×C\mathbf{Z}\in[1,V]^{T\times C}, where each frame is represented by CC codes from a codebook of size VV. The decoder then reconstructs the waveform from these codes.

A commonly used quantization method is RVQ. RVQ discretizes 𝐇\mathbf{H} using CC quantization layers, where each layer ll contains a codebook 𝐐l∈ℝV×D\mathbf{Q}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times D}. Here, 𝐪kl∈ℝD\mathbf{q}_{k}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{D} denotes the kk-th codeword of 𝐐l\mathbf{Q}^{l}. RVQ performs quantization sequentially across quantization layers. At layer ll, the nearest codeword is selected from the corresponding codebook 𝐐l\mathbf{Q}^{l} as zil=arg⁡mink∈[1,V]⁡‖𝐫il−𝐪kl‖22z_{i}^{l}=\arg\min_{k\in[1,V]}\|\mathbf{r}_{i}^{l}-\mathbf{q}_{k}^{l}\|_{2}^{2}, where zil∈[1,V]z_{i}^{l}\in[1,V] and 𝐫il∈ℝD\mathbf{r}_{i}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{D} denote a code and a residual embedding at frame ii and quantization layer ll, respectively. The residual embedding is updated recursively as 𝐫il+1=𝐫il−𝐪zill\mathbf{r}_{i}^{l+1}=\mathbf{r}_{i}^{l}-\mathbf{q}_{z_{i}^{l}}^{l}, with 𝐫i1=𝐡i\mathbf{r}_{i}^{1}=\mathbf{h}_{i}, where 𝐡i∈ℝD\mathbf{h}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D} is the ii-th frame-level embedding in 𝐇\mathbf{H}. We define the stacked frame-level residual embeddings of layer ll as 𝐑l∈ℝT×D\mathbf{R}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D}.

II-B Compression in Dynamic Frame-Rate Codecs

The encoder of a neural audio codec produces frame-level embeddings 𝐇\mathbf{H} at a fixed codec frame rate, yielding TT frames. A compression model groups these TT frames into MM variable-length segments. Each segment is represented by CC codes and an integer duration.

Formally, the compression model determines segmentation boundaries ℬ={b0,b1,…,bM}\mathcal{B}=\{b_{0},b_{1},\dots,b_{M}\}, a sorted set with b0=0b_{0}=0, bM=Tb_{M}=T, and bm∈[1,T−1]b_{m}\in[1,T-1]. The mm-th segment spans the frame interval (bm−1,bm](b_{m-1},b_{m}]. Some methods control MM explicitly using a target compression rate γ∈(0,1]\gamma\in(0,1], yielding M=⌊γ​T⌋M=\lfloor\gamma T\rfloor, while others determine MM implicitly through a threshold τ\tau. The frame-level embeddings within each segment are averaged to produce segment-level embeddings 𝐇¯∈ℝM×D\bar{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times D}. The segment-level embeddings are paired with the durations 𝐝∈ℕM\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{M}, where the duration of the mm-th segment dmd_{m} is bm−bm−1b_{m}-b_{m-1}. The segment-level embeddings 𝐇¯\bar{\mathbf{H}} are quantized using RVQ, as described in Section II-A, producing segment-level codes 𝐙¯∈[1,V]M×C\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\in[1,V]^{M\times C}. This (code, duration) format resembles the classical run-length encoding scheme [21]. During reconstruction, 𝐙¯\bar{\mathbf{Z}} is repeated according to 𝐝\mathbf{d} to produce frame-level codes 𝐙^∈[1,V]T×C\hat{\mathbf{Z}}\in[1,V]^{T\times C}. This restores the frame rate expected by the decoder. The three compression models below share this pipeline and differ only in how they derive ℬ\mathcal{B}.

Dynamic Programming (DP)-Based Compression.

CodecSlime [15] determines ℬ\mathcal{B} by minimizing the total reconstruction error between the original frame-level embeddings 𝐇\mathbf{H} and the segment-level embeddings 𝐇¯\bar{\mathbf{H}}. The reconstruction loss for this segment is ℓ⁡(j,s)=∑t=j−s+1j‖𝐡t−𝐡¯j,s‖22\ell(j,s)=\sum_{t=j-s+1}^{j}\|\mathbf{h}_{t}-\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{j,s}\|_{2}^{2}. Each segment is constrained to contain at most UU frames. Let f⁡[j,n]f[j,n] denote the minimum total reconstruction loss when the first jj frames are compressed into nn segments. Cosine Similarity-Based Compression.

FlexiCodec [14] determines ℬ\mathcal{B} by thresholding the similarity between adjacent frame-level embeddings. Let σt=cos⁡(𝐡t,𝐡t+1)\sigma_{t}=\cos(\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t+1}) denote the similarity between frames tt and t+1t+1. A boundary is placed wherever the similarity drops below a threshold τ\tau, yielding ℬ={0,T}∪{t:σt<τ}\mathcal{B}=\{0,T\}\cup\{\,t:\sigma_{t}<\tau\,\}.
The number of segments MM is therefore not fixed in advance but controlled implicitly by the threshold τ\tau, unlike the DP-based compression model.

Density Peak Clustering-Based Compression.

VARSTok [13] derives ℬ\mathcal{B} using density peak clustering. For each frame tt, a local density ρt\rho_{t} is computed from its κ\kappa nearest neighbors, and a peak distance δt\delta_{t} is the temporal distance to the nearest frame with higher density:

We use κ=5\kappa=5, β=0.2\beta=0.2, and U=4U=4, following the original paper.

All of these prior works force all quantization layers to share the same segmentation boundaries. In contrast, LACE applies an independent compression step at each quantization layer, enabling layer-specific segmentation boundaries. Because LACE is agnostic to the choice of compression model, any of these methods can be used as the compression step within the proposed framework.

III Method

We propose LACE, a dynamic frame rate codec that applies an independent compression step at each quantization layer, allowing each layer to choose its own segmentation boundaries. We then introduce two mechanisms, union alignment and boundary anchor, to make the duration consistent for downstream TTS training.

III-A Layer-Wise Compression

Fig. 1: Overview of the LACE codec. At each quantization layer, the residual embeddings are independently compressed before being passed to the quantization layer. Frames with the same color belong to the same segment.

Unlike prior dynamic frame rate codecs that apply a single compression step and share segmentation boundaries across quantization layers, LACE performs layer-wise compression on the residual embeddings. At layer ll, we apply the compression step described in Section II-B to 𝐑l\mathbf{R}^{l}. This produces segment-level residual embeddings 𝐑¯l∈ℝMl×D\bar{\mathbf{R}}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{M^{l}\times D}, durations 𝐝l∈ℕMl\mathbf{d}^{l}\in\mathbb{N}^{M^{l}}, and layer-specific segmentation boundaries ℬl\mathcal{B}^{l}, where MlM^{l} is the number of segments at layer ll. The segment-level residual embeddings 𝐑¯l\bar{\mathbf{R}}^{l} are then passed to quantization layer ll, yielding segment-level codes 𝐙¯l∈[1,V]Ml\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{l}\in[1,V]^{M^{l}}.

To compute the residual embeddings for the next layer, we expand 𝐙¯l\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{l} to frame-level codes 𝐙^l∈[1,V]T\hat{\mathbf{Z}}^{l}\in[1,V]^{T} by repeating each code according to 𝐝l\mathbf{d}^{l}. The next residual embedding is then computed as 𝐫il+1=𝐫il−𝐪z^ill\mathbf{r}_{i}^{l+1}=\mathbf{r}_{i}^{l}-\mathbf{q}^{l}_{\hat{z}^{l}_{i}}. Unlike the standard RVQ update in Section II-A, 𝐪z^ill\mathbf{q}^{l}_{\hat{z}^{l}_{i}} is obtained by quantizing a segment-level residual embedding, whereas 𝐪zill\mathbf{q}^{l}_{z^{l}_{i}} in the original update is obtained from a frame-level residual embedding. We use the frame-level residual embedding 𝐫il\mathbf{r}_{i}^{l} on the right-hand side so that 𝐫il+1\mathbf{r}_{i}^{l+1} captures both the compression error and the quantization error from layer ll. Figure 1 illustrates layer-wise compression.

III-B Union Alignment

Fig. 2: An overview of union alignment. Before alignment (top), quantization layers 1 and 2 have different segmentation boundaries, resulting in inconsistent durations across layers. After alignment (bottom), the union of segmentation boundaries from both layers is applied to all layers.

The layer-wise compression produces different segmentation boundaries ℬl\mathcal{B}^{l} for each quantization layer ll. As a result, codes from different layers that cover the same time span can have different durations. This is problematic for TTS because the model would need to predict durations for each layer while ensuring that the total duration after upsampling is identical across layers. We address this with union alignment, which constructs shared segmentation boundaries by taking the union across layers: ℬunion=⋃l=1Cℬl\mathcal{B}^{\text{union}}=\bigcup_{l=1}^{C}\mathcal{B}^{l}. We then re-segment the segment-level codes of every layer using ℬunion\mathcal{B}^{\text{union}}. As shown in Figure 2, when a boundary in ℬunion\mathcal{B}^{\text{union}} falls inside an existing segment, that segment is split into sub-segments. For example, the boundary b1=2b_{1}=2 from the first layer splits the first segment in the second layer. The resulting sub-segments keep the same code and receive new corresponding durations. After alignment, all layers share the same segmentation boundaries and durations, at the cost of increasing the effective frame rate.

III-C Boundary Anchor

Union alignment can increase the effective frame rate because |ℬunion|≥|ℬl||\mathcal{B}^{\text{union}}|\geq|\mathcal{B}^{l}| for all ll. In the worst case, each layer introduces distinct boundaries and |ℬunion||\mathcal{B}^{\text{union}}| approaches TT, negating the compression benefit. To mitigate this, we introduce a boundary anchor. We choose an anchor layer l∗l^{*}, where layers l≤l∗l\leq l^{*} are allowed to introduce new boundaries, while deeper layers l>l∗l>l^{*} must reuse boundaries from earlier layers: ℬanchor=⋃l=1l∗ℬl\mathcal{B}^{\text{anchor}}=\bigcup_{l=1}^{l^{*}}\mathcal{B}^{l}.
By restricting the compression step at layers l>l∗l>l^{*} to place boundaries only at positions in ℬanchor\mathcal{B}^{\text{anchor}}, we limit the growth of |ℬunion||\mathcal{B}^{\text{union}}|. Here, l∗l^{*} is a tunable hyperparameter that trades off between quality and the effective frame rate.

For DP-based compression, we implement this restriction with a constrained cost ℓ~​(j,s)\tilde{\ell}(j,s), which equals the segment loss ℓ⁡(j,s)\ell(j,s) of Section II-B if both jj and j−sj{-}s lie in ℬanchor\mathcal{B}^{\text{anchor}} and +∞+\infty otherwise. We solve the same recursion as Equation (1) with ℓ\ell replaced by ℓ~\tilde{\ell}, and use the unconstrained DP for layers l≤l∗l\leq l^{*}. While this formulation is specific to DP-based compression, the boundary anchor itself is model-agnostic. It can be applied to other compression models in Section II-B by restricting its candidate boundaries to ℬanchor\mathcal{B}^{\text{anchor}}.

III-D Theoretical Analysis

Section III-C introduced the anchor layer l∗l^{*} as a hyperparameter that trades off quality against the effective frame rate. We now analyze the quality side of this tradeoff: how the choice of l∗l^{*} affects the expected quantization error. In Sections II-B and III-A, the compression step merges the frames before the quantization layer, and the codes are repeated back to the frame level after it. Since all repeated frames within a segment are identical, repeating before or after quantization yields the same result. We therefore combine merging and repetition into a single matrix multiplication 𝐀l​𝐑l\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}, where 𝐑l\mathbf{R}^{l} is the frame-level residual embedding at layer ll. Concretely, 𝐀l∈ℝT×T\mathbf{A}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T} is block-diagonal with one block per segment, and the block for a segment of length dmd_{m} is the dm×dmd_{m}\times d_{m} matrix with every entry equal to 1/dm1/d_{m}. Since 𝐀l\mathbf{A}^{l} is symmetric and idempotent, the compression error 𝐑l−𝐀l​𝐑l\mathbf{R}^{l}-\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l} is orthogonal to any frame-level embeddings that use the same segmentation boundaries as layer ll. Let 𝐐^l∈ℝT×D\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D} denote the frame-level quantized embeddings, which stack the selected codewords 𝐪z^ill\mathbf{q}^{l}_{\hat{z}^{l}_{i}}, so the residual update of Section III-A becomes 𝐑l+1=𝐑l−𝐐^l\mathbf{R}^{l+1}=\mathbf{R}^{l}-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}.

The quantized embeddings 𝐐^l\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l} repeat one codeword across each segment. Subtracting them in the residual update can therefore only change the part of 𝐑l\mathbf{R}^{l} that is constant within each segment. The compression error, which varies within segments, passes to the next layer untouched. When all layers share segmentation boundaries, the quantized embeddings of every layer are constant within the same segments, so the compression error survives all CC quantization layers. It becomes an error that quantization cannot remove. We formalize this intuition under three assumptions: (i) every layer merges at least two frames (Ml<TM^{l}<T), so the compression error is strictly positive, (ii) each quantization layer is well-trained, approximately satisfying the centroid condition [22], so that 𝔼⁡[‖𝐀l​𝐑l−𝐐^l‖F2]≤ϵl​𝔼​[‖𝐀l​𝐑l‖F2]\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]\leq\epsilon_{l}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}] with ϵmax=suplϵl<1\epsilon_{\max}=\sup_{l}\epsilon_{l}<1, and (iii) the fraction of expected residual energy captured by the compression step, αl=𝔼⁡[‖𝐀l​𝐑l‖F2]/𝔼⁡[‖𝐑l‖F2]\alpha_{l}=\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]/\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}], is bounded below by αmin=inflαl>0\alpha_{\min}=\inf_{l}\alpha_{l}>0.

III-E TTS Training with LACE Tokens

Unlike standard TTS models [2, 23], a TTS model trained on LACE tokens must also predict the durations 𝐝\mathbf{d}. After union alignment, all CC quantization layers share the segmentation boundaries ℬunion\mathcal{B}^{\text{union}} and durations 𝐝\mathbf{d}. Therefore, the model predicts durations only for the first quantization layer and applies the predicted durations to all subsequent layers.

We use an autoregressive decoder-only Transformer that predicts the segment-level codes 𝐙¯\bar{\mathbf{Z}} in a delay-pattern format [23]. At each generation step for the first-layer code, the model additionally predicts a duration for that code. Duration prediction is formulated as a classification task. We add a duration head alongside the code prediction head and include a learned duration embedding in the input representation, which is added to the code embedding. The model is trained with cross-entropy loss for code prediction and focal loss for duration prediction to address class imbalance, with loss weights of 1 and 3, respectively. At inference, we use nucleus sampling with a top-pp value of 0.8 and a temperature of 1.0.

IV Experimental Setting

IV-A Dataset

We use the LibriTTS dataset [20] at 24 kHz sampling rate. The training set consists of train-clean-100, train-clean-360, and train-other-500. Evaluation is performed on test-clean. The same dataset is used for both reconstruction and TTS experiments. For TTS, a reference utterance is randomly selected from a different utterance of the same speaker to condition the model on speaker identity.

IV-B Model Architecture

IV-B1 Codec Models

We build our method on three neural audio codecs: SoundStream [8], EnCodec [9], and the Descript Audio Codec (DAC) [7], all pretrained on LibriTTS using ESPnet-Codec [24] recipes22
                2

              https://huggingface.co/espnet/libritts_soundstream24k, https://huggingface.co/espnet/libritts_encodec_24k, https://huggingface.co/espnet/libritts_dac_24k. We integrate the proposed layer-wise compression into the quantization layers (Section III-A) and fine-tune each model end-to-end from the pretrained weights for 80k iterations using the original codec objective. We use Adam [25] with learning rate 10−410^{-4}, an exponential decay schedule (rate 0.99980.9998 per step, minimum 10−510^{-5}), and shared optimization settings for the generator and discriminator. All experiments use 4 V100 32GB GPUs.

IV-B2 TTS Model

The TTS model is a 24-layer Transformer with 16 attention heads, model dimension 1024, feedforward dimension 4096, and dropout 0.1. Input transcripts are converted to phoneme sequences using the espeak-ng phonemizer [26]. A randomly truncated 3-second reference utterance is prepended to condition the model on speaker identity. The model is trained for 40k iterations on 4 V100 32GB GPUs using Adam with learning rate 10−410^{-4} and 4000 warmup steps.

IV-C Baselines

We compare LACE with CodecSlime [15], FlexiCodec [14], and VARSTok [13]. Because these methods use different codec backbones and training setups, we re-implement them on LibriTTS with the same codec backbones and fine-tuning configuration, changing only the compression model. These baselines use single compression, where the compression step is applied once before the quantization layers so all layers share the same segmentation boundaries. In contrast, LACE applies compression independently at each quantization layer.

IV-D Rate Computation

For reconstruction, we report the effective frame rate before union alignment, since alignment is not required. For threshold-based compression models that yield different effective frame rates across layers, we report the mean effective frame rate across layers. The bitrate accounts for both code and duration bits, where each duration costs log2⁡U\log_{2}U bits per segment. For a fair comparison, we compare at matched bitrate (∼8.6\sim 8.6 kbps). Note that because LACE adds one duration sequence per layer, it reaches this bitrate at a lower effective frame rate than single compression (22.5 vs. 27 Hz).

IV-E Evaluation Metrics

Reconstruction.

We compute word error rate (WER) with Whisper-Large [27] by transcribing the reconstructed audio. UTMOS [28] assesses perceptual naturalness, while PESQ [29] and STOI [30] measure signal quality and intelligibility. For speaker similarity (SpkSim), we compute the cosine similarity between X-vectors from the generated and reference audio, extracted with WavLM-base [31] fine-tuned for speaker verification33
                3

              https://huggingface.co/microsoft/wavlm-base-plus-sv. All metrics use VERSA [32] to compute.

TTS.

Since TTS generation is non-deterministic, we generate 10 utterances per input and select the one with the lowest WER under Whisper-Small, following prior work [33]. The selected utterance is evaluated with Whisper-Large for WER, along with UTMOS and SpkSim. We also report the real-time factor (RTF), the ratio of generation time to audio duration, to assess inference efficiency. To complement these objective metrics, we conduct a human evaluation using mean opinion score (MOS) and speaker similarity mean opinion score (SMOS) to evaluate naturalness and speaker similarity, respectively. Specifically, we randomly sample 25 test utterances and synthesize them with each TTS system. Then, we collect ratings from 35 evaluators.

V-C Analysis

We perform two analyses to better understand LACE. The first examines how fast the residual embeddings change at each quantization layer. The second empirically validates the quantization error bound from Section III-D.

V-C1 Histogram of Cosine Similarity

On the pretrained codecs, we compute the cosine similarity between consecutive frame-level residual embeddings, σtl=cos⁡(𝐫tl,𝐫t+1l)\sigma^{l}_{t}=\cos(\mathbf{r}^{l}_{t},\mathbf{r}^{l}_{t+1}). Figure 4 shows that its distribution concentrates around 11 at earlier layers and gradually shifts toward 00 at deeper layers. This indicates that residual embeddings change more rapidly at deeper layers and confirms that different layers change at different rates, motivating layer-wise compression. Moreover, the slower variation of earlier layers suggests that segmentation decisions have a larger impact on these layers than on deeper layers. We therefore let earlier layers choose its own segmentation boundaries before deeper layers, justifying the boundary anchor (Section III-C).

V-C2 Empirical Result of VQ Error

While Figure 4 only motivates layer-wise compression, this analysis directly measures the error caused by shared segmentation boundaries. We measure the quantization error 𝔼⁡[‖𝐑C+1‖F2]\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{C+1}\|_{F}^{2}] on the pretrained DAC model with DP compression while varying the anchor layer l∗l^{*}. Figure 5 shows that LACE yields consistently lower quantization error than single compression, matching Equation (3). The right panel shows the gap widening as more layers are used. Single compression converges to a constant error floor, while LACE keeps reducing the error. The left panel shows the role of γ\gamma. As γ→1\gamma\to 1, compression merges almost no frames, so α1→1\alpha_{1}\to 1 and the floor (1−α1)​𝔼​[‖𝐇‖F2](1-\alpha_{1})\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}] vanishes, explaining the narrow gap at high compression rates. At lower rates the floor grows and the benefit of LACE becomes more pronounced.

VI Conclusion

We presented LACE, a dynamic frame rate codec that applies an independent compression step at each quantization layer. Unlike prior methods that force all layers to share the same segmentation boundaries, LACE allows each layer to choose its own segmentation boundaries. To enable downstream TTS training, we introduced union alignment, which constructs shared segmentation boundaries across layers, and boundary anchor, which limits the growth of the effective frame rate. Experiments on LibriTTS show that LACE consistently outperforms single-compression baselines across multiple codec backbones and compression models on the reconstruction task. Applied to TTS, LACE improves inference efficiency while maintaining competitive quality.

I-A Upper Bound of the Expected Quantization Error

Setup.

Let 𝐇∈ℝT×D\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D} be the frame-level embeddings drawn from an in-domain speech distribution. At layer ll, the compression step performs two operations: (1) averaging the frame-level embeddings within each segment, and (2) repeating each segment-level embedding according to its duration. In Sections II-B and III-A, the repetition happens after the quantization layer rather than before, since all repeated frames within a segment are identical, the two orders yield the same result. We therefore combine both operations into a single matrix multiplication 𝐀l​𝐑l\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}, where 𝐀l∈ℝT×T\mathbf{A}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T} is the compression matrix at layer ll and 𝐑l\mathbf{R}^{l} is the frame-level residual embedding from Section III-A, with 𝐑1=𝐇\mathbf{R}^{1}=\mathbf{H}. The matrix 𝐀l\mathbf{A}^{l} is block-diagonal: the block of the mm-th segment has every entry equal to 1/dm1/d_{m}, where dmd_{m} is the segment duration. From this structure, 𝐀l\mathbf{A}^{l} is symmetric ((𝐀l)⊤=𝐀l(\mathbf{A}^{l})^{\top}=\mathbf{A}^{l}) and idempotent ((𝐀l)2=𝐀l(\mathbf{A}^{l})^{2}=\mathbf{A}^{l}).

Let VQl​(⋅)\text{VQ}_{l}(\cdot) denote quantization at layer ll followed by upsampling, so that the frame-level quantized embedding is 𝐐^l=VQl​(𝐀l​𝐑l)\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}=\text{VQ}_{l}(\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}) and the residual update of Section III-A becomes 𝐑l+1=𝐑l−𝐐^l\mathbf{R}^{l+1}=\mathbf{R}^{l}-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}. Since 𝐐^l\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l} is constant within each segment of layer ll, applying the compression matrix leaves it unchanged:

αl=𝔼⁡[‖𝐀l​𝐑l‖F2]𝔼⁡[‖𝐑l‖F2]\displaystyle\alpha_{l}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]}

(2)

as the fraction of expected residual energy captured by the compression step at layer ll, and assume it is uniformly bounded below by αmin=inflαl>0\alpha_{\min}=\inf_{l}\alpha_{l}>0. Lemma 1 shows αl≤1\alpha_{l}\leq 1, together with Assumption 1 and Equation (11), αl<1\alpha_{l}<1.

Lemma 1 (Compression never increases energy).
For any 𝐑∈ℝT×D\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D} and any compression matrix 𝐀\mathbf{A},

‖𝐀𝐑‖F2≤‖𝐑‖F2.\displaystyle\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{R}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{R}\|_{F}^{2}.

(3)

Proof. The compression matrix acts independently on each segment and each feature dimension, so it suffices to prove the claim for a single segment and a single dimension. Let x1,…,xdmx_{1},\dots,x_{d_{m}} denote the scalar values within a segment of duration dmd_{m}. Compression replaces every value with the segment mean μ=1dm​∑i=1dmxi\mu=\frac{1}{d_{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{m}}x_{i}, so the energy of the segment changes from ∑i=1dmxi2\sum_{i=1}^{d_{m}}x_{i}^{2} to dm​μ2=1dm​(∑i=1dmxi)2d_{m}\mu^{2}=\frac{1}{d_{m}}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{m}}x_{i}\big)^{2}. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality with the all-ones vector,

(∑i=1dmxi⋅1)2≤(∑i=1dmxi2)​(∑i=1dm12)=dm​∑i=1dmxi2,\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{m}}x_{i}\cdot 1\Big)^{2}\leq\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{m}}x_{i}^{2}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{m}}1^{2}\Big)=d_{m}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{m}}x_{i}^{2},

(4)

which gives dm​μ2≤∑i=1dmxi2d_{m}\mu^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d_{m}}x_{i}^{2}. Summing over all segments and all feature dimensions yields the claim.  ■\blacksquare

Lemma 2 (Expected rate-distortion bound).
Under Assumption 2, the expected quantization error at layer ll satisfies

𝔼⁡[‖𝐀l​𝐑l−𝐐^l‖F2]≤ϵl​𝔼​[‖𝐀l​𝐑l‖F2],\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}\big]\leq\epsilon_{l}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}\big],

(5)

where ϵl=1−𝔼⁡[‖𝐐^l‖F2]/𝔼⁡[‖𝐀l​𝐑l‖F2]<1\epsilon_{l}=1-\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]/\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]<1.

Proof. Write 𝐗=𝐀l​𝐑l\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l} for the quantization input and 𝐗^=𝐐^l\hat{\mathbf{X}}=\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l} for its output. The quantization partitions the input space into regions {𝒱k}\{\mathcal{V}_{k}\} and maps every input in region 𝒱k\mathcal{V}_{k} to the output 𝐜k\mathbf{c}_{k}. By the centroid condition, 𝐜k=𝔼⁡[𝐗∣𝐗∈𝒱k]\mathbf{c}_{k}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}], so the expected quantization error within each region is zero:

𝔼⁡[𝐗−𝐜k∣𝐗∈𝒱k]=𝔼⁡[𝐗∣𝐗∈𝒱k]−𝐜k=𝟎.\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{c}_{k}\mid\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}]-\mathbf{c}_{k}=\mathbf{0}.

(6)

By the law of total expectation, the quantization error is therefore orthogonal to the quantization output in expectation:

𝔼⁡[⟨𝐗−𝐗^,𝐗^⟩F]\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\mathbf{X}-\hat{\mathbf{X}},\,\hat{\mathbf{X}}\rangle_{F}\big]
=∑kℙ⁡(𝐗∈𝒱k)​⟨𝔼⁡[𝐗−𝐜k∣𝐗∈𝒱k],𝐜k⟩F\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{k})\,\big\langle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{c}_{k}\mid\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}],\,\mathbf{c}_{k}\big\rangle_{F}

=0,\displaystyle=0,

(7)

where 𝐜k\mathbf{c}_{k} is pulled out of the conditional expectation because it is constant within its region. The cross-term thus vanishes in the expected Pythagorean decomposition:

𝔼⁡[‖𝐗‖F2]=𝔼⁡[‖𝐗^‖F2]+𝔼⁡[‖𝐗−𝐗^‖F2].\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{X}\|_{F}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{\mathbf{X}}\|_{F}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{X}-\hat{\mathbf{X}}\|_{F}^{2}].

(8)

Rearranging gives the exact equality

𝔼⁡[‖𝐗−𝐗^‖F2]=(1−𝔼⁡[‖𝐗^‖F2]𝔼⁡[‖𝐗‖F2])​𝔼​[‖𝐗‖F2]=ϵl​𝔼​[‖𝐗‖F2],\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{X}-\hat{\mathbf{X}}\|_{F}^{2}]=\Big(1-\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{\mathbf{X}}\|_{F}^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{X}\|_{F}^{2}]}\Big)\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{X}\|_{F}^{2}]=\epsilon_{l}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{X}\|_{F}^{2}],

(9)

and Assumption 2 (𝔼⁡[‖𝐗^‖F2]>0\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{\mathbf{X}}\|_{F}^{2}]>0) gives ϵl<1\epsilon_{l}<1.  ■\blacksquare

Lemma 3 (Orthogonality).
For any 𝐑,𝐒∈ℝT×D\mathbf{R},\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D} and any compression matrix 𝐀\mathbf{A}, the compression error 𝐑−𝐀𝐑\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{R} is orthogonal to any compressed embedding 𝐀𝐒\mathbf{A}\mathbf{S} under the Frobenius inner product:

⟨𝐑−𝐀𝐑,𝐀𝐒⟩F=Tr⁡(𝐑⊤​(𝐀−𝐀2)​𝐒)=0,\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{R},\,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{S}\rangle_{F}=\mathrm{Tr}\big(\mathbf{R}^{\top}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}^{2})\mathbf{S}\big)=0,

(10)

using symmetry and idempotence of 𝐀\mathbf{A}. In particular, choosing 𝐒=𝐑\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{R} yields the Pythagorean identity:

‖𝐑‖F2=‖𝐀𝐑‖F2+‖𝐑−𝐀𝐑‖F2.\displaystyle\|\mathbf{R}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{R}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{R}\|_{F}^{2}.

(11)

Lemma 4 (Per-layer energy contraction).
For every layer ll that computes new boundaries,

𝔼⁡[‖𝐑l+1‖F2]≤λl​𝔼​[‖𝐑l‖F2],\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l+1}\|_{F}^{2}]\leq\lambda_{l}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}],

(12)

where λl=1−αl​(1−ϵl)<1\lambda_{l}=1-\alpha_{l}(1-\epsilon_{l})<1.

Proof. Decompose the residual update as 𝐑l+1=(𝐑l−𝐀l​𝐑l)+(𝐀l​𝐑l−𝐐^l)\mathbf{R}^{l+1}=(\mathbf{R}^{l}-\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l})+(\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}). By Equation (1), the second term can be rewritten as a compressed embedding:

𝐀l​𝐑l−𝐐^l=𝐀l​𝐑l−𝐀l​𝐐^l=𝐀l​(𝐑l−𝐐^l).\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}=\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}-\mathbf{A}^{l}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}=\mathbf{A}^{l}(\mathbf{R}^{l}-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}).

(13)

Therefore, the two terms are orthogonal by Lemma 3 with 𝐒=𝐑l−𝐐^l\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{R}^{l}-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}, and their squared norms add:

𝔼⁡[‖𝐑l+1‖F2]\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l+1}\|_{F}^{2}]
=𝔼⁡[‖𝐑l−𝐀l​𝐑l‖F2]+𝔼⁡[‖𝐀l​𝐑l−𝐐^l‖F2]\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l}-\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]

≤(1−αl)​𝔼​[‖𝐑l‖F2]+ϵl​αl​𝔼​[‖𝐑l‖F2]\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{l})\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]+\epsilon_{l}\,\alpha_{l}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]

=λl​𝔼​[‖𝐑l‖F2].\displaystyle=\lambda_{l}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}].

(14)

The first term follows from Equation (11) and the definition of αl\alpha_{l} in Equation (2), which together give 𝔼⁡[‖𝐑l−𝐀l​𝐑l‖F2]=(1−αl)​𝔼​[‖𝐑l‖F2]\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l}-\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]=(1-\alpha_{l})\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]. The second term applies Equation (5) and then Equation (2): 𝔼⁡[‖𝐀l​𝐑l−𝐐^l‖F2]≤ϵl​𝔼​[‖𝐀l​𝐑l‖F2]=ϵl​αl​𝔼​[‖𝐑l‖F2]\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]\leq\epsilon_{l}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]=\epsilon_{l}\,\alpha_{l}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}].  ■\blacksquare

Lemma 5 (Invariance of the compression error).
If 𝐀l=𝐀⋆\mathbf{A}^{l}=\mathbf{A}^{\star} for all l≥l∗l\geq l^{*}, then for all such layers:

𝐑l+1−𝐀⋆​𝐑l+1=𝐑l−𝐀⋆​𝐑l.\displaystyle\mathbf{R}^{l+1}-\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{l+1}=\mathbf{R}^{l}-\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{l}.

(15)

Proof. Applying 𝐀⋆\mathbf{A}^{\star} to the residual update 𝐑l+1=𝐑l−𝐐^l\mathbf{R}^{l+1}=\mathbf{R}^{l}-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l} and using Equation (1):

𝐀⋆​𝐑l+1=𝐀⋆​𝐑l−𝐐^l.\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{l+1}=\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{l}-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}.

(16)

Subtracting Equation (16) from the residual update cancels 𝐐^l\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l} and yields the claim. Hence, the compression error introduced at layer l∗l^{*} passes through all subsequent layers unchanged: it cannot be reduced by any quantization that reuses 𝐀⋆\mathbf{A}^{\star}.  ■\blacksquare

Theorem 1 (Hierarchy of error bounds).
After all CC quantization layers, the expected residual error satisfies:

𝔼⁡[‖𝐑C+1‖F2]≤[1−αl∗​(1−ϵmaxC−l∗+1)]​∏l=1l∗−1λl​𝔼​[‖𝐇‖F2].\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{C+1}\|_{F}^{2}]\leq\Big[1-\alpha_{l^{*}}\big(1-\epsilon_{\max}^{\,C-l^{*}+1}\big)\Big]\prod_{l=1}^{l^{*}-1}\lambda_{l}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}].

(17)

Proof. Decompose the final residual as 𝐑C+1=(𝐑C+1−𝐀⋆​𝐑C+1)+𝐀⋆​𝐑C+1\mathbf{R}^{C+1}=(\mathbf{R}^{C+1}-\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{C+1})+\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{C+1}. The two parts are orthogonal by Lemma 3, and applying Lemma 5 repeatedly over layers l∗,…,Cl^{*},\dots,C replaces the first part with the compression error at layer l∗l^{*}:

𝔼⁡[‖𝐑C+1‖F2]\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{C+1}\|_{F}^{2}]
=𝔼⁡[‖𝐑l∗−𝐀⋆​𝐑l∗‖F2]\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{R}^{l^{*}}-\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{l^{*}}\|_{F}^{2}\big]

+𝔼⁡[‖𝐀⋆​𝐑C+1‖F2].\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{C+1}\|_{F}^{2}\big].

(18)

The first term equals (1−αl∗)​𝔼​[‖𝐑l∗‖F2](1-\alpha_{l^{*}})\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l^{*}}\|_{F}^{2}] by Equation (11) and the definition of αl∗\alpha_{l^{*}} in Equation (2).

For the second term, consider any layer l≥l∗l\geq l^{*}. These layers
share the same compression matrix (𝐀l=𝐀⋆\mathbf{A}^{l}=\mathbf{A}^{\star}),
so Equation (16) shows that
𝐀⋆​𝐑l+1\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{l+1} is exactly the quantization error of layer ll. Since 𝐀l=𝐀⋆\mathbf{A}^{l}=\mathbf{A}^{\star}, the quantizer input 𝐀l​𝐑l\mathbf{A}^{l}\mathbf{R}^{l} equals 𝐀⋆​𝐑l\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{l}, so Lemma 2 (Equation (5)) applies with input 𝐀⋆​𝐑l\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{l} and bounds it:

𝔼⁡[‖𝐀⋆​𝐑l+1‖F2]=𝔼⁡[‖𝐀⋆​𝐑l−𝐐^l‖F2]≤ϵmax​𝔼​[‖𝐀⋆​𝐑l‖F2].\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{l+1}\|_{F}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{l}-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}]\leq\epsilon_{\max}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{l}\|_{F}^{2}].

(19)

Applying this bound recursively over the C−l∗+1C-l^{*}+1 layers from l∗l^{*} to CC, and then converting the compressed energy at layer l∗l^{*} to residual energy via Equation (2):

𝔼⁡[‖𝐀⋆​𝐑C+1‖F2]\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{C+1}\|_{F}^{2}]
≤ϵmaxC−l∗+1​𝔼​[‖𝐀⋆​𝐑l∗‖F2]\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{\max}^{\,C-l^{*}+1}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}^{\star}\mathbf{R}^{l^{*}}\|_{F}^{2}]

=ϵmaxC−l∗+1​αl∗​𝔼​[‖𝐑l∗‖F2].\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\max}^{\,C-l^{*}+1}\,\alpha_{l^{*}}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l^{*}}\|_{F}^{2}].

(20)

Summing the two terms gives [1−αl∗​(1−ϵmaxC−l∗+1)]​𝔼​[‖𝐑l∗‖F2]\big[1-\alpha_{l^{*}}\big(1-\epsilon_{\max}^{\,C-l^{*}+1}\big)\big]\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l^{*}}\|_{F}^{2}]. Bounding 𝔼⁡[‖𝐑l∗‖F2]\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{l^{*}}\|_{F}^{2}] by unrolling Lemma 4 over layers 1,…,l∗−11,\dots,l^{*}-1 yields Equation (17).  ■\blacksquare

Equation (17) reveals a hierarchy of bounds as C→∞C\to\infty:

•

Shared segmentation boundary (l∗=1l^{*}=1). All layers reuse the boundaries of the first layer, corresponding to single compression. The second term of Equation (18) vanishes and the error converges to a constant floor:

limC→∞𝔼⁡[‖𝐑C+1‖F2]=(1−α1)​𝔼​[‖𝐇‖F2],\displaystyle\lim_{C\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{C+1}\|_{F}^{2}]=(1-\alpha_{1})\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}],

(21)

which is exactly the compression error of the first layer and is strictly positive by Assumption 1.

•

Boundary anchor (1<l∗<C1<l^{*}<C). The error floor is reduced by the contraction of the layer-wise phase:

limC→∞𝔼⁡[‖𝐑C+1‖F2]≤(1−αl∗)​∏l=1l∗−1λl​𝔼​[‖𝐇‖F2].\displaystyle\lim_{C\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{C+1}\|_{F}^{2}]\leq(1-\alpha_{l^{*}})\prod_{l=1}^{l^{*}-1}\lambda_{l}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}].

(22)

•

Layer-wise compression without anchor (LACE). Every layer computes new boundaries, so Lemma 4 applies at every layer:

𝔼⁡[‖𝐑C+1‖F2]≤∏l=1Cλl​𝔼​[‖𝐇‖F2]≤λmaxC​𝔼​[‖𝐇‖F2],\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{R}^{C+1}\|_{F}^{2}]\leq\prod_{l=1}^{C}\lambda_{l}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}]\leq\lambda_{\max}^{\,C}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}],

(23)

where λmax=1−αmin​(1−ϵmax)<1\lambda_{\max}=1-\alpha_{\min}(1-\epsilon_{\max})<1, so the bound converges to zero as C→∞C\to\infty.

Therefore, recomputing boundaries inside the residual loop removes the constant error floor inherent to a shared segmentation boundary, while the boundary anchor interpolates between the two regimes.

Remark. This analysis assumes that layers l>l∗l>l^{*} reuse the segmentation boundary of layer l∗l^{*} exactly. In our implementation (Section III-C), these layers may instead select any subset of ℬanchor\mathcal{B}^{\text{anchor}}. The theorem corresponds to the special case where the full segmentation boundary of layer l∗l^{*} is reused.
