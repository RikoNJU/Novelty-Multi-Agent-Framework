When does distribution shift break graph neural networks calibration?

Journal: Neural Networks

Abderaouf Bahi

Address: Computer Science and Applied Mathematics Laboratory (LIMA),
Faculty of Science and Technology, Chadli Bendjedid University, El Tarf 36000, Algeria

Abstract

Graph neural networks (GNNs) are increasingly deployed in real-world applications where distribution shift is unavoidable. However, how such shifts affect model calibration, defined as the agreement between predictive confidence and actual accuracy, remains poorly understood, and existing graph calibration methods typically rely on labeled validation data from the deployment distribution. In this work, I present the first closed-form theoretical characterization of GNN calibration under distribution shift. I show that calibration is governed by a single scalar quantity that explicitly depends on structural changes between the source and target graphs, as well as feature quality. This characterization precisely identifies when a model becomes over-confident, under-confident, or remains calibrated, and directly yields the optimal temperature scaling strategy. I further extend the analysis to graph convolutional networks with symmetric normalization, multi-class classification, and covariate shift, and derive a theoretical upper bound on the expected calibration error. My analysis also reveals that, under homogeneous distribution shift, a single global temperature is theoretically optimal, providing a principled explanation for why more complex node-wise recalibration methods offer no additional benefit. Building on these theoretical insights, I propose STAC, a source-free, label-free calibration method. Experiments on synthetic benchmarks demonstrate substantial calibration improvements, while evaluations on five real-world graph datasets show that reliable calibration without target labels remains challenging despite the strong predictive power of the theory. These findings identify label-free accuracy estimation under distribution shift as the central unresolved challenge for practical calibration of GNNs and establish a theoretical foundation for future research on trustworthy graph learning.

1 Introduction

Graph neural networks (GNNs) Bahi [2026] are now routinely deployed in settings where the graph seen
at test time differs from the one used for training: social and financial networks
evolve, sensor and infrastructure graphs are rewired after failures or upgrades, and
citation, molecular or recommendation graphs used at inference are drawn from different
sub-populations than those used to train the model. In many of these settings raw
predictive accuracy is not the primary bottleneck; what determines whether a GNN can be
trusted inside a fraud-review, clinical-triage, or infrastructure-monitoring pipeline is
whether its confidence scores are calibrated: whether a prediction reported with
90%90\% confidence is, in fact, correct 90%90\% of the time. Miscalibration is not
cosmetic: it silently corrupts every downstream mechanism that consumes probability
outputs, from selective prediction and human-in-the-loop triage to risk-weighted
decision rules and model ensembling.

Two observations make GNN calibration under distribution shift both practically
important and theoretically awkward. (i) GNNs are miscalibrated in
structure-dependent ways; unlike image classifiers (typically over-confident), they are
often under-confident in-distribution, and the size of the gap correlates with
graph properties such as homophily and node degree [Wang et al., 2021b, Hsu et al., 2022].
(ii) Standard calibrators need labels from the test distribution: temperature
scaling [Guo et al., 2017] and its graph-specific
variants [Wang et al., 2021b, Hsu et al., 2022, Zhuang et al., 2025, Li et al., 2025, Tang et al., 2024] all
fit a temperature (or a temperature-valued function of the node) by minimizing negative
log-likelihood on a labeled validation set drawn from the same distribution as
training. Under shift that temperature is simply wrong, and no target labels are
available to refit it.

Despite an increasingly rich empirical literature on GNN calibration (Section 2),
no existing account explains, from first principles, how and why shift
moves a GNN’s confidence away from its accuracy, in which direction, and by
how much. Existing post-hoc graph calibrators (CaGCN, GATS, GETS, WATS,
SimCalib) are all validated in-distribution and are, by construction, vulnerable to
exactly the failure mode that matters most in deployment: a validation set that no
longer represents the test distribution. Empirical studies of GNN reliability under
shift [Trivedi et al., 2024] document that the problem is real and that architectural
expressivity alone does not fix it, but stop short of a mechanism. I supply that
mechanism for a model simple enough to analyze in closed form yet expressive enough to
reproduce, quantitatively, the calibration behaviour of trained GCNs in simulation and on
five real graphs spanning two orders of magnitude in edge homophily
(Fig. 1 previews the core result).

2 Related work

2.1 Calibration of neural networks

Modern networks trained with cross-entropy are systematically miscalibrated, typically
over-confident on natural images [Guo et al., 2017]. Temperature
scaling [Guo et al., 2017], dividing the logits by a single scalar fit by
minimizing NLL on a labeled validation set, remains the most widely used post-hoc fix,
extended to class-wise and Dirichlet variants [Kull et al., 2019]. Expected
calibration error (ECE) and reliability diagrams [Naeini et al., 2015] are the standard
diagnostic; large-scale studies [Minderer et al., 2021] show calibration is
sensitive to architecture, augmentation, and training length even in-distribution, and
training-time remedies such as focal loss [Mukhoti et al., 2020] trade accuracy for
calibration by reshaping the loss directly. Ovadia et al. [Ovadia et al., 2019] showed that all of
these fixes degrade under distribution shift on image classifiers; the present paper
supplies, for GNNs, the mechanism that was left as an empirical observation there.

2.2 In-distribution calibration of GNNs

GNNs miscalibrate in ways that are structurally distinct from image classifiers: rather
than being uniformly over-confident, they are frequently under-confident in-distribution,
and the size of the gap correlates with graph properties such as node degree and
homophily [Wang et al., 2021b, Hsu et al., 2022]. A first family of methods learns a per-node
temperature from an auxiliary model trained on labeled data: CaGCN [Wang et al., 2021b]
regresses temperatures with a second GCN; GATS [Hsu et al., 2022] attends over
structural covariates identified as drivers of miscalibration; GETS [Zhuang et al., 2025]
ensembles several temperature-generating experts in a mixture-of-experts head;
WATS [Li et al., 2025] conditions the temperature on heat-kernel graph-wavelet features
that encode multi-scale structure without relying on neighbours’ logits;
SimCalib [Tang et al., 2024] ties calibration error directly to nodewise embedding
similarity. A second family regularizes the training loss itself rather than
post-processing logits, e.g. the graph calibration loss of Wang et al. [Wang et al., 2022a]. All of
these methods are trained and validated on data from the same distribution as the
test graph; none models, or is evaluated under, distribution shift, and, as I discuss
in Section 7.2, every one of them inherits the failure mode of
ordinary temperature scaling once the target distribution moves. A related line
quantifies predictive uncertainty rather than calibration per se, e.g. JuryGCN’s
jackknife variance estimates [Kang et al., 2022]; such estimates are informative but
do not by themselves specify a recalibration temperature.

2.3 GNN reliability under distribution shift

The gap this paper targets, calibration under shift, has so far been approached
empirically rather than analytically. Trivedi et al. [Trivedi et al., 2024] document, across covariate,
concept, and graph-size shifts, that increased GNN expressivity does not by itself improve
confidence-indicator quality, and propose G-Δ\DeltaUQ, a stochastic-anchoring scheme that
estimates epistemic uncertainty from a single model and uses it to modulate
confidence; this consistently improves out-of-distribution ECE relative to Monte Carlo
dropout and vanilla temperature scaling, but offers no closed-form account of why
shift moves calibration or of what the optimal correction is, and it requires retraining
the base GNN with a stochastic-centering objective rather than recalibrating a frozen
model. Huang et al. [Huang et al., 2023] take a complementary, distribution-free route:
conformalized GNNs (CF-GNN) wrap any trained GNN with a topology-aware conformal
predictor that provably achieves a target marginal coverage, trading the pointwise notion
of calibration this paper studies for a set-valued guarantee that holds under
exchangeability but is not designed to track how miscalibration grows as homophily
or feature noise change. My closed-form slope κ\kappa is, to my knowledge, the first
result that predicts the sign, magnitude, and optimal correction of GNN miscalibration
under shift analytically, and the first to explain why a single global temperature
should be expected to suffice.

2.4 Distribution shift and out-of-distribution generalization on graphs

A separate and much larger literature targets accuracy, rather than calibration,
under graph distribution shift. Invariance-based training regularizes representations to
be stable across environments (EERM [Wu et al., 2022], SR-GNN [Zhu et al., 2021]),
out-of-distribution generalization is benchmarked systematically by
GOOD [Gui et al., 2022], and OOD-GNN [Li et al., 2022] proposes an architecture-level
generalization bound. You et al. [You et al., 2023] derive a model-based risk bound for graph domain
adaptation and use it to design a spectral regularizer (GDA-SpecReg), theory-grounded in
the same spirit as my approach, but aimed at bounding the target error, not at
characterizing the resulting confidence-accuracy gap, and it requires access to
the source graph at adaptation time. GTrans [Jin et al., 2023] adapts the graph itself
(features and edges) at test time rather than the model, but again optimizes accuracy.
None of these methods produce, or are evaluated on, calibrated probabilities.

2.5 Source-free and test-time adaptation

Outside calibration, source-free test-time adaptation removes the requirement of access
to source data at deployment by adapting model parameters using only the target input
distribution: entropy minimization (Tent [Wang et al., 2021a]), self-supervised
test-time training [Sun et al., 2020] (specialized to graphs
by Wang et al. [Wang et al., 2022b]), and source-hypothesis transfer (SHOT [Liang et al., 2020])
all optimize an unsupervised proxy for accuracy on the target data. STAC
(Section 5) is source-free in the same sense (only a scalar threshold is
carried from the source model), but optimizes a calibration objective rather than
accuracy, and, following Corollary 1, deliberately restricts the
adaptation to a single global scalar under homogeneous shift rather than adapting model
weights.

2.6 Label-free performance estimation under shift

ATC [Garg et al., 2022] and disagreement-based generalization-gap
estimators [Jiang et al., 2022] predict a model’s accuracy on unlabeled, shifted
data from confidence statistics and prediction disagreement under perturbation,
respectively. Theorem 1 shows that these estimators supply exactly the
missing ingredient a calibration method needs (a single target-accuracy scalar pins down
T⋆=1/κT^{\star}=1/\kappa), and Section 6 shows this is simultaneously STAC’s strength
(it reduces recalibration to one well-studied sub-problem) and its weakness (existing
estimators are not accurate enough under graph shift; Section 7.4).

2.7 Homophily-aware and physics-inspired GNN architectures

My signal coefficient cK​(h)c_{K}(h) recovers the homophily threshold that motivates a family
of spectral and physics-inspired GNN architectures designed to remain accurate across the
homophily spectrum: FAGCN [Bo et al., 2021] learns a signed low/high-pass filter,
GPR-GNN [Chien et al., 2021] learns signed propagation weights, and
GraphCON [Rusch et al., 2022] and A-DGN [Gravina et al., 2023] use
oscillatory/anti-symmetric ODE dynamics to propagate information without the
over-smoothing that erodes signal at low homophily. These works change the
architecture to remain accurate across hh; I hold the architecture fixed and
instead show how hh governs the confidence-accuracy gap of whatever architecture
produced the logits, via cK​(h)c_{K}(h) in Proposition 2. The two views are
complementary: a homophily-robust architecture changes the operating point, but the
calibration slope, and the optimality of a single temperature, follow from
Theorem 1 regardless of which architecture produced the logits, consistent
with my real-graph experiments, which calibrate SIGN/logistic-regression features, not
the linear aggregation of Section 3 directly.

3 Problem setup

3.1 Problem statement

I am given a source graph Gs=(As,Xs,Ys)G_{s}=(A_{s},X_{s},Y_{s}) with node labels, on which a GNN ff is
trained and calibrated (e.g. by ordinary temperature scaling on a source validation
split, so ff is calibrated on GsG_{s}). At test time I observe a target graph
Gt=(At,Xt)G_{t}=(A_{t},X_{t}) from the same feature/label space but a different
distribution (different homophily, different feature noise, or both), with no
target labels and no further access to GsG_{s} or YsY_{s}. The model ff is frozen:
re-training is not assumed to be an option in the deployment settings I target
(streaming graphs, on-device inference, or simply the cost of retraining a production
model). The question I answer is: how does the confidence-accuracy relationship of
ff’s frozen predictions change from GsG_{s} to GtG_{t}, and how can it be corrected without
labels?

3.4 Why a linear GNN on a CSBM?

A skeptical reader may ask why I restrict the closed-form analysis to a linear classifier
on Gaussian CSBM features rather than a deep, nonlinear GNN. I follow a long tradition in
learning theory (of which the CSBM itself is a canonical graph
instance [Deshpande et al., 2018]) of trading architectural realism for a model simple
enough to solve exactly, then checking numerically how far its predictions travel toward
the regime of interest. Three considerations support this choice here. First, mean/GCN
aggregation followed by a linear read-out is not merely a toy: it is the exact computation
performed by SGC [Wu et al., 2019] and, feature-wise, by SIGN [Frasca et al., 2020], both
competitive with deeper GNNs on the heterophilous benchmarks I use in
Section 6, so the model class I analyze is a real, widely used GNN family,
not merely an approximation of one. Second, the object I characterize, the map from
logit magnitude to accuracy, depends on the aggregation step, which mixes same-
and different-class neighbours in a homophily-dependent ratio, far more than on the
specific nonlinearity of the classifier head; this is why the closed-form slope, derived
for a linear head, tracks a self-loop GCN’s simulated slope closely
(Section 6.1, Fig. 6) and a logistic-regression head on
SIGN features on five real graphs (Section 6.4). Third, and most
importantly, every qualitative claim the theory makes (the sign of over/under-confidence,
the existence of a single optimal temperature, the non-benefit of per-node calibration
under homogeneous shift) is falsifiable and is checked against simulation and real data
throughout Section 6; I treat the tractable model as a source of falsifiable,
quantitative predictions about a real computation (mean/GCN aggregation plus a linear or
near-linear head), not as a claim about mechanism inside arbitrarily deep, nonlinear GNNs.

4.1 The aggregated discriminant coordinate

Lemma 1 (Aggregated coordinate).

Under mean aggregation, conditional on yi=+1y_{i}=+1 and degree did_{i}, the discriminant
coordinate gi=μ^⊤​zig_{i}=\hat{\mu}^{\top}z_{i} has mean (2​h−1)​r(2h{-}1)r and variance
(4​h​(1−h)​r2+σ2)/di\big(4h(1{-}h)r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\big)/d_{i}, and is asymptotically Gaussian as di→∞d_{i}\to\infty.

Remark 1 (Reading the lemma).

The mean (2​h−1)​r(2h{-}1)r is exactly the excess of same-class over different-class neighbours,
scaled by the class-mean separation rr; it is positive for homophilic graphs (h>1/2h>1/2),
zero at h=1/2h=1/2 where a neighbour is a coin flip over the two classes, and negative for
heterophilic graphs, where aggregation systematically pulls a node’s representation
toward the wrong class. The variance combines two independent noise sources, label
randomness among neighbours (4​h​(1−h)​r24h(1{-}h)r^{2}) and feature noise (σ2\sigma^{2}), and both
shrink as 1/di1/d_{i}: high-degree nodes average out noise exactly as one would expect from a
sample mean of did_{i} roughly independent terms. Gaussianity is not an approximation
tacked on for convenience; it is a genuine Lindeberg central-limit effect over the did_{i}
neighbours, which is why the closed forms below are accurate already at moderate degree
(d≈20d\approx 20, Section 6).

Remark 2 (Reading Eq. (1)).

Equation (1) says homophily controls the sign and strength of the
aggregation signal, (2​h−1)(2h{-}1): moving hth_{t} toward 12\tfrac{1}{2} shrinks the signal faster
than the variance, so κ<1\kappa<1 and the frozen model becomes over-confident; a target more
homophilous than the source (ht>hsh_{t}>h_{s}, both >1/2>1/2) gives κ>1\kappa>1 and the model becomes
under-confident, because the same logit magnitude that was appropriate for a noisier
source aggregation now under-states how reliable the cleaner target signal actually is.
The formula is antisymmetric in a useful sense: κ⁡(hs)=1\kappa(h_{s})=1 by construction, and κ\kappa
crosses zero exactly when ht=1/2h_{t}=1/2, where the aggregated signal vanishes and no logit
magnitude, however large, can be predictive, an early warning that the bound in
Proposition 4 becomes uninformative there. The dependence on ρ\rho enters
only through the combination 4​h​(1−h)​ρ4h(1{-}h)\rho, i.e. SNR matters only insofar as it
competes with the same same-/different-class mixing variance that homophily itself
controls; at ρ→∞\rho\to\infty (noiseless features) Eq. (1) reduces to the
purely structural ratio (2​ht−1)/(2​hs−1)(2h_{t}{-}1)/(2h_{s}{-}1).

Corollary 1 (Global temperature is optimal).

Under homogeneous shift (a single κ\kappa), T⋆=1/κT^{\star}=1/\kappa makes the model equal to the
true conditional, attaining minimum population NLL and zero population ECE. Any per-node
temperatures {Ti}\{T_{i}\} with Ti≠T⋆T_{i}\neq T^{\star} on a positive-measure set strictly increase NLL.
Per-node recalibration can therefore only help under heterogeneous shift.

Remark 3 (Why this corollary matters operationally).

Corollary 1 is a negative result stated as a positive one: it says that
any extra modeling effort spent on a per-node temperature function is provably
wasted under homogeneous shift, no matter how expressive the per-node model is, because
the population-optimal per-node solution is already the constant function T⋆T^{\star}. This
is a strong, falsifiable prediction about methods like CaGCN and GATS
(Section 2), which fit a per-node temperature even when the shift
they are correcting for is homogeneous; Section 6.3 tests the
prediction directly with a dedicated ablation (STAC-global vs. STAC-full) and finds it
holds: the per-node stage changes error-detection ranking only marginally, and not more
than an ablation that discards graph structure entirely.

violated, far from it.

5 STAC: a source-free, label-free recalibration method

Theorem 1 says the entire job is to estimate one number, T⋆=1/κT^{\star}=1/\kappa,
from unlabeled target data (Fig. 3, Algorithm 1). I
call the resulting method STAC (Structure-aware Test-time Adaptive
Calibration). STAC (i) estimates target accuracy a^\hat{a} from unlabeled data by
combining thresholded confidence [Garg et al., 2022] with prediction disagreement under
input/edge perturbations [Jiang et al., 2022], then (ii) chooses the
single TT so that mean confidence equals a^\hat{a}. The method is source-free (only a
scalar threshold is carried from training), adds O⁡(1)O(1) parameters, and needs no GPU.

Complexity.

Algorithm 1 requires MM additional forward passes
of the (frozen) model over the target graph (no gradients, no retraining), plus an
O⁡(M2)O(M^{2}) pairwise-disagreement computation per node and a one-dimensional line search for
TT. In my experiments M=6M=6–1010 suffices, and the whole procedure runs in seconds on
CPU for graphs with tens of thousands of nodes; the dominant cost is the MM forward
passes, which is the same order of cost as computing MM-sample Monte Carlo dropout
uncertainty, but here applied at test time to a deterministic frozen model via input and
edge perturbations rather than at training time to a stochastic one.

Optional per-node stage.

A second, optional stage modulates the global
temperature per node, Ti=Tglob​exp⁡(c​tanh⁡(ϕi⊤​w+b))T_{i}=T_{\text{glob}}\exp(c\tanh(\phi_{i}^{\top}w+b)), where ϕi\phi_{i}
collects structure-aware signals (feature- and edge-perturbation instability,
homophily-corrected neighbour-agreement, prediction entropy, margin, and log-degree), fit
by minimizing the same label-free binned calibration loss with a small ridge penalty on
ww. By Corollary 1, this stage is not expected to help under
homogeneous shift, and I include it purely as an empirical test of that prediction: an
ablation without structural signals (logits only) isolates whether any residual benefit
comes from graph structure specifically or merely from having a higher-capacity per-node
function. Section 6.3 reports the outcome of this ablation.

Relation to the theory.

The reduction to a single scalar a^\hat{a} is not a
simplifying choice I make for convenience; it is what Theorem 1 and
Corollary 1 prove is sufficient under homogeneous shift. This is
what distinguishes STAC’s global stage from the per-node calibrators surveyed in
Section 2: those methods use a per-node function because they have no
theoretical reason to expect a global scalar to be optimal, whereas STAC uses a global
scalar because the theory says it is optimal, and only reaches for a per-node
correction as a deliberately falsifiable ablation.

6.3 STAC under shift (synthetic)

On CSBM node classification with a trained 2-layer GCN, across feature, homophily, and
heterogeneous shifts, STAC’s global stage roughly halves the ECE gap versus
source-temperature scaling and approaches the oracle. Figure 9 makes the
mechanism concrete under increasing covariate-shift intensity. The left panel shows ECE
for four methods: the uncalibrated model degrades steadily as shift grows; source-TS,
fit once on the source validation split, barely helps and at high shift intensity is

roughly half-way between source-TS and the oracle at every shift level; the oracle

perfectly; the residual gap between the dashed and solid curves is exactly what limits

7 Discussion, limitations, and open problems

I merge discussion and limitations deliberately: every limitation below is the direct,
falsifiable counterpart of a claim made in Section 4, and reading them
together is what makes the theory’s scope honest rather than merely optimistic.

7.1 The calibration slope as a diagnostic

Beyond motivating STAC, κ\kappa is useful on its own: Eq. (1) can be
evaluated from an estimate of (hs,ht,ρ)(h_{s},h_{t},\rho) alone, quantities that are far easier to
estimate without labels than accuracy itself (homophily can be estimated from a
label-propagation proxy or from the model’s own agreement statistics; SNR from feature
clustering), to predict whether a deployed GNN is likely to be over- or
under-confident on a new graph before any recalibration is attempted at all, using
Proposition 4 as a conservative severity bound.

7.2 Why source-fitted calibrators fail under shift

Every in-distribution calibrator surveyed in Section 2 (temperature
scaling itself, CaGCN, GATS, GETS, WATS, SimCalib) fits its correction (a scalar or a
per-node function) by minimizing NLL on a labeled split assumed to represent the
distribution the model will see at test time. Theorem 1 makes explicit
why this assumption breaking is fatal: the fitted correction is optimal for
κ⁡(hs,hs,ρ)=1\kappa(h_{s},h_{s},\rho)=1 by construction, but the deployment-time slope is
κ⁡(hs,ht,ρ)≠1\kappa(h_{s},h_{t},\rho)\neq 1 whenever ht≠hsh_{t}\neq h_{s}, and no amount of additional labeled
source data changes this, because the source split never observes hth_{t}. This is
precisely the mechanism Fig. 9 makes visible: source-TS’s assumption curve
is flat by construction, while the true target accuracy moves. Methods with a per-node
correction do not escape this: a per-node function fit on source data still targets
κ⁡(hs,hs,ρ)=1\kappa(h_{s},h_{s},\rho)=1 pointwise, uniformly across nodes, so it fails in exactly the same
direction as global temperature scaling, merely with additional, source-fitted degrees of
freedom that provide no protection against a shift in hh.

7.3 When does per-node calibration help?

Corollary 1 localizes the value of per-node calibration precisely: it
helps if and only if different nodes experience different effective slopes κi\kappa_{i}
(heterogeneous shift), never as a matter of raw modeling capacity. This reframes the
comparison in Section 2 between global and per-node calibrators: the
question is not “does a more expressive per-node model calibrate better” but “is the
shift itself homogeneous or heterogeneous”, which is a property of the deployment
scenario, not of the calibrator. My heterogeneous-shift ablation
(Section 6.3) is a first attempt to answer this operationally, and it
suggests that answering it well requires per-node shift-detection signals that are
more informative than the structural features I tried; see
Section 7.6.

7.4 The crux is label-free accuracy estimation

This is the paper’s central limitation, and I state it as plainly as possible: STAC’s
entire practical reliability rests on a^\hat{a}, and Section 6.4 shows
that on real, heterophilous, high-KK graphs (roman-empire, amazon-ratings), both ATC and
disagreement-based estimators can be badly wrong, overshooting true accuracy by a wide
margin, and thereby set T⋆T^{\star} in the wrong direction. Two implications follow, one
constructive and one methodological. Constructively, the oracle results
(Table 2) show the theory’s ceiling is high: if a^\hat{a} were
accurate, a single temperature would suffice everywhere I tested, from h=0.05h{=}0.05 to
h=0.84h{=}0.84 and up to K=18K{=}18 classes, so the return on improving label-free accuracy
estimation specifically for graphs is large. Methodologically, ATC and
disagreement were designed and validated primarily on covariate shift for
i.i.d. image/text data [Garg et al., 2022, Jiang et al., 2022]; graphs add at least two
confounds absent there: aggregation compounds shift across hops, so a node’s effective
shift depends on its neighbourhood’s shift, not only its own features, and homophily
itself is a further axis of shift that has no counterpart in the i.i.d. setting these
estimators were built for. A concrete, falsifiable next step is a graph-native accuracy
estimator that conditions disagreement on the local homophily estimated at the same node,
or that combines STAC’s estimator with the epistemic-uncertainty signal of
G-Δ\DeltaUQ [Trivedi et al., 2024] (Section 2), which was explicitly
validated under graph covariate and concept shift and could plausibly correct exactly the
overshoot I observe on amazon-ratings and roman-empire.

7.5 Scope of the theory

The closed forms assume Gaussian class-conditional features and are asymptotic in degree
dd (a Berry–Esseen-type correction is O(d−1/2)O(d^{-1/2})); Section 6 shows this
is already accurate at moderate degree (d≈20d\!\approx\!20) but I do not claim a
non-asymptotic, finite-degree guarantee. The CSBM is also a fixed-homophily model:
real graphs mix a global homophily with substantial node-to-node heterogeneity, which is
exactly the gap between the homogeneous-shift theory of Section 4 and the
heterogeneous-shift ablation of Section 6.3. Finally, near chance
accuracy (as on parts of roman-empire and amazon-ratings) the logistic approximation
underlying Theorem 1 is least informative, precisely the regime where
label-free accuracy estimation is also hardest (Section 7.4); these
two limitations compound rather than being independent, which is why real-graph
performance in Table 2 is weakest exactly where the pointwise theory is
weakest.

7.6 Heterogeneous shift is the open per-node problem

Corollary 1 says when per-node calibration is needed
(heterogeneous shift) but not how to detect, node by node, which effective κi\kappa_{i}
applies; my attempt (Section 6.3) with generic structural
perturbation signals moved error-detection AUROC only marginally above a
structure-free baseline. I view this negative result as informative rather than
discouraging: it narrows the open problem to finding per-node signals that specifically
track local shift severity; candidates include local homophily estimated from a
node’s immediate neighbourhood (directly motivated by Eq. (1)), a per-node
analogue of the epistemic-uncertainty estimator of Trivedi et al. [Trivedi et al., 2024], or the
per-node conformal width produced by Huang et al. [Huang et al., 2023], which is a different
notion of per-node reliability (set size) that could plausibly correlate with κi\kappa_{i}
without requiring labels.

7.7 Broader outlook

I see three natural combinations with the empirical literature surveyed in
Section 2. First, G-Δ\DeltaUQ’s per-model epistemic-uncertainty
estimate [Trivedi et al., 2024] could serve directly as the per-node shift-severity
signal that Section 7.6 shows is currently missing. Second,
the distribution-free coverage guarantees of conformalized
GNNs [Huang et al., 2023] address a set-valued, not pointwise, notion of
reliability; combining a globally-corrected temperature with a conformal wrapper is
plausible and would let the two guarantees (closed-form pointwise calibration and
distribution-free coverage) compose rather than compete. Third, GDA-SpecReg’s
risk bound [You et al., 2023] bounds target error; an accuracy bound of that kind,
if tight enough, is itself a candidate label-free a^\hat{a} for STAC, directly addressing
Section 7.4.

8 Conclusion

This paper gives, to my knowledge, the first closed-form account of how distribution
shift moves the confidence-accuracy relationship of a graph neural network: a single
calibration slope κ\kappa, explicit in source/target homophily and feature
signal-to-noise ratio, determines simultaneously the direction of miscalibration
(κ<1\kappa<1 over-confident, κ>1\kappa>1 under-confident), its magnitude (via the proven
ECE bound), and its exact correction (T⋆=1/κT^{\star}=1/\kappa, provably optimal at the
population level and provably sufficient as a single global scalar under homogeneous
shift). The theory is not a qualitative story: it predicts the measured slope and the
oracle temperature quantitatively in simulation (r=0.99r=0.99), and its central operational
consequence (a single temperature recovers calibration) holds on five real graphs
spanning two orders of magnitude in homophily and up to 1818 classes.

Turning the theory into a fully automatic, label-free recalibrator reduces to estimating
one number, target accuracy, from unlabeled data; I showed this reduction is correct in
that a good estimate (the oracle) suffices, but that today’s off-the-shelf estimators
(ATC, disagreement) are not yet accurate enough on real, heterophilous graphs to realize
the theory’s full promise. I see this not as a weakness of the theory but as its most
useful output: it isolates exactly the sub-problem (shift-robust, graph-native accuracy
estimation) on which future work should concentrate to convert closed-form understanding
into a deployable, zero-label GNN calibrator, and Section 7 lays out
concrete, falsifiable directions toward that goal, including combinations with recent
empirical uncertainty-quantification and conformal-prediction methods for GNNs. More
broadly, I hope the calibration-slope viewpoint offers a template for other
structure-dependent reliability questions on graphs: write down the smallest model that
reproduces a failure mode in closed form, derive its falsifiable consequences, and test
how far its predictions travel toward real, deployed graph learning systems.

A.8 Discussion of scope

The results are exact in the large-degree Gaussian limit (Assumption 2) with a
Bayes-direction, source-calibrated linear classifier (Assumption 3). Two
honest caveats, expanded operationally in Section 7.5: (i)
finite-degree corrections are O(d−1/2)O(d^{-1/2}) (Remark 8), negligible in the
moderate-accuracy regime but growing as accuracy approaches 12\tfrac{1}{2} or 11;
(ii) for deep nonlinear GNNs the closed forms hold only qualitatively, though the
operational consequence (a single global temperature suffices under homogeneous
shift) is confirmed on real graphs in Section 6.4
(e.g. amazon-ratings ECE →0.0240.085\!\to\!0.024 after one oracle temperature).
