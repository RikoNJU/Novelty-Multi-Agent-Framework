Abstract.

Accurate traffic flow forecasting is a crucial research topic in transportation management. However, it is a challenging problem due to rapidly changing traffic conditions, high nonlinearity of traffic flow, and complex spatial and temporal correlations of road networks.
Most existing studies either try to capture the spatial dependencies between roads using the same semantic graph over different time steps, or assume all sensors on the roads are equally likely to be connected regardless of the distance between them. However, we observe that the spatial dependencies between roads indeed change over time, and two distant roads are not likely to be helpful to each other when predicting the traffic flow, both of which limit the performance of existing studies.
In this paper, we propose Temporal Graph Learning Recurrent Neural Network (TGLRN) to address these problems.
More precisely, to effectively model the nature of time series, we leverage Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to dynamically construct a graph at each time step, thereby capturing the time-evolving spatial dependencies between roads (i.e., microscopic view). Simultaneously, we provide the Adaptive Structure Information to the model, ensuring that close and consecutive sensors are considered to be more important for predicting the traffic flow (i.e., macroscopic view).
Furthermore, to endow TGLRN with robustness, we introduce an edge sampling strategy when constructing the graph at each time step, which eventually leads to further improvements on the model performance.
Experimental results on four commonly used real-world benchmark datasets show the effectiveness of TGLRN.

1. Introduction

Research on intelligent transport systems has been actively conducted recently as
traffic information becomes easily accessible thanks to real-time collection and management technology.
Accurate traffic flow forecasting based on historically observed data is a crucial part in intelligent transport systems, which makes it possible to maximize the use of transportation facilities, increase transportation efficiency, and promote the convenience and safety of people. However, accurate forecasting of future traffic flows based on historical data is challenging because it is necessary to simultaneously capture the complex spatial dependencies and temporal dynamics of road networks.

Figure 1. (a) Traffic flow, and (b) sensor locations that exist consecutively. From a macroscopic view, consecutive sensors exhibit similar trends (upward or downward) due to spatial proximity. (c) At the microscopic level, we employ RNNs to model the dynamic spatial dependencies between sensor pairs, constructing different weighted graphs for each time step.

For traffic forecasting, traditional statistical machine learning models such as historical average (HA), autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) (Box and Pierce, 1970), and support vector regression (SVR) (Wu et al., 2004) have been widely used. However, as they fall short of capturing complex spatio-temporal relationships between roads, researchers have been recently adopting deep learning techniques (Tedjopurnomo et al., 2020).
Specifically, temporal dependencies have been modeled using recurrent neural networks (RNN) (Li et al., 2017; Xie et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2021; Bai et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2018a). Additionally, some studies have explored the use of convolutional neural networks (CNN) (Yu et al., 2017; Guo et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2019; Lu et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2020; Li and Zhu, 2021; Fang et al., 2021) and the attention mechanism (Park et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2020; Lan et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2019) for modeling temporal dependency.
Besides, as roads that are geographically adjacent to each other
have high spatial dependencies, a road network can be expressed based on a graph whose nodes denote sensors on the roads and edges denote the connections between the sensors where the edge weights are determined by the distance between the sensors.
With a graph constructed in this way, spatial dependencies can be modeled using graph convolution networks (GCN) (Kipf and Welling, 2016; Defferrard et al., 2016; Bruna et al., 2013; Veličković et al., 2017). However, since they rely on a graph constructed with adjacent roads, it is impossible to learn spatial dependencies between geographically distant nodes. To address this problem and discover the hidden spatial dependencies between roads, there are studies that use learned graph structures (Wu et al., 2019; Shang et al., 2021; Cao et al., 2020), graph Laplacian (Bai et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021), or dynamic time warping (DTW) (Li and Zhu, 2021; Fang et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2020).

Although current deep learning-based methods have shown effectiveness in forecasting traffic flow by capturing both spatial and temporal dependencies, they are constrained in two significant aspects: First, existing methods often overlook the dynamic nature of spatial relationships among roads, which change in response to real-time conditions from a microscopic perspective. As depicted in Figure 1, to accurately reflect the evolving spatial dependencies of traffic flow from a microscopic viewpoint, we could consider modeling that constructs more refined graphs at each time step. Typically, existing approaches utilize the same semantic information graph
at every time step, lacking the capability to adjust the structures of spatial dependencies dynamically at each time step.
While some methods attempt to capture these Dynamic Time Graphs (Lan et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2022; Han et al., 2021; Ye et al., 2022), they differ significantly from our model for the following reasons.

Figure 2. (a) Traffic flow, and (b) location of consecutive sensors in two geographically distant areas. The two geographically distant areas exhibit distinct trends. Hence, we construct a graph where road pairs are likely to be connected if the roads are within a certain distance.

Second, existing methods
assume that all sensors on the roads are equally likely to be connected by edges (even though the weights may differ) regardless of the distance between them.
More precisely, methods that rely on the learned graph structures, and graph Laplacian construct graphs in which every pair of nodes is connected (i.e., complete graph).
Moreover, methods that rely on DTW constructs graphs in which two nodes are connected solely based on the similarity between their traffic flow values, regardless of the distance between them.
Existing methods that adopt Dynamic Time Graphs (Lan et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2022; Han et al., 2021; Ye et al., 2022) also do not consider the relative distance of the sensors.
However, two distant roads are not likely to be helpful to each other when predicting the traffic flow of each road, because, in general, roads in geographically distant areas have different trends, as shown in Figure  2.
Even if two roads in geographically distant areas have shown similar patterns in the past, when an event (e.g., traffic jam or local event) occurs on one road, this road would exhibit a completely different pattern from the other road as these two roads are distant from each other.
Hence, we argue that the strategy for learning graph structures should involve a targeted approach, focusing on geographically proximate sensors positioned consecutively (i.e., macroscopic view), instead of incorporating every sensor available.

Furthermore, in dynamic environments like traffic networks, robustness in prediction is paramount. Consider the myriad of unpredictable events that can influence traffic, such as sudden changes in weather, unplanned roadworks, or unforeseen public events. Such unpredictable factors can drastically change the traffic patterns on roads. Even subtle changes in external factors can ripple through the traffic network, causing significant and sometimes surprising changes in flow and congestion. These myriad variations highlight the need for models that are not only accurate but also robust. A model that is too rigid or overly tailored to past patterns may fail to adapt to new and unexpected scenarios, leading to suboptimal predictions. The inherent unpredictability and complexity of traffic patterns further underscore the necessity for robust prediction mechanisms in traffic forecasting.

Figure 3. Overall architecture of TGLRN.

In the light of the aforementioned limitations, we propose Temporal Graph Learning Recurrent Neural Network (TGLRN) for traffic flow forecasting. Unlike existing models, TGLRN dynamically constructs an input graph at each time step by introducing node embeddings that change over time (i.e., microscopic view).
More precisely, to effectively model the nature of time series, we dynamically compute the hidden state of each node using the RNN structure at each time step, and construct dynamic graphs using the time-varying node embeddings (Refer to Figure 1(c)).
Moreover, we introduce the Adaptive Structure Information to the model in which graphs are constructed by considering the relative geographical distance between the nodes (i.e., macroscopic view).
Specifically, we provide the model another graph in which consecutive nodes that are located within a certain geographical distance from one another exhibit high spatial dependencies (Refer to Figure 1 and Figure  2).

Furthermore, to endow TGLRN with robustness, we introduce an edge sampling strategy when constructing the graph at each time step, which eventually leads to further improvements on the model performance. Through extensive experiments on four commonly used real-world benchmark datasets, we demonstrate that TGLRN shows superior performance compared with state-of-the-art models.

2. Related Work

2.1. Traffic Flow Forecasting

Classical and Deep learning-based approaches.
Classical statistical machine learning models used for traffic forecasting include HA, ARIMA(Box and Pierce, 1970), KNN, and SVR(Wu et al., 2004). Although these methods achieved promising results, these models make predictions solely based on temporal data from previous time steps, ignoring spatial dependencies between roads. Recent deep learning-based approaches capture complex spatio-temporal dependencies and show more accurate prediction performance. More precisely, (Zhang et al., 2018b; Yao et al., 2018a; Yao et al., 2018b) learn temporal features using RNN and spatial features using CNN both of which facilitate simultaneous modeling of complex spatio-temporal dependencies.
However, since the above models are primarily designed for grid-structured data, adapting them to road networks is not straightforward.

Graph-based approaches.
In order to address the above challenge, recent studies adopt GCN (Kipf and Welling, 2016) to perform spatial-temporal forecasting. More precisely, DCRNN (Li et al., 2017) models the spatial dependencies between roads by applying a diffusion process to the graph structure, and the temporal dependencies using the sequence to sequence architecture based on RNN. STGCN (Yu et al., 2017) proposes learning spatio-temporal dependencies using GCN and temporal convolution. ASTGCN (Guo et al., 2019) captures dynamic spatial and temporal correlations using spatial-temporal attention in graph convolution and temporal convolution.
However, as these models rely on the geographic distance between roads to construct graphs, they overlook the spatial dependencies between distant roads despite possible dependencies therein.
Hence, in order to capture spatial dependencies that cannot be captured using geographical distance, Graph Wavenet (Wu et al., 2019) learns the adjacency matrix through learnable node embeddings, and AGCRN (Bai et al., 2020) learns graph Laplacian through learnable node embeddings. Furthermore, to model Dynamic Time Graphs that change over time, methodologies such as (Lan et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2022; Han et al., 2021; Ye et al., 2022) exist.

3. Preliminaries

3.1. Problem Definition

We define a road network as a directed graph 𝒢={𝒱,ℰ,𝒜}\mathcal{G}=\{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{A}\}, where 𝒱\mathcal{V} is the set of nodes with N=|𝒱|N=\left|\mathcal{V}\right| traffic sensors, ℰ⊆𝒱×𝒱\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V} is the set of edges, and 𝒜∈ℝN×N\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N} is an adjacency matrix of 𝒢\mathcal{G}. In general, 𝒢\mathcal{G} is constructed based on the relationship between spatial neighbors, and it can be denoted by 𝒢s​p={𝒱,ℰs​p,𝒜s​p}\mathcal{G}^{sp}=\{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}^{sp},\mathcal{A}^{sp}\}, where 𝒜s​p∈ℝN×N\mathcal{A}^{sp}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
connects consecutively existing sensors of the actual road network:

(1)

𝒜i​js​p={1,if sensors ​i​ and ​j​ exist consecutively,0,otherwise. \mathcal{A}_{ij}^{sp}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if sensors }i\text{ and }j\text{ exist consecutively,}\\
0,&\text{otherwise. }\end{cases}

In this research, we define ‘consecutively existing’ sensors using two distinct and separate configurations across datasets. The primary configuration aligns with the sequence of travel on a roadway: a sensor is considered ‘consecutive’ to another if it is the immediate next sensor encountered along the travel path. Specifically, sensor jj is ‘consecutive’ to sensor ii if a vehicle passes by sensor ii first and then sensor jj. The secondary configuration is predicated on spatial proximity, where ‘consecutively existing’ refers to a small, predefined number of sensors that are geographically nearest to a reference sensor.

The traffic flow data observed on node ii at time tt is denoted by 𝐱ti∈ℝF\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{F}, where FF is the number of features. 𝐗(t)=(𝐱t1,𝐱t2,…,𝐱tN)∈ℝN×F\mathbf{X}^{(t)}=\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{1},\mathbf{x}_{t}^{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{t}^{N}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times F} denotes the traffic flow matrix of the entire road network at time step tt. The problem of traffic forecasting is defined as learning a function ℱ\mathcal{F} to forecast the next TT time steps based on data collected for previous T′T^{\prime} time steps, and the road network 𝒢\mathcal{G}:

(2)

[𝐗(t−T′+1):t,𝒢]→ℱ[𝐗(t+1):(t+T)].\left[\mathbf{X}^{\left(t-T^{\prime}+1\right):t},\mathcal{G}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{F}}}{{\rightarrow}}\left[\mathbf{X}^{(t+1):(t+T)}\right].

3.2. Dynamically Changing Graph Structures

We are interested in graph structures that dynamically change over time. Let 𝒢t={𝒱,ℰt,𝒜t}\mathcal{G}_{t}=\{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{t},\mathcal{A}_{t}\} represent the directed road network at time step tt. The sequence of dynamically changing graph structures over time is denoted by {𝒢(t−T′+1),…,𝒢t}\left\{\mathcal{G}_{\left(t-T^{\prime}+1\right)},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{t}\right\}.
Then, the problem of traffic forecasting defined in Equation 2 is reformulated as follows:

(3)

[𝐗(t−T′+1):t,𝒢(t−T′+1):t]→ℱ[𝐗(t+1):(t+T)].\left[\mathbf{X}^{\left(t-T^{\prime}+1\right):t},\mathcal{G}_{\left(t-T^{\prime}+1\right):t}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{F}}}{{\rightarrow}}\left[\mathbf{X}^{(t+1):(t+T)}\right].

4. Proposed Method: TGLRN

4.1. Model Overview

In this section, we begin by explaining the input layer (Section 4.2) followed by the two main components, i.e., Dynamic Graph Construction Block (Section 4.3) and Spatio-Temporal Block (Section 4.4). Lastly, we introduce the prediction layer (Section 4.5) and the final loss function (Section 4.6). Figure 3 shows the overall architecture of the Temporal Graph Learning Recurrent Neural Network (TGLRN).

4.2. Input Layer

The input layer consists of a single linear layer without non-linearity. This layer is responsible for transforming the feature dimension of the input data into a higher dimensional space.
That is, the input data 𝐗(t)∈ℝN×F\mathbf{X}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times F} is transformed into 𝐗~(t)∈ℝN×D\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D},
where F<DF<D.

4.3. Dynamic Graph Construction Block

4.3.1. Dynamic Node Embedding

As described in Section 1, our objective is to model the evolving spatial dependencies among nodes within a road network accurately. To capture such spatial dependencies, it is necessary to construct different graphs over time, and our goal is to learn the graph structure at each time step. As shown in Figure 3, we first define two initial node embeddings Es​t​_​0∈ℝN×dE_{st\_0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d} and Ee​d​_​0∈ℝN×dE_{ed\_0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d} for constructing a graph, where NN and dd denote the number of nodes and the dimension size of node embeddings, respectively. By using the RNN structure (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997; Chung et al., 2014), we obtain intermediate dynamics of hidden states Es​t​(t)∈ℝN×dE_{st}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d} and Ee​d​(t)∈ℝN×dE_{ed}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d} (i.e., node embedding) of nodes as follows:

(4)

z=σ⁡(fz​([E∗​(t); Linear​(𝐗(t−1))])),\displaystyle z=\sigma\left(f_{z}\left(\left[E_{*}(t);\textsf{ Linear}\left(\mathbf{X}^{(t-1)}\right)\right]\right)\right),

r=σ⁡(fr​([E∗​(t); Linear​(𝐗(t−1))])),\displaystyle r=\sigma\left(f_{r}\left(\left[E_{*}(t);\textsf{ Linear}\left(\mathbf{X}^{(t-1)}\right)\right]\right)\right),

h′=g⁡([r⊙E∗​(t); Linear​(𝐗(t−1))]),\displaystyle h^{\prime}=g\left(\left[r\odot E_{*}(t);\textsf{ Linear}\left(\mathbf{X}^{(t-1)}\right)\right]\right),

E∗​(t−1)=(1−z)⊙h′+z⊙E∗​(t),\displaystyle E_{*}(t-1)=(1-z)\odot h^{\prime}+z\odot E_{*}(t),

where fzf_{z} and frf_{r} are neural networks, ⊙\odot denotes the Hadamard product, σ\sigma denotes sigmoid activation function, ∗* can be either s​tst or e​ded. Note that given the current time step tt, we run through the past. i.e., the next time step is t−1t-1.
We can simplify the above equations as:

(5)

E∗​(t−1)=GRU-Cell​(E∗​(t),𝐗(t−1)).E_{*}(t-1)=\textsf{GRU-Cell}({E}_{*}(t),\mathbf{X}^{(t-1)}).

In the formulation of our Dynamic Node Embedding model, we establish two separate embeddings, Es​t​(t)E_{st}(t) and Ee​d​(t)E_{ed}(t), to distinctly address the directional aspects of traffic flows within the network. This separation is critical for the implementation of the Diffusion Convolution Operation (Li et al., 2017)—detailed in Section 4.4.1—which relies on a directional graph to effectively model traffic flows. The necessity to differentiate traffic direction - distinguishing upstream from downstream patterns - is crucial for this operation and motivates our use of Es​t​(t)E_{st}(t) and Ee​d​(t)E_{ed}(t) to construct a graph that inherently reflects directional influences, thereby enhancing the accuracy of the temporal-spatial dependency representation in dynamic road networks.

4.3.2. Graph Construction Layer

In this section, we aim to construct graph 𝒢t={𝒱,ℰt,𝒜t}\mathcal{G}_{t}=\{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{t},\mathcal{A}_{t}\} for each time step tt. As shown in Figure 4, at each time step tt, the Graph Construction Layer has learnable node embeddings Es​t​_​b​a​s​e​(t)E_{st\_base}(t) and Ee​d​_​b​a​s​e​(t)E_{ed\_base}(t) with the same dimensions as Es​t​(t)E_{st}(t) and Ee​d​(t)E_{ed}(t), and Es​t​_​b​a​s​e​(t)E_{st\_base}(t) and Ee​d​_​b​a​s​e​(t)E_{ed\_base}(t) are used for gating. Node embeddings after gating are computed as follows:

(6)

E∗_​g​a​t​e​(t)=E∗​(t)⊙σ⁡( Linear ​(E∗_​b​a​s​e​(t))),E_{*\_gate}(t)=E_{*}(t)\odot\sigma\left(\textsf{ Linear }\left(E_{*\_base}(t)\right)\right),

where ⊙\odot denotes the Hadamard product, σ\sigma denotes sigmoid activation function, ∗* can be either s​tst or e​ded. The above gating process facilitates the model to pass only important information when constructing the graph.

Learning Important Structure.
Having obtained node embeddings Es​t​_​g​a​t​e​(t)E_{st\_gate}(t) and Ee​d​_​g​a​t​e​(t)E_{ed\_gate}(t), we compute the weight of the adjacency matrix 𝒜t\mathcal{A}_{t}.
Let ωi​jt\omega_{{i}{j}}^{t} denote the edge weight of adjacency matrix 𝒜t\mathcal{A}_{t}, where vi,vj∈𝒱​ and ​(vi,vj)v_{i},v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}\text{ and }(v_{i},v_{j}) ∈ℰt\in\mathcal{E}_{t}.
At each time step tt, the node embeddings Es​t​_​g​a​t​e​(t)=(es​t,1t,es​t,2tCLOSE,E_{st\_gate}(t)=(e_{st,1}^{t},e_{st,2}^{t}, OPEN⋯,es​t,Nt)∈ℝN×d\cdots,e_{st,N}^{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}, Ee​d​_​g​a​t​e​(t)=(ee​d,1t,ee​d,2t,⋯,ee​d,Nt)E_{ed\_gate}(t)=(e_{ed,1}^{t},e_{ed,2}^{t},\cdots,e_{ed,N}^{t}) ∈ℝN×d\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d} are used to construct the edge weight ω\omega as follows:

(7)

where Linear is a linear layer without non-linearity. Note that ωi​jt\omega_{ij}^{t} determines how likely viv_{i} and vjv_{j} are connected at time step tt. As learning progresses, the ω\omega value continuously increases or decreases, which leads the ω\omega value to be very large or very small. This causes the distribution of {ω11t,ω12t,⋯,ωn​nt}\{\omega_{11}^{t},\omega_{12}^{t},\cdots,\omega_{nn}^{t}\} to have a high variance, and disturbs the construction of a valid dynamic graph at each time step tt. (8)

ω11t,ω12t,⋯,ωn​nt←normalize⁡(ω11t,ω12t,⋯,ωn​nt),\omega_{11}^{t},\omega_{12}^{t},\cdots,\omega_{nn}^{t}\leftarrow\operatorname{normalize}\left(\omega_{11}^{t},\omega_{12}^{t},\cdots,\omega_{nn}^{t}\right),

where normalize⁡(⋅)\operatorname{normalize}\left(\cdot\right) function normalizes the mean and standard deviation of the input data into 0 and α\alpha, respectively. α\alpha is a hyperparameter that controls the influence of the neural network, and we set α=1\alpha=1. Finally, sigmoid is used as the activation function.
Then, to construct a dynamic graph based on the structure that is important at time step tt, we associate each edge (vi,vj)∈ℰt(v_{i},v_{j})\in\mathcal{E}_{t} with a random variable pi​jt∼Bernoulli​(ωi​jt)p_{ij}^{t}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\omega_{ij}^{t}), where (vi,vj)(v_{i},v_{j}) remains in ℰt\mathcal{E}_{t} if pi​jt=1p_{ij}^{t}=1, and is dropped otherwise. Our goal is to make pi​jtp^{t}_{ij} learnable so that the constructed graph preserves the important structure at time step tt. However, as pi​jt=1p_{ij}^{t}=1 is a discrete random variable, we cannot directly calculate the gradient of ωi​jt\omega_{ij}^{t}, which hinders an end-to-end training of the model. Hence, we relax the discrete pi​jt=1p_{ij}^{t}=1 to be a continuous variable in (0, 1) by using the Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization trick (Maddison et al., 2016; Jang et al., 2016) as follows:

(9)

pi​jt←Sigmoid⁡((log⁡δ−log⁡(1−δ)+log⁡ωi​jt−log⁡(1−ωi​jt))/τ),\small p_{{i}{j}}^{t}\leftarrow\operatorname{Sigmoid}((\log{\delta}-\log(1-\delta)+\log{\omega_{{i}{j}}^{t}}-\log(1-\omega_{{i}{j}}^{t}))/\tau),

where δ∼Uniform⁡(0,1)\delta\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1), τ\tau is a temperature hyperarameter.
As temperature hyper-parameter τ\tau approaches 00, pi​jtp_{{i}{j}}^{t} gets closer to being binary. We set τ=1\tau=1 as we want the edge weight to vary according to its importance.

The Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization trick is a commonly chosen method for graph structure learning (Zhu et al., 2021b). By adopting the Gumbel-Softmax trick, TGLRN can learn important edges based on the edge weight ωi​jt\omega_{{i}{j}}^{t} rather than always selecting the edge with highest ωi​jt\omega_{{i}{j}}^{t}. This enables our graph structure learner to flexibly control the characteristics (e.g., sparsity) of the final output graph in data-driven manner.

Edge Sampling Strategy.
We find that rather than incorporating all the edges whose weight pi​jt>0p_{ij}^{t}>0, randomly selecting edges not only makes the training more stable, but also leads to further improvements on the model performance. More precisely, the random edge sample strategy is defined as follows:

(10)

𝒜i​jt={0, if ​γ<ρ,pi​jt, otherwise, \small\mathcal{A}^{t}_{ij}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }\gamma<\rho,\\
p^{t}_{ij},&\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

where ρ∼Uniform⁡(0,1)\rho\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1) and γ\gamma is a hyperarameter. The hyperparameter γ\gamma controls the sampling probability, e.g., if γ=0.4\gamma=0.4, 40%40\% of the edges are sampled and used.
Note that the above sampling strategy can be considered as a form of data augmentation, often referred to as ‘graph structural perturbation’ in the literature on graph self-supervised learning (Zhu et al., 2021a; Zhu et al., 2020; Thakoor et al., 2022). This strategy effectively introduces robustness to perturbation (i.e., noise) in the learned representations, as it exposes the model to various structural changes in the training data.

Sampling: Gumbel-Softmax vs Edge.
In our model, both the Gumbel-Softmax Trick and Edge Sampling are used, each with a distinct purpose. The Gumbel-Softmax Trick involves sampling to approximate gradients for discrete variables, essential for learning the importance of edges in the adjacency matrix. Conversely, Edge Sampling is used as data augmentation, which enhances the model’s robustness to data variations by generating varied samples from the learned distribution. In summary, the two sampling strategies serve different but crucial roles in model learning and generalization.

4.3.3. Structure Information

In this section, we define the Structure Information of the road networks that consists of the Target Space, which is a set of nodes that are likely to be connected by edges.
More precisely, we utilize an adjacency matrix 𝒜s​p\mathcal{A}^{sp}, in which nodes that exist consecutively to each other are connected (Equation 1). We assume that each node in 𝒜s​p\mathcal{A}^{sp} has a self-loop, that is, 𝒜i​is​p=1\mathcal{A}_{ii}^{sp}=1 for i∈{1,⋯,N}i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}.

Definition 0.

(Structure Information 𝒮k∈{0,1}N×N\mathcal{S}^{k}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}) Let d⁡(vi,vj)d(v_{i},v_{j}) denote the shortest path distance between vi∈𝒱v_{i}\in\mathcal{V} and vj∈𝒱v_{j}\in\mathcal{V} in terms of 𝒜s​p\mathcal{A}^{sp}.
For vi∈𝒱v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}, we define the Target Space 𝒯\mathcal{T} of viv_{i} over 𝒮k\mathcal{S}^{k} as 𝒯⁡(𝒮k,vi)={vj∈𝒱∣d⁡(vi,vj)≤k}\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{S}^{k},v_{i})=\{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}\mid d(v_{i},v_{j})\leq k\}, where k∈ℕk\in\mathbb{N}.
In other words, 𝒯⁡(𝒮k,vi)\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{S}^{k},v_{i}) denotes the set of nodes whose shortest path distance from viv_{i} is less than or equal to kk.
Then, each vi∈𝒱v_{i}\in\mathcal{V} connects all vj∈𝒯⁡(𝒮k,vi)v_{j}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{S}^{k},v_{i}) with an edge weight 𝒮i​jk=1\mathcal{S}^{k}_{{i}{j}}=1. 𝒮k\mathcal{S}^{k} is formally defined in an adjacency form as follows:

(11)

𝒮i​jk={1,d⁡(vi,vj)≤k0, otherwise. \mathcal{S}_{ij}^{k}=\begin{cases}1,&d(v_{i},v_{j})\leq k\\
0,&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Some existing methods construct an adjacency matrix by connecting all nodes within a certain distance, utilizing a purely distance-based approach that relies on the absolute distances between sensors. This reliance may introduce bias as the graph’s connectivity heavily fluctuates based on distance, potentially distorting the accurate representation of the sensor network and its inherent traffic patterns. In contrast, our proposed Structure Information Sk{S}^{k}, considering all consecutive sensors within kk-hops from a specific sensor, encapsulates local characteristics and maintains the relative spatial positioning among sensors.
This comprehensive view is particularly valuable for capturing and analyzing spatial patterns and trends. Consequently, 𝒮k\mathcal{S}^{k} offers a less biased, more nuanced perspective of the sensor network, effectively preserving relative spatial information.

4.3.4. Adaptive Structure Information

To incorporate a macroscopic view of the road networks, the most straightforward and intuitive approach is to select from 𝒜t\mathcal{A}^{t} only the nodes that belong to 𝒮k\mathcal{S}^{k}. More formally, the edge weight of the adjacency matrix 𝒜i​jt\mathcal{A}_{ij}^{t} at time step tt can be described as follows:

(12)

𝒜i​jt={0, if ​vj∉𝒯⁡(𝒮k,vi),pi​jt, otherwise.\mathcal{A}_{ij}^{t}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }v_{j}\notin\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{S}^{k},v_{i}),\\
p_{ij}^{t},&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

By integrating 𝒮k\mathcal{S}^{k} and considering kk as a hyperparameter, this framework effectively encapsulates the macroscopic structural patterns within the road networks.
However, rather than uniformly applying the same kk-hop range for all nodes and all time steps, we propose a more adaptive model that considers varying hop ranges for each node and time step. Assume we have a Structure Information Group, denoted as 𝒮​𝒢L={𝒮1,𝒮2,…,𝒮L}\mathcal{SG}_{L}=\{\mathcal{S}^{1},\mathcal{S}^{2},\ldots,\mathcal{S}^{L}\}.

Definition 0.

(Hop Range Selector Rt:𝒱→{1,2,3,…,L}R_{t}:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\{1,2,3,\ldots,L\})
The Hop Range Selector Rt​(vi)R_{t}(v_{i}) determines the number of hops that a node viv_{i} should consider in the network 𝒜s​p\mathcal{A}^{sp} at time tt. This function assigns to each node a natural number l∈{1,2,3,…,L}l\in\{1,2,3,\ldots,L\}, representing the hop range that viv_{i} is deemed to interact with within the network at that time.

Definition 0.

(Adaptive Structure Information ℐt∈{0,1}N×N\mathcal{I}_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N})
For node vi∈𝒱v_{i}\in\mathcal{V} at time tt, we define the Dynamic Target Space 𝒯𝒟\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{D}} of viv_{i} over ℐt\mathcal{I}_{t}, as the set of nodes that are within a certain hop range hh, which maximizes the probability of the Hop Range Selector RtR_{t}. This is formally given by:

(13)

𝒯𝒟​(ℐt,vi):=𝒯⁡(𝒮h,vi),\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{I}_{t},v_{i}):=\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{S}^{h},v_{i}),

where hh is chosen as the hop range for which viv_{i} has the highest probability of needing to consider, based on RtR_{t}, out of all possible hop range l∈{1,2,3,…,L}l\in\{1,2,3,\ldots,L\}. Thus, hh is determined by:

(14)

h=argmax𝑙​P​(Rt​(vi)=l).h=\underset{l}{\operatorname{argmax}}\ P(R_{t}(v_{i})=l).

For the given time step tt, each node vi∈𝒱v_{i}\in\mathcal{V} is then connected to all nodes within its Dynamic Target Space vj∈𝒯𝒟​(ℐt,vi)v_{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{I}_{t},v_{i}) with an edge weight (ℐt)i​j=1(\mathcal{I}_{t})_{ij}=1. ℐt\mathcal{I}_{t} is formally defined in an adjacency form as follows:

(15)

(ℐt)i​j={1,if ​vj∈𝒯𝒟​(ℐt,vi)0,otherwise.(\mathcal{I}_{t})_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }v_{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{I}_{t},v_{i})\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Now, the remaining issue is how to model P⁡(Rt​(vi)=l)P(R_{t}(v_{i})=l). To effectively model the nature of time series, we again leverage Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs). Similar to the approach described in Section 4.3.1, we first define the initial node embedding Eh​_​0∈ℝN×mE_{h\_0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times m}, where NN and mm denote the number of nodes and the dimension size of node embeddings, respectively. By using the Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) (Chung et al., 2014), we obtain the intermediate dynamics of hidden states Eh​(t)∈ℝN×mE_{h}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times m} for nodes as follows:

(16)

Eh​(t−1)=GRU-Cell​(Eh​(t),𝐗(t−1)).E_{h}(t-1)=\textsf{GRU-Cell}(E_{h}(t),\mathbf{X}^{(t-1)}).

Let Eh​(t)i∈ℝmE_{h}(t)_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m} denote the hidden state embedding of node viv_{i} at time step tt. We introduce a neural network, denoted as N​N​(⋅):ℝm→ℝLNN(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{L}, which is composed of multiple linear layers culminating in a softmax function. Then, the probability P⁡(Rt​(vi)=l)P(R_{t}(v_{i})=l) is given by:

(17)

P⁡(Rt​(vi)=l)=N​N​(Eh​(t)i)​[l],P(R_{t}(v_{i})=l)=NN(E_{h}(t)_{i})[l],

where l∈{1,2,3,…,L}l\in\{1,2,3,\ldots,L\}. Through this network, we are able to learn Adaptive Structure Information for each time step tt. Due to the utilization of the argmax\operatorname{argmax} function in Equation (14), we incorporate the Gumbel-Softmax technique to permit gradient propagation during the learning process.

In the final step, to capture the macroscopic view of the road networks using Adaptive Structure Information, the adjacency matrix 𝒜t\mathcal{A}^{t} is pruned to include only nodes that are part of ℐt\mathcal{I}_{t}. Formally, the edge weights in the adjacency matrix at each timestep are defined as follows:

(18)

𝒜i​jt={0, if ​vj∉𝒯𝒟​(ℐt,vi)pi​jt, otherwise \mathcal{A}_{ij}^{t}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }v_{j}\notin\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{I}_{t},v_{i})\\
p_{ij}^{t},&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

4.4. Spatio-Temporal Block

4.4.1. Spatial Processing Layer (SPL)

In SPL, the encoded input signal (Section 4.2), and the adjacency matrix constructed by Dynamic Graph Construction Block (Section 4.3) are used to capture the spatial dependencies between the roads. We adopt diffusion convolution layer (Li et al., 2017), which models the spatial dependency of traffic flow as a diffusion process. Through forward and reverse direction diffusion processes, diffusion convolution provides flexibility to models that use asymmetric adjacency matrices.
More precisely, the diffusion convolution operation over the graph signal 𝐗~∈ℝN×D\tilde{\mathbf{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}, filter fθf_{\theta}, and the adjacency matrix 𝒜∈ℝN×N\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N} is defined as follows:

(19)

+∑k=0K−1(θk,2(𝑫I−1𝒜⊤)k)𝐗~:,p\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(\theta_{k,2}\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{I}^{-1}\mathcal{A}^{\top}\right)^{k}\right)\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{:,p}

where p∈{1,⋯,D}p\in\{1,\cdots,D\}, 𝜽∈ℝK×2\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times 2} is a learnable matrix, KK is the number of the diffusion steps. 𝑫O−1​𝒜\boldsymbol{D}_{O}^{-1}\mathcal{A} and 𝑫I−1​𝒜⊤\boldsymbol{D}_{I}^{-1}\mathcal{A}^{\top} are state transition matrices. Here

With the convolution operation ⋆𝒢\star_{\mathcal{G}}, the Diffusion Convolutional Layer that maps DD-dimensional features to D′D^{\prime}-dimensional features can be defined as follows:

(20)

4.4.2. Temporal Processing Layer (TPL)

To extract the temporal dynamics from the traffic flows, we use 1D temporal convolution (Yu et al., 2017) followed by the Gate Tanh Unit (GTU) (Dauphin et al., 2017) along the temporal dimension. Let 𝓧∈ℝN×T′×D\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times T^{\prime}\times D} be the input hidden representation of TPL, where NN is the number of nodes, T′T^{\prime} is the input time steps, DD is the input channel dimension. Using the convolution kernel Λ∈ℝK​s×D×2​D\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{Ks\times D\times 2D}, input 𝓧\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}} is transformed into [U,V]∈ℝN×(T′−K​s+1)×(2​D)[U,V]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\left(T^{\prime}-Ks+1\right)\times\left(2D\right)}, where K​sKs is temporal convolution kernel size and U,V∈ℝN×(T′−K​s+1)×DU,V\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times(T^{\prime}-Ks+1)\times D}. Then, the temporal convolution using GTU is defined as follows:

(23)

𝓧∗𝒯Λ=T​a​n​h​(U)⊙σ⁡(V),\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}*_{\mathcal{T}}\Lambda=Tanh(U)\odot\sigma(V),

where UU,VV are inputs of the gates in GTU, ⊙\odot denotes the Hadamard product, and T​a​n​hTanh and σ\sigma denote Tanh and Sigmoid activation functions, respectively. We also adopt the residual structure in TPL, which can be written as follows:

(24)

TPL(𝓧)=LayerNorm(𝓧∗𝒯Λ+𝓧:,T′−Ks+1:,:),\operatorname{TPL}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\right)=\operatorname{LayerNorm}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}*_{\mathcal{T}}\Lambda+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{:,T^{\prime}-Ks+1:,:}\right),

where TPL⁡(𝓧)∈ℝN×(T′−K​s+1)×D\operatorname{TPL}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\left(T^{\prime}-Ks+1\right)\times D}.

4.4.3. Output Layer

Since padding is not involved when performing 1D temporal convolution in TPL, the time dimension of the data is compressed as it is processed by several consecutive Spatio-Temporal Layers. In the output layer, to compress all the temporal dimension information, 1D temporal convolution is performed using a temporal kernel with the same size as the temporal dimension of the input hidden representation. Therefore, the result of the output layer has ℝN×D\mathbb{R}^{N\times D} dimension.

4.5. Prediction Layer

As shown in Figure 3, TGLRN consists of multiple Spatio-Temporal Blocks. Let 𝓞𝒊∈ℝN×D\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D} be the output of the ii-th Spatio-Temporal Block. Then, we concatenate all outputs of Spatial-Temporal Blocks along the feature dimension, and feed it into the Prediction Layer, which is composed of a Linear Layer and produces predictions for future TT time steps as follows:

(25)

[𝐗^(t+1),⋯,𝐗^(t+T)]=[𝓞1,⋯,𝓞n]​Wp+bp,\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{\left(t+1\right)},\cdots,\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{\left(t+T\right)}\right]=\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\right]W_{p}+b_{p},

where nn is the number of Spatio-Temporal Blocks, Wp∈ℝn​D×T×FW_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{nD\times T\times F} is a learnable parameter and bp∈ℝT×Fb_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times F} is the bias, which is added node-wise. Here FF is the number of features we want to predict.
We set F=1F=1 because we want to predict the traffic flow from the model.

Let [𝐗(t−T′+1),⋯,𝐗t]\left[\mathbf{X}^{\left(t-T^{\prime}+1\right)},\cdots,\mathbf{X}^{t}\right] be the input for TGLRN. Then, the model predicts values [𝐗^(t+1),⋯,𝐗^(t+T)]\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{\left(t+1\right)},\cdots,\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{\left(t+T\right)}\right] for the future TT time steps. (26)

5. Experiments

5.1. Experimental Setup

Datasets.
In order to evaluate the performance of the TGLRN, we conduct experiments on four real-world highway traffic datasets, i.e., PeMS03, PeMS04, PeMS07 and PeMS08, which are collected by the Caltrans Performance Measurement System (PeMS) in real time every 30 seconds (Chen et al., 2001). The traffic data are aggregated into 5-minutes intervals, which means there are 12 time steps in the traffic flow per hour (i.e., T=12T=12). The input data is re-scaled using Z-score normalization. The details of the datasets are summarized in Table 2.

Baselines.
We compare TGLRN with the following baselines:

•

HA (Hamilton, 2020) uses an average value of inputs as prediction of the next time step value.

•

ARIMA (Box and Pierce, 1970) is a representative classical time series model for predicting future values.

•

FC-LSTM (Sutskever et al., 2014) uses RNNs with fully connected LSTM hidden units.

•

DCRNN (Li et al., 2017) combines diffusion convolution with recurrent neural networks in a sequence-to-sequence manner.

•

STGCN (Yu et al., 2017) combines graph convolutional structure with gated temporal convolution.

•

ASTGCN (Guo et al., 2019) utilizes spatial and temporal attention mechanisms with spatial-temporal convolution to learn the dynamic spatial-temporal correlations.

•

STSGCN (Song et al., 2020) utilizes localized spatial-temporal graph convolutional module to synchronously capture the complex localized spatial-temporal correlations directly.

•

STFGNN (Li and Zhu, 2021) captures local and global correlations simultaneously, by assembling a Gated dilated CNN module with spatial-temporal fusion graph module.

•

AGCRN (Bai et al., 2020) utilizes data adaptive graph generation module to infer the inter-dependencies among different traffic series.

•

STGODE (Fang et al., 2021) extracts longer-range spatial-temporal correlations using continuous representation of GNNs.

•

Z-GCNETs (Chen et al., 2021) utilizes time-aware zigzag persistence and zigzag topological layer for time-aware graph convolutional networks.

•

DSTAGNN (Lan et al., 2022) utilizes spatial-temporal attention module and gated convolution module to exploit dynamic spatial-temporal correlation.

Figure 5. Prediction curves of DSTAGNN and TGLRN on PeMS08 dataset.

Figure 6. Prediction curves of TGLRN and TGLRN w/o edge sampling on PeMS08 dataset.

Evaluation Protocol.
We adopt three commonly used evaluation metrics to evaluate the model performance: mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE).
All datasets are split with a ratio of 6:2:2 into training sets, validation sets, and test sets. We use one hour of historical data to predict the next 60 minutes data, that is, as datasets are aggregated into 5-minute windows, 12 time steps of historical data are used to predict future 12 time steps data.
Experiments are conducted on Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6226R CPU @ 2.90GHz and NVIDIA RTX A6000.

Implementation Details.
The detailed hyperparameter setting of TGLRN is as follows:
In Graph Construction Layer described in Section 4.3.2, the dimensionality of node embeddings (i.e., dd) is tuned in {4,8,16,32,64}\{4,8,16,32,64\} and the edge sampling parameter γ\gamma is tuned in {0.05,0.1,0.2,0.3}\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.3\}. As described in Section 4.3.4, we use the Structure Information Group for constructing graphs. Specifically, the Structure Information Group is tuned in {𝒮​𝒢5,𝒮​𝒢7,𝒮​𝒢10,𝒮​𝒢15}\{\mathcal{SG}_{5},\mathcal{SG}_{7},\mathcal{SG}_{10},\mathcal{SG}_{15}\}. We used two SPLs and TPLs in Spatio-Temporal Block, and the hidden dimension size is fixed to 64 (i.e., OPEND=D′=64)D=D^{\prime}=64). The number of Spatio-Temporal Blocks in the model is tuned in {3,4,5,6}\{3,4,5,6\}, the number of the diffusion steps (i.e., KK) is fixed to 2 in SPL, the temporal convolution kernel size (i.e., K​sKs) is fixed to 6 in TPL. Learning rate is fixed to 0.005, and dropout rate is tuned in {0.05,0.1,0.15,0.2}\{0.05,0.1,0.15,0.2\}.

5.3. Model Analysis

5.3.1. Component Analysis

To investigate the effect of each component of TGLRN, we conduct additional experiments on PeMS04 dataset with five variants of TGLRN described as follows:

•

w/o TPL: This model does not equip with TPL described in Section 4.4.2.

•

w/o SPL: This model does not equip with SPL described in Section 4.4.1.

•

geo-base: This model uses Structure
Information, denoted as 𝒮1\mathcal{S}^{1}, by applying Equation (12) instead of using Adaptive Structure Information, to consider only geographically connected nodes.

•

w/o sampling: This model does not adopt edge sampling strategy described in Section 4.3.2.

•

all-connect: This model uses a fully connected graph as the structure information ignoring the relative geographical distance between nodes.

We have the following observations in Figure 8.
1) SPL and TPL significantly affect the prediction performance, and they are essential model components. Notably, TPL contributes more to the performance improvement than SPL, which implies that when forecasting based on historical temporal data, modeling temporal dependencies is more important than modeling spatial dependence.
2) The inferior results of geo-base and all-connect demonstrate the importance of using Adaptive Structure Information to appropriately restrict the geographic distances between node pairs in the graphs.
3) The results of w/o sampling demonstrate that our proposed edge sampling strategy is crucial and contributes to improving the model performance.

5.3.2. Analysis on Structure Information

In Table 3, we have the following observations:
1)
The model performance degrades when constructing a graph using roads that are too distant (i.e., 𝒮30\mathcal{S}^{30} and 𝒮∞\mathcal{S}^{\infty}).
We argue that in the macroscopic view, as roads in geographically distant areas have different trends (Figure 2), considering too distant roads is not helpful for predicting the traffic flow of each road.
2)
The model performance degrades when constructing a graph using roads that are too close (i.e., 𝒮1\mathcal{S}^{1}), whereas the performance is optimal when applying our proposed Adaptive Structure Information (i.e., ℐ\mathcal{I}). These results underscore the effectiveness of our Adaptive Structure Information method by optimally selecting a search range for each node at every time step.
3) Considering dynamic spatial dependencies over time consistently outperforms the case when the model is trained solely based on a static graph (i.e., ℐ∗\mathcal{I}^{*}). We argue that in the microscopic view, as each time step inherently carries different spatial dependencies, considering spatial dependencies between roads that change over time is crucial.

5.3.4. Analysis on Gumbel-Softmax Trick

We conduct experiments to validate the benefit of training TGLRN based on important structure learned by the Gumbel-Softmax Trick. In Table 5, we observe that we achieve further improvements in the overall performance when using the Gumbel-Softmax trick rather than directly using sigmoid on edge weights. We attribute this to the fact that Gumbel-Softmax trick samples meaningful nodes by capturing the importance of edges. However, it is important to note that even without the Gumbel-Softmax trick, we still achieve the state-of-the-art performance.

5.3.5. Visualization of Hop Range Selection Using Adaptive Structure Information

In Section 4.3.4, we defined Adaptive Structure Information, enabling the model to learn suitable hop ranges. The rationale for this approach, as discussed in Section 3.1, is that each dataset has different criteria and methods for constructing adjacency matrices 𝒜s​p\mathcal{A}^{sp} based on spatial neighbor relationships. Additionally, the optimal hop range might vary for each node and each time step. Through Figure 9, we visualized utilization of each Structure Information 𝒮k\mathcal{S}^{k} within the Structure Information Group 𝒮​𝒢L={𝒮1,𝒮2,…,𝒮L}\mathcal{SG}_{L}=\{\mathcal{S}^{1},\mathcal{S}^{2},\ldots,\mathcal{S}^{L}\} for various datasets. These results demonstrate that the distribution of Structure Information utilization, selected via the Hop Range Selector, differs across datasets, indicating that the range of consideration varies for each node and time step for optimal performance. Notably, for dataset PeMS07, the high utilization of S15S^{15} was observed. In such cases, we conducted experiments to explore a broader range by setting the hyperparameter LL to values greater than 15, but these experiments were observed not to have a significant impact on performance improvement. This suggests that beyond a certain range, the additional Structure Information may not yield meaningful improvements, potentially leading to redundant information.

5.4. Efficiency Analysis

5.4.1. Training Time Comparison

In Table 6, we compare the training time spent per epoch for training TGLRN, DSTAGNN (Lan et al., 2022), and Z-GCNETs (Chen et al., 2021). We observed that TGLRN spent a moderate amount of time per epoch despite its advanced architecture design, which demonstrates that TGLRN is scalable.

5.4.2. Space Complexity Analysis

In Table 7, we compare the number of model parameters of TGLRN with two recent state-of-the-art methods, i.e., DSTAGNN (Lan et al., 2022) and Z-GCNETs (Chen et al., 2021). Specifically, TGLRN outperforms DSTAGNN even with less number of parameters. Moreover, although Z-GCNETs requires a relatively small number of model parameters, it is greatly outperformed by TGLRN.
Note that the Graph Construction Layer requires O⁡(T′​N2)O\left(T^{\prime}N^{2}\right) space complexity, which means TGLRN consumes more memory as the number of nodes NN increases.
This becomes a problem when sensors are expanded, or new sensors are installed in new areas. One way to solve this problem is dividing the area based on a geographical location and training separate models, which we leave as future work.

6. Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a novel neural network model, called Temporal Graph Learning Recurrent Neural Network (TGLRN), for traffic flow forecasting. TGLRN effectively generates and trains graphs with different semantic information according to time changes through the RNN structure (i.e., microscopic view).
In the process of constructing graphs, we train the model using Adaptive Structure Information to select an optimal range for connecting node pairs in the graphs, aiming to exclude unnecessary information during the training (i.e., macroscopic view). In addition, we devise an edge sampling strategy when constructing the graph at each time step to endow the model with robustness, which eventually leads to further improvements. Experiments on four real-world datasets demonstrate that TGLRN captures the complex spatio-temporal dependencies of road networks, and provides superior performance of traffic flow forecasting.
