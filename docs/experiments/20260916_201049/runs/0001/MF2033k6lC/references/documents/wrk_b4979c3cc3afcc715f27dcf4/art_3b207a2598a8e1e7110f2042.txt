Abstract

The generation of voluminous scientific data poses significant challenges for efficient storage, transfer, and analysis.
Recently, error-bounded lossy compression methods emerged due to their ability to achieve high compression ratios while controlling data distortion.
However, they often overlook the inherent spatial and temporal correlations within scientific data, thus missing opportunities for higher compression.
In this paper we propose GraphComp, a novel graph-based method for error-bounded lossy compression of scientific data.
We perform irregular segmentation of the original grid data and generate a graph representation that preserves the spatial and temporal correlations. Inspired by Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), we then propose a temporal graph autoencoder to learn latent representations that significantly reduce the size of the graph, effectively compressing the original data. Decompression reverses the process and utilizes the learnt graph model together with the latent representation to reconstruct an approximation of the original data. The decompressed data are guaranteed to satisfy a user-defined point-wise error bound.
We compare our method against the state-of-the-art error-bounded lossy methods (i.e., HPEZ, SZ3.1, SPERR, and ZFP) on large-scale real and synthetic data.
GraphComp consistently achieves the highest compression ratio across most datasets, outperforming the second-best method by margins ranging from 22% to 50%.

Index Terms: Scientific Data Compression, Meta Timestamps, Temporal Graph Autoencoder, Error Bounds

I Introduction

Modern sensing and simulation technologies generate large volumes of scientific data, in the order of hundreds of terabytes to petabytes, across different domains such as weather and climate [1, 2, 3], geopotential simulations [4], and seismic wave analysis [5].
The large data volume poses significant challenges on storage, network and computational infrastructures [6].
To address these challenges, advanced data reduction techniques for the compression of large scientific data become essential for efficient storage, transfer, and analysis.

Scientific data libraries (e.g., NetCDF [7] and HDF511
            1

          http://www.hdfgroup.org/HDF5) implement traditional lossless compression techniques, such as Zlib22
            2

          http://www.zlib.net/ and Zstandard33
            3

          https://github.com/facebook/zstd/, that guarantee data fidelity, but only achieve low compression ratios, rarely exceeding 2×\times [8].
At the other end of the spectrum, there exist error-unbounded lossy compression methods [9, 10, 11] that promise impressive compression ratios but may introduce large errors, thus compromising the accuracy and utility of the decompressed data.

The consensus of the scientific community is to utilize error-bounded lossy compression to achieve better compression ratios than lossless methods, while ensuring some error metric appropriate for the particular application domain (e.g., relative point-wise error) to be within a user-defined bound ϵ\epsilon.
Several error-bounded lossy compressors have demonstrated good performance in handling scientific data44
            4

          There are many types of scientific data, including numerical, categorical, text, and image data; this paper specifically focuses on numerical data., including HPEZ [12], SZ3.1 [13], SPERR [14], and ZFP [15].
Meanwhile, recent developments in deep neural networks resulted in NN-based approaches, such as autoencoder-based SZ [16], coordinate network-based compressors [17], and super-resolution network-based SZ [18].

Fig. 2: Overview of GraphComp, which includes three primary steps: \raisebox{-.9pt}{1}⃝ graph initialization, \raisebox{-.9pt}{2}⃝ graph representation learning, and \raisebox{-.9pt}{3}⃝ grid reconstruction. To guarantee a point-wise error bound, we further integrate an error-bounded module.

Challenges.
Despite advancements in error-bounded lossy compression, significant challenges remain.
Existing methods often fail to adequately consider the spatial correlations within scientific data.
For instance, regional temperature data, as depicted in the example of Figure 1(a), exhibit complex spatial patterns where nearby elements frequently express similar attributes.
Compression methods, such as HPEZ [12] and SZ3.1 [13], may fail to accurately capture such irregular relationships, thus compromising the potential for optimal spatial correlation exploitation for compression.

Moreover, the temporal dynamics inherent to scientific datasets are often overlooked.
Temporal correlations, as illustrated in consecutive timestamps of the example in Figure 1(b), require explicit modeling to ensure the dependencies are preserved during compression.
Existing methods such as SPERR [14] and ZFP [15], may introduce distortions to such temporal patterns, effectively lowering the compression ratio.

Our solution.
We propose GraphComp, a novel graph-based error-bounded lossy compression method for scientific data, that leverages temporal graph autoencoders.
Unlike regular grid structures, which may fail to capture complex nonlinear correlations, graphs excel in representing irregular spatial relationships.
Temporal graphs enhance this capability by capturing temporal dynamics, enabling GraphComp to effectively compress complex scientific data that exhibit both spatial and temporal correlations.

An overview of GraphComp is presented in Figure 2.
Intuitively, scientific data consist of regions that contain roughly homogeneous values but may be of irregular shape. GraphComp employs the Felzenszwalb [19] algorithm, a well-known method for image segmentation, to partition each timestamp of the original data.
The partitioning is then transform into a graph, where each region corresponds to a node and there is an edge between nodes if the corresponding regions are adjacent. The graph is augmented with aggregate information about the regions.
The resulting series of graphs for all timestamps, depicted in Figure 2\raisebox{-.9pt}{1}⃝, reliably preserve both spatial and temporal correlations across the data.

The graph series forms the input to our proposed temporal graph autoencoder (named T-AutoG) for learning latent representations.
T-AutoG utilizes graph convolutional layers to learn the spatial information from graph data, transforming them into graph embeddings.
Subsequently, the embeddings are processed by convolutional layers to capture temporal information, producing latent representations that capture complex node interactions and their temporal dynamics, as shown in Figure 2\raisebox{-.9pt}{2}⃝.

The latent representations together with the learnt graph models are used in a reversed process to reconstruct the grid data, shown in Figure 2\raisebox{-.9pt}{3}⃝. Obviously, the reconstructed data are an approximation of the original data. To guarantee that the point-wise relative error is within a user-defined bound ϵ\epsilon, we compute and store the residual errors in a compact form. This information is used during decompression to correct any erroneously reconstructed data. We utilize a combination of error-controlled quantization, Huffman encoding, and lossless compression of the quantized data, following existing approaches [12, 13]. Consequently, GraphComp transforms the original data to a compressed version consisting of the following components: (i) a compressed form of the segmentation; (ii) the weights of T-AutoG; (iii) a compressed form of the latent representations; and (iv) a compressed form of the residual errors. The total size (in bytes) of the compressed version is much smaller than the size of the original data.

We conduct comprehensive experiments on both real (including the private RedSeareanalysis dataset [1, 3], and public Era5 data [4]), as well as synthetic data.
The results show that the compression ratios of our GraphComp method are, in most cases, higher than the compared state-of-the-art methods (i.e., HPEZ, SZ3.1, SPERR, and ZFP), under all tested error bounds.
Compared to the second-best method, GraphComp improves compression ratios from 22% up to 50%.

Note that the spatial resolution of the RSRA dataset is higher [1, 3] than that of the publicly available ones (e.g., ERA5); the finer resolution puts additional stress on the compressors, revealing their inefficiencies. Since RSRA is private, for reproducibility and to serve the scientific community, we develop a synthetic data generator based on generative adversarial networks and trained on the RSRA data. We show that the behaviour of the compressors on the synthetic data is similar to that on the real data. Both the data generator and the GraphComp code are available as open-source.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose GraphComp, a graph-based error-bounded lossy compression method that results in high compression ratios of scientific data while preserving data integrity.

•

We develop T-AutoG, a temporal graph autoencoder designed to capture both spatial and temporal information from the transformed graph data, facilitating latent representation learning and grid data reconstruction.

•

We train a generative adversarial model to generate synthetic data that preserve the statistical properties of the proprietary RSRA data, providing a valuable resource for the community.

•

We conduct extensive experiments on real and synthetic datasets, to demonstrate the superiority of GraphComp against state-of-the-art compression techniques.

III Related Work

Scientific data compression.
Recent advances in scientific simulation and sensing led to the generation of voluminous data across diverse fields such as weather and climate [2, 3, 4], seismic wave analysis [5], and turbulence studies66
            6

          https://wci.llnl.gov/simulation/computer-codes/miranda.
The proliferation of data poses significant challenges in terms of efficient storage and transfer.
While lossless compression techniques are prevalent in database management systems [20, 21, 22], their application to scientific data is often limited by modest compression ratios77
            7

          For example, Gorilla [22], an in-memory database with lossless compression, reports up to 10×10\times compression ratio for time series. However, on the Redsea data the compression ratio is less than 2×2\times., typically less than 2×2\times [8].
In response to the limitation, there has been a surge to explore lossy compression methods.
The primary challenge lies in achieving maximal compression while bounding the error, thereby ensuring the integrity of data decompression for subsequent analysis.

Error-bounded lossy compressors.
We focus on error-bounded lossy compressors, where the quality of decompressed data remains within specified error tolerances.
ZFP [15] offers a fixed-bitrate mode through the truncation of blocks of orthogonally transformed data.
SPERR [14] is a lossy compressor for structured scientific data, incorporating the advanced wavelet compression algorithm SPECK [23, 24].
SZ-based methods, including SZ2+ [25], SZ3 [26], SZauto [27], and HPEZ [12], are among the most popular error-bounded lossy compressors.
They typically involve four main steps: data prediction, error quantization, Huffman encoding, and lossless residual error compression.
SZ-based methods have evolved in their approach to prediction, ranging from simple one-dimensional adaptive curve-fitting techniques such as previous-value, linear-curve, and quadratic-curve fitting [28], to more complex multidimensional first-order and second-order Lorenzo predictors [25], and dynamic multidimensional spline interpolation, including linear and cubic splines [13].
HPEZ [12] introduces novel interpolation strategies (e.g., multidimensional interpolation, and natural cubic splines) along with auto-tuning driven by quality metrics.

Neural networks for scientific data compression.
Recently, the proliferation of deep neural network techniques has spurred the development of deep learning-based methods for scientific data compression.
There are two main categories:
(i) The first one includes lossy methods without error bounds.
For example, Hayne et al. [29] evaluate compression performance on 2D floating-point scientific data using an existing autoencoder [30] designed for natural image compression.
Huang et al. [11] develop a coordinate-based neural network for compressing weather and climate data. Such methods report high compression ratios but the accuracy of the decompressed data may be arbitrarily low.
(ii) The second category comprises error-bounded lossy compression methods.
This includes AE-SZ  [16], which incorporates a Slice-Wasserstein autoencoder network, and SRN-SZ [18], which employs super-resolution networks to enhance compression performance.

Remote sensing compression.
The remote sensing community deals with scientific data, mostly in the form of images [31].
Neural network-based methods for remote sensing image compression, such as COSMIC [32] and HL-RSCompNet [33], are optimized for visual presentation where minor inaccuracies in pixel values are acceptable. However, they may fail to preserve the numerical precision
required for many scientific applications; furthermore, they do not achieve competitive compression ratios88
            8

          For comparison, HL-RSCompNet [33] on the RedSea500 dataset yields compression ratio ρ=45.51\rho=45.51 without error guarantee, whereas error-bounded methods such as SPERR [14] and SZ3.1 [13] achieve ρ=69.23\rho=69.23 and ρ=77.87\rho=77.87, respectively, under error bound ϵ=10−2\epsilon=10^{-2}..

IV Graph-based compression

In this section, we propose GraphComp, a novel graph-based error-bounded lossy compression method for scientific data.
GraphComp comprises three main steps (see Figure 2):
(i) data segmentation and graph initialization, which partitions each timestamp into irregular regions and maps the partitioning to a graph (Section IV-A);
(ii) graph representation learning, which trains T-AutoG, a temporal graph autoencoder to generate latent representations of the graph (Section IV-B); and
(iii) grid reconstruction, which uses the trained model and the latent representations to reconstruct an approximation of the original data (Section IV-C).
Similarly to existing work [12, 13], GraphComp is augmented by (iv) an error-bounded module, where the residual error is quantized, encoded by the Huffman algorithm and compressed losslessly (Section IV-C).
This module is necessary to guarantee that the point-wise relative error of the decompressed data is bounded by a user-defined threshold ϵ\epsilon.
Below, we explain these steps in detail.

IV-A Data segmentation and graph initialization

The input to our compressor is a temporal scientific dataset
X∈ℝT×M×NX\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times M\times N}. Since the data correspond to some physical process, intuitively in each timestamp there exist regions pip_{i} that are relatively smooth. For each such region, we can replace the values of all points in pip_{i} with some aggregate value, e.g., the mean pi¯\overline{p_{i}}, essentially compressing the original data. With high probability, the relative difference between the original value of each data point and pi¯\overline{p_{i}} will be bounded by ϵ\epsilon. Data points not satisfying this condition, will be handled later by the error-bounded module (Section IV-C).

The following questions arise:
(i) how to identify the smooth regions, which are expected to have irregular shapes;
(ii) how to represent the relationships among those regions; and
(iii) how to efficiently store the spatial extents of all regions.

Data segmentation.
To answer the first question, observe that the problem is a combination of data and space partitioning; hence, it does not map well to traditional clustering, such as kk-means [34], DBSCAN [35], or hierarchical clustering [36].
Instead, the problem resembles image segmentation. Since the subsequent steps of our method require spatially connected regions, we cannot employ NN-based segmentation [37], such as Segnet [38], or U-Net [39, 40], because they may generate disconnected regions. Instead, we focus on segmentation methods such as Quickshift [41], Compact Watershed [42] and the Felzenszwalb algorithm [19]. Based on our ablation study (Section V-E), we employ the latter for the rest of this paper.

The Felzenszwalb algorithm [19] is a graph-based approach that treats each data point as a node with edges representing value similarities.
The algorithm efficiently handles spatial correlations and accurately identifies natural boundaries using a minimum spanning tree. The resulting partitioning is a set of regions with irregular spatial extents; regions do not overlap and their union covers the entire space.

Meta-timestamp selection.
Since data changes at each timestamp, the resulting segmentation also varies. Storing the spatial extents of the segmentation regions for every timestamp would require a lot of space which negates any compression achieved by value aggregation. Thus, we refrain from segmenting all timestamps. Instead, we carefully select a minimal yet effective set of representative timestamps (that we call meta-timestamps) that optimize compression performance by maintaining a balance between segmentation accuracy and storage efficiency. Intuitively, an effective meta-timestamp should approximate the data distribution while minimizing temporal variations of the timestamps that it represents.

Our meta-timestamps selection is illustrated in Algorithm 1. Interestingly, to determine the selection of meta-timestamps, we do not use the computationally expensive compression ratio (that requires the execution of the compression algorithm). We propose a more efficient evaluation that incorporates the Mean Absolute Difference (MAbsD), defined as the mean absolute difference between the values of meta-timestamp and the mean of the group of timestamps it represents. MAbsD can be efficiently computed, significantly reducing the optimization cost.

Graph-based representation.
After selecting the meta-timestamps, we apply a segmentation algorithm (e.g., Felzenszwalb) to generate segmentation schemes for these timestamps. These segmentations are then propagated to neighboring timestamps, ensuring a limited number of segmentation schemes across the dataset.
Each timestamp is then represented as a graph GG, where nodes correspond to segmented regions, edges connect adjacent regions, and each node is assigned the mean value of its region as its feature.

The mapping of the original grid data into graphs allows for the explicit representation of irregular spatial relationships. In the next section we will use it to train a temporal graph autoencoder.

IV-B Graph representation learning

In this section, we propose a temporal graph autoencoder, called T-AutoG, to encode the graph data into latent representations.
These are compact representations of the graph data and significantly reduce the storage requirements, while preserving essential spatial and temporal information.
For instance, temperature data may exhibit varying patterns depending on time and location. T-AutoG dynamically adapts to these variations, extracting compact latent representations that preserve critical global relationships. This adaptability ensures both high compression ratios and accurate data reconstruction, making it indispensable for downstream tasks like error-bounded compression.

T-AutoG utilizes graph convolution layers to capture spatial information followed by convolution layers to capture temporal information.
The latent representations are subsequently decoded back into graph data.
The architecture of our graph autoencoder is depicted in Figure 3.

Fig. 3: Overview of our temporal graph autoencoder T-AutoG for latent representation, which learns spatial and temporal patterns; the decoder is omitted.

Spatial information.
Building on recent advancements in graph convolution networks (GCN) [43], we utilize graph convolution layers to learn the graph embedding. Inspired by previous work in this field [44], our approach leverages the following layer-wise propagation rule in a GCN:
for each timestamp tt, the propagation rule for the ll-th layer uses adjacency matrix At∈{0,1}|V|×|V|A_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{|V|\times|V|} and graph embedding Htl∈ℝ|V|×dH^{l}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|\times d} as input, to update the graph embedding to Htl+1H^{l+1}_{t}. Formally:

Htl+1\displaystyle H^{l+1}_{t}
=ϕ⁡(A~t​Htl​Wl)\displaystyle=\phi\left(\tilde{A}_{t}H^{l}_{t}W^{l}\right)

(4)

where ϕ\phi is a non-linear activation function and Wl∈ℝd×dW^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} denotes the shared weights at layer ll for each timestamp.
A~t\tilde{A}_{t} is defined as follows (for simplicity, the timestamp index tt may be omitted):

A~=D~−12​(A+I)​D~−12,D~=diag​(∑jA~i​j)\tilde{A}=\tilde{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(A+I)\tilde{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}},\quad\tilde{D}=\text{diag}\left(\sum\nolimits_{j}\tilde{A}_{ij}\right)

(5)

where D~\tilde{D} is the degree matrix of A~\tilde{A}.
The initial graph embedding Ht0H^{0}_{t} is derived from the graph feature FtF_{t}, as follows:

Ht0=FtH^{0}_{t}=F_{t}

(6)

The final graph embedding HtLH^{L}_{t} is obtained through iterative application of graph convolution operations,
where LL denotes the final layer.

Our graph convolution enables effective propagation and transformation of node features across the graph by utilizing a normalized Laplacian to achieve smooth feature aggregation from adjacent nodes.
Consequently, T-AutoG captures both local and global structures within the graph, enhancing the fidelity of the transformed features.
The graph convolution layers learn spatial information for all timestamps, each corresponding to a graph, using the shared weight matrix WW.
The consistent structure of all graphs, resulting from the unified segmentation in graph initialization, ensures the consistent application of the learned spatial information.

The decoder transforms the encoded graph embedding HtLH^{L}_{t} back into an adjacency matrix through pairwise inner product calculations: At^=HtL​(HtL)′\hat{A_{t}}=H^{L}_{t}\left(H^{L}_{t}\right)^{\prime}, where (HtL)′\left(H^{L}_{t}\right)^{\prime} denotes the transpose of the graph embedding matrix.
The decoder receives the graph embedding as input and outputs a symmetric matrix, where each entry represents the inner product between corresponding embedding.
This transformation is crucial for accurately reconstructing graph data from their latent representations.
Due to the effectiveness and simplicity of inner product calculations, it is extensively utilized in tasks such as graph reconstruction [45, 46] and is particularly advantageous for applications that require relational data representations within graph network architectures [47].

Temporal information.
After obtaining graph embeddings 𝒢L\mathcal{G}^{L}, we learn the latent representation through the temporal dimension TT to further reduce the size of graph embedding, as illustrated in Figure 3.
Formally, the graph embeddings are expressed as:

𝒢L={H0L,⋯,HtL,⋯,HT−1L}\mathcal{G}^{L}=\left\{H^{L}_{0},\cdots,H^{L}_{t},\cdots,H^{L}_{T-1}\right\}

(7)

where 𝒢TL∈ℝT×|V|×d\mathcal{G}^{L}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times|V|\times d}.

To achieve data compression while retaining sufficient information for accurate reconstruction, we use convolutional layers to learn latent representations from the graph embeddings.
First, the convolution layers focus on temporal interactions, learning the complex relationships between nodes and their evolution over time.
Second, these layers extract key features across temporal dimensions, effectively reducing the size of the graph embedding.
The simplified formulation is defined as follows:

𝒢^T−1L=g⁡(f⁡(𝒢T−1L))\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{L}_{T-1}=g\left(f\left(\mathcal{G}^{L}_{T-1}\right)\right)

(8)

where f⁡(⋅)f(\cdot) denotes the encoder and g⁡(⋅)g(\cdot) represents the decoder.
Given the straightforward nature of the convolution process, we omit the pseudocode for brevity.

To enhance the compactness and spatial efficiency of latent representations, we implement L2 regularization.

IV-C Decompression

After decoding the compressed representation, GraphComp reconstructs the grid data by mapping decoded graph features back to their respective spatial regions using segmentations from meta-timestamps.

Grid reconstruction.
GraphComp uses T-AutoG to decode the latent representation (see Figure 4-\raisebox{-.9pt}{1}⃝).
The mean value obtained from each decoded graph node is the representative temperature for the corresponding region.
Specifically, we assign the feature of each node to all the grid points within its corresponding region (as shown in Figure 4-\raisebox{-.9pt}{2}⃝). This ensures that the reconstructed grid data accurately reflects the spatial temperature variations captured by the graph structure.

Error-bounded module.
In the reconstructed grid data, some values may not satisfy the relative point-wise error bound ϵ\epsilon. Similarly to existing research, we store the point-wise residual error in compressed form and use this information to correct the erroneous data points.
Recall Equation 2: first, linear-scale quantization converts the relative difference |xi′−xi|vrange⁡(X)\frac{|x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i}|}{\mathrm{vrange}(X)} between each predicted xi′∈X′x^{\prime}_{i}\in X^{\prime} and original value xi∈Xx_{i}\in X into integers that are multiples of ϵ\epsilon.
Then, a Huffman encoder followed by Zstandard lossless compressor, store the quantized errors in compressed form.
The process is implemented in the error-bounded module (see Figure 2).
For technical details, interested readers should refer to [25, 48].

Compression optimization.
To further improve the compression ratio, we compress the segmentation, the weights of T-AutoG, and the latent representation using Zstandard lossless compressor; thereby, reducing their sizes by 2×2\times and up to 5×5\times.

V-B Overall compression ratio

Table III presents the compression ratio ρ\rho of all real large datasets, for three relative error bounds, ϵ=10−2,10−3,10−4\epsilon=10^{-2},10^{-3},10^{-4}. It is clear that GraphComp outperforms its competitors in most tested cases, achieving a 22% to 50% improvement. For example, for ϵ=10−2\epsilon=10^{-2} GraphComp compresses RedSea96K (i.e., temperature data) by 141.75×141.75\times, meaning that the original size of 373.1GB is reduced to 2.63GB. For the same settings, the next best method, SZ3.1, achieves ρ=105.21\rho=105.21; therefore, GraphComp is 35% better.

As expected, for tighter error bounds (e.g., ϵ=10−4\epsilon=10^{-4}) the compression ratio of all methods decreases. When the error bound is tighter, the probability of erroneous predictions increases for all methods. Consequently, the corresponding error-bounding modules must store additional information to correct the residual error, thus decreasing the final compression ratio.

Observe that different variables in the same dataset behave differently. For example, RedSea96K represents atmospheric temperatures, whereas RedSea96K-Z is the zonal wind speed (i.e., the projection of wind speed vector to the xx-axis). For the former, all methods achieve better compression because temperature data are smoother and easier to predict.
Also note that all Era5 data are more compressible than the RedSea data since the spatial resolution of Era5 is lower, meaning that each data point corresponds to an aggregation of measurements of a larger region on the Earth’s surface; therefore, the distribution of values tends to be smoother.

Fig. 6: Compression ratio of the RedSea96K dataset using a different number of timestamps (ϵ=10−2\epsilon=10^{-2}).

GraphComp outperforms all methods for all dataset with a large number of timestamps (RedSea and Era5). For the smaller-sized datasets (Miranda, Scale and Cesm) than contain less than 500 timestamps the performance of GraphComp is not the best. This can be attributed to the fact that a limited number of timestamps restrict GraphComp ability to exploit temporal correlations, thus, offering a lower compression ratio. To verify our claim, we use the RedSea dataset and compute the compression ratio ρ\rho for different numbers of timestamps ranging from 50 to 96K. The results of Figure 6 show that GraphComp achieves substantial improvements in ρ\rho with increasing number of timestamps, while methods like HPEZ and SZ3.1 show only minimal gains. These results demonstrate GraphComp’s ability to scale and effectively utilize temporal correlations for compression. Additionally, these results suggest that increasing the number of timestamps of smaller sized datasets such as Miranda, Scale, and Cesm  would similarly enhance GraphComp’s performance, potentially outperforming other methods.

Size of compressed data components.
GraphComp stores the compressed data as four separate components: (i) segmentation, that stores the spatial extents of the regions generated by the the Felzenszwalb algorithm for virtual timestamp XμX_{\mu};
(ii) model weights for autoencoder T-AutoG;
(iii) latent representation of the graphs in set 𝒢\mathcal{G}; and
(iv) compressed residual error information, which is used by the error-bounded module to correct errors.

V-F WGAN: Synthetic temperature data

In this section, we introduce a generative adversarial network, specifically a Wasserstein GAN (WGAN) [49], to generate temperature data that reflects the characteristics of our proprietary RedSea dataset [1, 3].

Although there exist many similar public real datasets (like the Era5 [4] family), they typically lack two characteristics: (i) the number of timestamps per region is limited; and (ii) their spatial resolution is coarse. Contrary RedSea consists of more than 96K timestamps and represents the data at a fine spatial resolution. Consequently, compression is more challenging and stresses the limits of the tested methods. For example, recall from Table III that the highest compression ratio achieved by any method for RedSea is ρ≃134\rho\simeq 134, whereas for Era5 the best ratio reaches ρ≃1,073\rho\simeq 1,073.

Given the proprietary nature of our RedSea data, direct sharing is infeasible. However, by using our WGAN-based generator, we can provide synthesized data that maintains the statistical characteristics of the original dataset. Moreover, the WGAN model can generate an expanded volume of synthetic data, which is vital for testing the efficacy of our compression method on more extensive datasets. For these reasons, we believe our WGAN generator is an important contribution both for reproducibility and as a service to the academic community.

To ensure the utility of WGAN-generated data in compression studies, we adopt a rigorous training process. This includes the implementation of a gradient penalty method to stabilize training and optimizing both the generator and critic [49] components. We then fine-tune the WGAN model to ensure that the generated data closely resembles the real data. We configure the batch size to 3232, the number of epoch to 10410^{4}, and the learning rate to 8⋅10−58\cdot 10^{-5}. When generating synthetic data, the number of timestamps can be adjusted to accommodate the full dataset, or to generate a smaller subset that fits within the GPU memory constraints.

To validate the similarities and differences between real and WGAN-generated data, we employ a comprehensive set of evaluation strategies, including visual comparisons, statistical properties comparison to quantify the correspondence between datasets, and benchmark performance testing using all aforementioned compression methods. The evaluation methods collectively provide a robust assessment of the relationship between the real and WGAN-generated datasets.

VI Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a novel graph-based error-bounded lossy compression method for scientific data, called GraphComp.
At each timestamp, we convert the original grid data into graphs using several segmentation schemes inspired by image processing.
Then, we propose T-AutoG, a temporal graph autoencoder, to learn the latent representations of the graph data, effectively reducing the size of the original data.
The decoder of T-AutoG along with the segmentation are utilized to transform the latent representation back into grid data. The maximum point-wise relative error of the decompressed data is guaranteed to be bounded by a user-defined error bound.
Due to the confidentiality of our RedSea data, we train WGAN a adversarial generative model to generate and open-source synthetic versions of the dataset.
Our experiments show that GraphComp achieves superior compression ratios: for a relative error bound of 10−210^{-2}, it compresses the temperatures in the RedSea data from 373.1GB down to 2.78GB. Moreover GraphComp consistently outperforms the SOTA methods, SPERR, ZFP, SZ3.1, and HPEZ, for most tested cases, by a margin of 22% up to 50%.
