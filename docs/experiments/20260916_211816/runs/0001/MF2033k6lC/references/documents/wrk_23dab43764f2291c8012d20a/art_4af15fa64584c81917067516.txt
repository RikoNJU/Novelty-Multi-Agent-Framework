Learning Implicit Causal World Models from Multi-Agent Demonstrations

Jasorsi Ghosh

Affiliation: Department of Computer Science

Affiliation: Purdue University

Email: ghosh117@purdue.edu

Abstract

In model-based reinforcement learning, world models exist as internal simulators, but their training often conflates statistical correlations with causal mechanisms. This problem is exacerbated in multi-agent systems where physical transitions are intertwined with strategic agent intents, causing world models to fail under distribution shift. We introduce Implicit Causal World Models to recover environmental dynamics from offline demonstrations without requiring pre-defined causal graphs. By incorporating policy variance, we render world models discoverable via the sequential backdoor condition. Evaluations across coordination tasks (Two-Door, Navigation, and Giveway) demonstrate that these models provide interpretable causal representations under both full and partial observability, with model accuracy scaling directly with interventional strength.

1 Introduction

In the evolution of model-based reinforcement learning (Moerland et al., 2022), world models have progressed from early predictive systems (Sutton, 1991) to sophisticated generative architectures (Hafner et al., 2024; Hafner et al., 2019; LeCun and others, 2022) that utilize latent state-space models to simulate future trajectories. We frame these world models as an agent’s ”internal simulators,” representing its belief about the environment. While world models improve sample efficiency (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018b) in controlled settings , they remain fundamentally limited by their reliance on statistical correlations rather than causal underlying mechanisms (Kipf et al., 2020). In high-stakes environments, this leads to the spurious correlation problem, where models incorrectly attribute state transitions to non-causal features present during expert demonstrations.

seen. The region below the dashed line is unseen in training. Full details in Appendix Z

This challenge is compounded in multi-agent systems, where environmental physics are intertwined (Lowe et al., 2020; Dasgupta et al., 2019) with the strategic, often confounded, intents of other agents. For example, an autonomous vehicle model might incorrectly learn to accelerate because an adjacent car moves, rather than because the traffic light turned green. In this scenario, the physics of the green light correlates perfectly with another agent’s intent to move, leading to causal confusion.

Causal World Models (Ahmed et al., 2020; Nam et al., 2026) redefine internal simulators by replacing black-box transition functions with a structured network of causal links representing the environment’s governing mechanisms. While prior work (Richens and Everitt, 2024) highlights the theoretical robustness of agents that learn causal models, this structural approach explicitly resolves distorted dynamics (Xia et al., 2022) introduced by selection bias or unobserved confounders in offline data. By utilizing structured world models , this grounding in environmental physics ensures that transition dynamics remain invariant (Peters et al., 2015), even under distributional shifts (Deng et al., 2025; Arjovsky et al., 2020) in the observational space.

We propose Implicit Causal World Models (ICWM), which leverage neural architectures to approximate structural causal models (Schölkopf et al., 2021; Pawlowski et al., 2020) without requiring the hand-engineered directed acyclic graphs often rendered intractable by the combinatorial complexity of multi-agent systems. Discovering true enviornment dynamics from policy rollouts requires overcoming temporal dependencies that violate the independent and identically distributed assumptions central to standard causal discovery (Peters et al., 2017). We address this by framing sequential trajectories as a vector autoregressive process (Runge et al., 2019) and utilizing the PCMCI+ algorithm (Runge, 2022) to account for both time-lagged and contemporaneous dependencies.

Adhering to the Pearl Causal Hierarchy (Pearl, 2019), achieving the identifiability of a true causal world model requires Layer 2 (Intervention) understanding. However, active hard interventions in multi-agent systems are often computationally or prohibitively costly (Kallus and Zhou, 2020). We propose bypassing this constraint by collecting demonstrations with varying degrees of stochastic interventional strength applied exclusively to agent actions via the expert’s policy. This method, parallels the implications of soft interventions described in the literature (Correa and Bareinboim, 2020), allows the Causal World Model to discover the true causal parents of environment dynamics from the spurious confounders present in the demonstrations.

In the framework of Causal Imitation Learning (Kumor et al., 2022), agent actions in multi-agent systems are treated as sequential interventions upon the environment by the expert policy. While behavioral cloning (Pomerleau, 1988) is the standard approach for imitating expert policies, it fails to discover the underlying environmental physics when demonstrations are influenced by unobserved confounders (de Haan et al., 2019). For the learned world model to be identifiable, the sequential backdoor condition (Zhang and Bareinboim, 2016) must be satisfied. This requires that the observed state history effectively d-separates agent actions from future states along all non-causal backdoor paths. We present a motivation study on the board game Tic-Tac-Toe in Appendix G.

In this paper (Figure 2), we address the limitations of model-based reinforcement learning in multi-agent settings by introducing a framework for discovering causal mechanisms from biased offline data. Our contributions as follows:

1.

Stochastic Interventional Data Collection (C1): We introduce a demonstration collection strategy that applies soft interventions through policy variance, satisfying the Pearl Causal Hierarchy requirements for identifiability without costly hard interventions.

2.

Multi-Agent Sequential Backdoor Condition (C2): We formalize and prove the Multi-Agent Sequential Backdoor Condition, establishing that interventional dynamics are recoverable from observational multi-agent data despite unobserved strategic confounding, and use it to decouple environmental physics from agent intent.

3.

Interventional Strength Recovers Causal Structure (C3): Across three coordination domains (Two-Door (Fickinger, 2020), Navigation (Bettini et al., 2022), and Giveway (Bettini et al., 2022)), under both full and partial observability and scaling to 2/3/42/3/4 agents, we show that discovery error falls monotonically as the interventional strength σ\sigma grows.

4.

Generalization Error Bound (C4): We establish, and confirm empirically, that the out-of-distribution policy evaluation error of the learned world model scales inversely with the interventional strength, falling by up to two orders of magnitude under adversarial policies.

5.

Intervention, Not Graph Quality, Is the Lever (C5): Pooling 1,2401{,}240 trained models, we show that the association between recovered-graph quality and predictive accuracy is a common-cause artefact of σ\sigma: conditioning on σ\sigma removes it, and a graph-free model matches the masked model at matched data. The causal mask earns its keep specifically under partial observation and layout extrapolation, and as a structural prior whose corruption is worse than no prior at all.

6.

Calibrated Out-of-Distribution Uncertainty (C6): We show that the learned models localize their predictive uncertainty to a minority of state dimensions under distribution shift, rather than degrading uniformly.

Impact of this work (Figure 6) seen as advances Model-Based Reinforcement Learning toward out-of-distribution robustness and structural interpretability, ensuring autonomous systems remain reliable even when surrounding agent behaviors deviate from training distributions. This framework facilitates verifiable safety, fault attribution, agent alignment, and making precise credit assignment to agent actions feasible.

2 Related Work

In our literature review, we identify practical bottlenecks in related problems and describe the gaps.

Limitations of Associative World Models: Model-Based Reinforcement Learning often relies on associative world models (DreamerV3, PlaNet, MuZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2020)) that predict future states by matching statistical distributions. Operating at Layer 1 of the Pearl Causal Hierarchy, these models are prone to causal confusion (de Haan et al., 2019), failing to distinguish between functional environment transitions and spurious correlations.

In multi-agent systems world models (Zhu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2025a), this manifest as an objective mismatch where models conflate invariant physical laws with the strategic intents of other agents. When dynamics are ”intertwined,” the world model develops a policy-dependent bias; consequently, shifting from expert to exploratory or adversarial policies causes catastrophic failure because the model expects environment physics to follow the expert’s specific behavior. We address this by treating agent intent as a formal confounder UU, adopting an interventional framework (Schölkopf et al., 2021) to ensure out-of-distribution robustness.

FOCUS (Zhu et al., 2022) prunes transitions using Kernel-based Conditional Independence tests (Zhang et al., 2012). These methods assume causal sufficiency. Multi-agent systems violate this assumption. Unobserved agent intents act as persistent confounders. Observational discovery yields fully-connected graphs in dense settings. We address this by treating intent as a formal confounder UU. We use an interventional framework (Schölkopf et al., 2021).

Scalability of Neural Causal Architectures: GNNs (Kipf et al., 2020; Scarselli et al., 2009), C-VAEs (Yang et al., 2023), and Neural SCMs (Pawlowski et al., 2020) approximate causal structures. Their message-passing requirements scale as O⁡(N2)O(N^{2}). This requirement makes them computationally prohibitive for dense multi-agent dynamics. Furthermore, true counterfactual reasoning remains intractable (Schölkopf et al., 2021).

Temporal Gaps in Causal Discovery:
Causal discovery is divided into constraint-based (Spirtes et al., 2000) methods, which use statistical independence tests, and score-based (Chickering, 2002) methods, which maximize a fit criterion (Verma and Pearl, 1990). Both paradigms are complicated by agent demonstrations that violate i.i.d. assumptions. While PCMCI+ (Runge, 2022) addresses autocorrelated and contemporaneous dependencies in time series, it has not been integrated into reinforcement learning transition models. We bridge this gap by using these techniques to inform the structural constraints of our world model.

The Intervention-Observation Paradox:
The reliance on costly hard interventions bottlenecks causal discovery (Eberhardt, 2008) and causal reinforcement learning algorithms (Zhang and Bareinboim, 2016). We bypass this by demonstrating that multi-agent policy variance provides sufficient interventional strength for Layer 2 identifiability. This parallels Causal Imitation Learning (CIL) theory, where the sequential backdoor condition (Zhang and Bareinboim, 2016) (SBC) is used to identify robust policies. We shift this focus, applying the SBC directly to the world model to ensure transition dynamics are identifiable even when agent intents act as unobserved confounders.

Comparison to Existing MAS Models:
Current multi-agent system frameworks (Ahmed et al., 2020; Madumal et al., 2019) often focus on emergent representations or communication protocols. Our framework differentiates itself by explicitly discovering environmental physics from agent strategy. Models that treat transitions as deterministic finite automata (Hopcroft et al., 2001) (DFA) to learn the system transition (Li et al., 2024; Vafa et al., 2024; Vafa et al., 2025b), and our work overlaps with these approaches in how structural constraints are integrated and learned alongside a generative model
Generative

Generative Video World Models: High-fidelity video models use diffusion and transformer processes to synthesize visually coherent states (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018a; Bruce et al., 2024). By prioritizing visual plausibility over causal grounding, these architectures are prone to physical hallucinations under policy shifts (Li et al., 2024; Tragoudaras et al., 2026). Over pixel space based world modeling (Assran et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2025), our framework instead prioritizes the discovery of invariant structural mechanisms to ensure robustness against spurious correlations inherent in training demonstrations.

3.3 Stochastic Soft Interventions

Hard interventions, such as d​o​(𝐚t=𝐚^)do(\mathbf{a}_{t}=\hat{\mathbf{a}}) or d​o​(𝐬t=𝐬^)do(\mathbf{s}_{t}=\hat{\mathbf{s}}), isolate causal effects by entirely severing incoming edges to a target variable (Pearl, 2009). We execute stochastic soft interventions. We perturb the underlying mechanisms by injecting controlled variance directly into the behavioral data (Appendix J).

In observational data, latent intent ui​n​t​e​n​tiu^{i}_{intent} persistently confounds agent actions. We break this confounding by applying a soft intervention to the agent’s policy (claim C1). Structurally, this operates as a definitive causal soft intervention (Eberhardt, 2008) by fundamentally altering the data-generating mechanism (see Appendix D).

3.4 Causal Identifiability and the Sequential Backdoor Condition

A causal query P⁡(y∣d​o​(x))P(y\mid do(x)) is identifiable if it is computable from the observed joint distribution given the causal graph (Pearl, 2009). We apply this to sequential decision-making to extract invariant dynamics from biased demonstrations.

Under the manipulated causal decision graph, when the Sequential Backdoor Criterion (SBC) (Zhang and Bareinboim, 2016) is satisfied, we estimate the causal effect of the action history 𝐚1:t\mathbf{a}_{1:t} on the subsequent state st+1s_{t+1} as P(st+1∣do(𝐚1:t))P(s_{t+1}\mid do(\mathbf{a}_{1:t})). is reducible to an observational expression. Therefore, the causal identifiability of the world model is asserted directly from the demonstrations.While this theorem guarantees identifiability for an isolated agent, the introduction of contemporaneous agents structurally violates the standard SBC. We detail the foundational criteria, the sequential graph surgery, and the strict necessity for a multi-agent extension in Appendix E.

4 Framework and Methodology

4.1 The Multi-Agent Sequential Backdoor Condition

To establish the structural identifiability of our Implicit Causal World Model (claim C2), we must demonstrate that the interventional distribution of the environmental transition can be uniquely computed from observational multi-agent data. Let Hk=(𝐬0:k,𝐚0:k−1)H_{k}=(\mathbf{s}_{0:k},\mathbf{a}_{0:k-1}) represent the observed history, UU the unobserved latent intent, AtiA^{i}_{t} the action of the target agent, and At−iA^{-i}_{t} the joint actions of all other agents.

We formalize the Multi-Agent Sequential Backdoor Condition (MAS-SBC) under the premise that agents operate via decentralized policies and transitions are Markovian with respect to the state-action space.

Theorem 1 (MAS-SBC).

Given the multi-agent causal decision graph 𝒢\mathcal{G}, the interventional transition dynamics P⁡(st+1∣d​o​(ati),Hk)P(s_{t+1}\mid do(a^{i}_{t}),H_{k}) can be resolved strictly through observational distributions via the sequential backdoor adjustment:

P⁡(st+1∣d​o​(ati),Hk)=∑at−iP⁡(st+1∣ati,at−i,Hk)​P​(at−i∣Hk)P(s_{t+1}\mid do(a^{i}_{t}),H_{k})=\sum_{a^{-i}_{t}}P(s_{t+1}\mid a^{i}_{t},a^{-i}_{t},H_{k})P(a^{-i}_{t}\mid H_{k})

(7)

The complete formal proof (claim C5) using Pearl’s do-calculus on the mutilated graph 𝒢A1:k−1i¯Ak+1:ti¯\mathcal{G}_{\overline{A_{1:k-1}^{i}}\underline{A_{k+1:t}^{i}}} is provided in Appendix F.

4.2 Identifiability via the MAS-SBC

Decoupling fsf_{s} from UU requires two things. Structural identifiability: Theorem 1 extends sequential adjustment by marginalizing over contemporaneous peer actions, blocking the multi-agent backdoor path Aki←U→Ak−i→sk+1A^{i}_{k}\leftarrow U\rightarrow A^{-i}_{k}\rightarrow s_{k+1}. Causal support: the adjustment cannot be evaluated on deterministic expert data, where atia^{i}_{t} is a deterministic function of UU and the two are collinear. Stochastic soft interventions (σ>0\sigma>0) break this collinearity and guarantee the positivity needed to compute interventional queries. In practice we apply these interventions independently and simultaneously to all agents, with ζ⁡(ati)⟂ζ⁡(atj)\zeta(a^{i}_{t})\perp\zeta(a^{j}_{t}).

4.3 Generalization Error Bound

To formalize the necessity of stochastic soft interventions, we establish a theoretical bound on the out-of-distribution policy evaluation error. We mathematically prove that the generalization error of the learned causal world model scales inversely with the square root of the interventional strength (σ\sigma) applied during data collection. The formal theorem (claim C4), the step-by-step derivation using the Simulation Lemma, and the discussion of its theoretical implications are provided in Appendix H.

4.4 Causal Structure Discovery

Assumptions. Our MAS formulation violates causal sufficiency by construction, since ui​n​t​e​n​tu_{intent} is unobserved. We therefore assume: (i) Strict temporal lag : the physical mechanism fsf_{s} acts with lag τ≥1\tau\geq 1, so no true causal interaction is instantaneous (Figure 7); and (ii) Contemporaneous confounding isolation : ui​n​t​e​n​tu_{intent} coordinates actions strictly at time tt and has no direct lagged effect on st+1s_{t+1} outside 𝐚t\mathbf{a}_{t}.

Structure Discovery. Under above assumptions, we treat the MAS trajectories in 𝒟σ\mathcal{D}_{\sigma} as a Vector Autoregressive (VAR) process. Since the sampled histories ℋtτ\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau} are almost IID across the dataset, we define the conditioning set as the state-action-reward history (ℋtτ)(\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}) up to a maximum lag τ\tau:

ℋtτ={(st−k,𝐚t−k,rt−k)∣1≤k≤τ}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}=\{(s_{t-k},\mathbf{a}_{t-k},r_{t-k})\mid 1\leq k\leq\tau\}

(8)

We utilize the PCMCI+ algorithm to evaluate the Conditional Mutual Information (CMI) (Wyner, 1978) and identify the directed edge set EE of the skeleton 𝒢f​u​l​l\mathcal{G}_{full}. The discovery yields two sub-graphs: Lagged discovery (El​a​gE_{lag}: edges Vt−k→VtV_{t-k}\to V_{t} spanning time steps) and contemporaneous discovery (Ec​o​n​t​e​m​pE_{contemp}: edges Vi,t→Vj,tV_{i,t}\to V_{j,t} within the same step). Under Assumption (i), invariant physical transitions lie in El​a​gE_{lag}. Assumption (ii) restricts spurious intent correlations to Ec​o​n​t​e​m​pE_{contemp}. We recover the unconfounded causal skeleton 𝒢s​k​e​l​e​t​o​n=(V,Es​k​e​l​e​t​o​n)\mathcal{G}_{skeleton}=(V,E_{skeleton}) by discarding contemporaneous dependencies:

Es​k​e​l​e​t​o​n={(Vt−k→Vt)∈El​a​g∣1≤k≤τ}E_{skeleton}=\{(V_{t-k}\to V_{t})\in E_{lag}\mid 1\leq k\leq\tau\}

Conditional independence tests in PCMCI+ assign a pp-value to each candidate edge. This yields an adjacency tensor 𝒞∈ℝ|V|×|V|×τ\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|\times|V|\times\tau} encoding these significance values (see Appendix I):

𝒟σ→PCMCI+𝒢s​k​e​l​e​t​o​n⟹𝒞∈ℝ|V|×|V|×τ\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}\xrightarrow{\text{PCMCI}^{+}}\mathcal{G}_{skeleton}\implies\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|\times|V|\times\tau}

4.5 Causal Masked World Model Architecture

We instantiate the Implicit Causal World Model (ICWM) by translating 𝒢s​k​e​l​e​t​o​n\mathcal{G}_{skeleton} edges into a tensor mask 𝐂\mathbf{C} that structurally enforces the learned SCM.

Neural Masking. Let transition function fsf_{s} be parameterized by neural network Φ\Phi with parameters θ\theta. Given input tensor 𝐱t=ℋtτ\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathcal{H}^{\tau}_{t} (history up to lag τ\tau), we satisfy the SBC/MAS-SBC by applying mask 𝐂\mathbf{C} via the Hadamard product:
s^t+1=Φ⁡(𝐱t⊙𝐂,θ)\hat{s}_{t+1}=\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}\odot\mathbf{C};\theta).
This restricts the receptive field so each prediction s^i,t+1\hat{s}_{i,t+1} depends strictly on its unconfounded parents P​a​(si,t+1)∈𝒢s​k​e​l​e​t​o​nPa(s_{i,t+1})\in\mathcal{G}_{skeleton}, isolating invariant physics.

Setup. We evaluate ICWM against five baselines (Appendix P): the associative world model AWM, the causal-pruning method FOCUS, a behavior-cloning world model BC-WM, a recurrent variant R-ICWM, and a generative C-VAE. AWM is the only graph-free architecture; every other model, ICWM included, receives the same soft PCMCI mask, a differentiable gate that weighs each input by the significance of its discovered causal links (Appendix P.2). Throughout, σ\sigma is the interventional strength of a dataset: σ=0\sigma\!=\!0 is purely observational expert data, σ→1\sigma\!\to\!1 is near-random exploration. We use two errors (Appendix Q): prediction error, the next-state MSE, divided by the next-state variance (MSE/Var) whenever we compare across environments; and graph error, the normalized spectral distance between the discovered and ground-truth causal graphs.

Domains. We use three multi-agent coordination domains (Figure 24) with high inter-agent confounding:

•

Two-Door: a room with a red and a blue door; agents must open the red door then the blue door, in order, and are rewarded for fast joint completion.

•

Navigation: multi-agent pathfinding where collision avoidance and reaching shared goals couple the agents strategically.

•

Giveway: two agents on opposite ends of a corridor too narrow to pass; they must negotiate a recessed notch to reach opposing goals without colliding.

Models train on datasets 𝒟σN\mathcal{D}^{N}_{\sigma} of NN episodes at interventional strength σ\sigma (Appendix S, R), and are evaluated on held-out biased-optimal (σ=0.5\sigma\!=\!0.5), random (σ=0.9\sigma\!=\!0.9), and adversarial policies.

We organize the results around five questions. Figure 3 answers the first three: (i) do soft interventions recover the true causal structure (claim C3), (ii) does the learnt model transfer out of distribution (claim C6), and (iii) what is responsible for that transfer (claim C5)? Figure 4 and Figure 5 answers the remaining two: (iv) where does the lever stop working and what exactly breaks?, and (v) does memory help, and where?

Q(i)(i). Figure 3A scores PC and PCMCI discovery against the ground-truth graph as σ\sigma grows. Graph error falls steadily in every environment, under both full and partial observation, and the trend survives scaling: with 22, 33, and 44 navigation agents the recovered graphs improve in the same way. In simple terms, adding randomness to the demonstrations is what turns observational data into an identifiable causal graph; without it (σ≈0\sigma\!\approx\!0) discovery is at its worst everywhere. Detailed per-metric results are in Appendix Z.

Q(i​i)(ii). We evaluate every trained model on an adversarial policy far outside the demonstration distribution (Figure 3B). On all three environments the variance-normalized error falls monotonically as training σ\sigma grows, by up to two orders of magnitude on navigation, matching our bound that OOD error scales inversely with intervention. The causal world models (ICWM, AWM) beat the generative C-VAE by a wide margin throughout.

The models also reason sensibly under this shift. Fitting a predictive density around each frozen model and comparing its per-axis widths OOD against in-distribution (Figure 3C), uncertainty inflates on only a minority of state dimensions while most stay at their in-distribution width: the models localize what they no longer know instead of becoming uniformly uncertain. The full cloud-width study is in Figure 49.

Q(i​i​i)(iii). Across every environment and both sample regimes, models handed better graphs also predict better out of distribution (Figure 3D; pooled within-cell Spearman ρ=+0.62\rho\!=\!+0.62). It is tempting to read this as “better graph ⇒\Rightarrow better model.” The data say otherwise (Figure 3E): σ\sigma drives graph error and OOD error down together, and once we condition on σ\sigma the correlation vanishes (ρ≈0\rho\!\approx\!0), with the graph-free AWM matching ICWM at matched data. The average-intervention trajectories in Figure 3D make this concrete: moving from σ=0.01\sigma\!=\!0.01 to σ=0.99\sigma\!=\!0.99 slides each model down and to the left, improving graph error and OOD error jointly. The interventional richness of the demonstrations is the lever; the recovered graph is its by-product. The graph still earns its keep where information is missing, under partial observation and layout extrapolation (Appendix Z), and as a prior that must not be wrong: corrupting it inflates error in every environment group (Appendix V).

Q(i​v)(iv). Where does the lever stop? Here we move the environment instead of the policy. We run the frozen models, without retraining, on seven altered layouts (Appendix N) and score each against its own error on the training layout, where ρ=1\rho\!=\!1 means lossless transfer (Figure 4A). The headline now inverts. On the three layouts that rearrange space, ρdyn\rho_{\mathrm{dyn}} rises with intervention, from a median of 1.9×1.9\times at σ=0.01\sigma\!=\!0.01 to 5.3×5.3\times at σ=0.99\sigma\!=\!0.99 (n=96n\!=\!96 paired conditions, 93%93\% worse, Wilcoxon p<10−14p\!<\!10^{-14}), and this holds for every architecture, ICWM and graph-free AWM alike. The reason is simple: exploratory data widens coverage only within the training geometry, so it buys nothing once the geometry itself moves. Three shifts isolate what breaks, each holding the geometry fixed and changing one factor. Wrap changes only the transition rule and stays at ≈1.0×\approx\!1.0\times for every σ\sigma (Mann–Whitney p<10−6p\!<\!10^{-6} against the space family). This is the decisive control: it shows the large ratios come from unfamiliar coordinates, not from a fragile dynamics model. Easy Give-Way lifts position error while its dynamics error falls (ρpos/ρdyn=4.2\rho_{\mathrm{pos}}/\rho_{\mathrm{dyn}}\!=\!4.2), and Anti-Causal leaves both near 1.01.0 (p<10−24p\!<\!10^{-24} between the two); it is in fact easier than the source, because shutting the red door confines the agent (Figure 4E,F).

Figure 5: Velocity error, R-ICWM minus ICWM (log10). Below 00 means R-ICWM is
better. Its edge grows as the trained speed of a region drops, in all three layouts (Source, Easy, Mirror).

Being off-manifold is not automatically harder. What matters is whether the changed mechanism is one the model has to predict. The mask still earns its keep here: corrupting it inflates error in all six groups (median 1.161.16 to 17.9×17.9\times, worst as σ→1\sigma\!\to\!1; Figure 4B,C). Scale is not the obstacle either. On 2/3/42/3/4-agent navigation, ICWM and AWM hold a ∼10−3\sim\!10^{-3} adversarial error as agents are added (Figure 4D), orders below C-VAE. The honest boundary is this: soft intervention identifies dynamics within a geometry, but not spatial extrapolation.

Q(v)(v). R-ICWM is ICWM plus a recurrent state, so it
should pay off where a single frame says least. We split the give-way corridor into three
regions and measure how fast agents actually moved there in training: the Wall is
fastest, the Connector at the bay’s mouth slower, and the Notch inside the
bay slowest, since agents only pause there to let the other pass. Comparing the two models
on the velocity part of the error alone (Figure 5), the gap tracks speed,
not the layout: in the fast Wall the models are even, and R-ICWM’s edge grows
through the Connector to the slow Notch, where it is one to three orders
of magnitude better. The pattern survives both give-way shifts, and is largest under
Mirror. Memory buys little when motion is fast and smooth, and a lot where it is
slow and stop-start. This is visible only in the velocity channel; the mixed dynamics error
averages it away (Appendix Y.7).

6 CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS

We introduce ICWM, which learns world models from multi-agent demonstrations collected under soft interventions. Our central finding is that interventional strength σ\sigma, small purposeful randomness in agent actions, is the lever on both causal-structure recovery and out-of-distribution accuracy: as it grows, discovered graphs sharpen and adversarial error falls by orders of magnitude, persisting as agents are added. The graph pays off where information is missing: on the stretched two-door layout (partial observation), ICWM reaches 0.53×0.53\times the graph-free AWM error, BC-WM 0.76×0.76\times, and FOCUS 0.80×0.80\times, while a corrupted graph is worse than none (Appendices Z, V). The lever has a precise boundary: rearranging the layout makes transfer error grow with σ\sigma (1.9×1.9\times to 5.3×5.3\times), while changing only the transition rule transfers at ≈1.0×\approx\!1.0\times, so σ\sigma identifies dynamics within a geometry, not spatial extrapolation (Figure 4, Appendix Y). Two limitations follow. First, the requirement for random actions can break the very cooperation the model needs to observe in tightly coordinated settings (most visibly in Giveway), motivating targeted rather than uniform interventions (Appendix J). Second, our guarantees do not extend to layout changes, and the model alone cannot tell a practitioner which regime a new deployment falls into.

Future work. Connecting these justified world models to causal imitation and causal Q-learning (Méndez-Molina et al., 2020), and to the steerability gap of associative models (Vafa et al., 2025a), is a natural next step. Beyond this, causally grounded world models are a prerequisite for runtime verification and self-correcting agentic systems: a model correct about which variables drive a transition can both screen proposed actions before execution, in the spirit of shielding (Alshiekh et al., 2017), and supply the error signal for gradient-free self-revision (Shinn et al., 2023).

Ethics Statement

This work studies learned
world models in simulated multi-agent environments. It involves no human
subjects, no personally identifiable information, and no real-world data, since
all demonstrations are generated programmatically in open-source simulators.
One point is worth stating clearly. Our method collects data under soft
interventions, which means deliberately adding randomness to an agent’s actions.
This is harmless in simulation, but in a robotic or safety-critical setting it
would mean perturbing a system whose failures carry real cost. We therefore do
not recommend applying uniform interventions outside simulation without a
separate safety layer, and we treat reducing the required intervention budget as
a safety concern and not only a question of efficiency. We declare no conflicts
of interest or sponsorship that influenced this work.

Appendix B Structural Causal Model Formalization

The transition dynamics of a Multi-Agent System are governed by the interplay between invariant environment rules and adaptive agent strategies. We formalize this via an SCM ℳ=⟨𝐔,𝐕,ℱ,P⁡(𝐔)⟩\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V},\mathcal{F},P(\mathbf{U})\rangle:

B.1 Exogenous and Endogenous Partitioning

The variables in our system are partitioned into two sets:

1.

Exogenous Variables (𝐔\mathbf{U}): These are variables determined outside the model. In our framework, we distinguish between Environmental Stochasticity (ue​n​v∼Ue​n​vu_{env}\sim U_{env}), which captures irreducible noise in physics (e.g., sensor noise or friction or stochastic in environment properties), and Unobserved Strategic Intents for agent ii, (ui​n​t​e​n​ti∼Ui​n​t​e​n​tu^{i}_{intent}\sim U_{intent}), which represent the high-level goals or coordination signals that drive agent behaviors but are not explicitly captured in the state space.

2.

Endogenous Variables (𝐕\mathbf{V}): These are the observable variables determined by the structural functions. For a single transition step (t)(t), these include the current environment state st∈𝒮=𝒮1×⋯×𝒮ns_{t}\in\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}^{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{S}^{n}, the joint action at∈𝒜=𝒜1×⋯×𝒜na_{t}\in\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}^{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{A}^{n}, the reward rt∈ℛ=ℛ1×⋯×ℛnr_{t}\in\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}^{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{R}^{n}, and the resulting next state st+1∈𝒮s_{t+1}\in\mathcal{S}. Where 𝒮\mathcal{S} is the state space, 𝒮i\mathcal{S}^{i} is the state space for agent ii, 𝒜\mathcal{A} is the action space, 𝒜i\mathcal{A}^{i} is the action space for agent ii, ℛ\mathcal{R} is the reward space, ℛi\mathcal{R}^{i} is the reward space for agent ii, and nn number of agents in the multi agent system.

B.2 Structural Functions and Dependencies

The set ℱ\mathcal{F} ={fs,fr​fa1,…,fan}=\{f_{s},f_{r}f_{a^{1}},\dots,f_{a^{n}}\} defines the causal mechanisms of the world. By expressing these as deterministic functions of their parents and noise, we can model the system as a directed graph where edges represent functional dependencies:

1.

Environment Physics (fsf_{s}): This function is the primary target of our world model. It maps the current state and joint actions to the next state. Critically, fsf_{s} is policy-invariant; it represents the underlying ”rules of the game” that remain constant regardless of whether the agents are acting optimally, randomly, or adversarially.

•

Quantity: P⁡(st+1∣st,𝐚t)P(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t})

•

Relationship: st+1←fs​(st,𝐚t,ue​n​v)s_{t+1}\leftarrow f_{s}(s_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t},u_{env})

2.

Reward Model (frf_{r}): This maps the state-action pair to a feedback signal. In MAS, rewards are often highly confounded because expert agents only receive high rewards by following specific, intent-driven trajectories.

•

Quantity: P⁡(rt+1∣st,𝐚t)P(r_{t+1}\mid s_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t})

•

Relationship: rt+1←fr​(st,𝐚t,ue​n​v)r_{t+1}\leftarrow f_{r}(s_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t},u_{env})

3.

Agent Policies (faif_{a^{i}}): Unlike fsf_{s}, the policy functions are agent-dependent. They are conditioned on the state sts_{t} and the latent intent ui​n​t​e​n​tiu^{i}_{intent}. This structure is the root of causal confusion: in expert data, the correlation between sts_{t}, ui​n​t​e​n​tiu^{i}_{intent}, and st+1s_{t+1} is often so strong that standard associative models cannot determine if st+1s_{t+1} was caused by the physics of sts_{t} or the specific intent-driven choice of atia^{i}_{t}.

•

Quantity: πi​(ai|st)\pi^{i}(a^{i}|s_{t}), where πi\pi^{i} is it​hi^{th} agents policy.

•

Relationship: ati←fai​(st,ui​n​t​e​n​ti)a^{i}_{t}\leftarrow f_{a^{i}}(s_{t},u^{i}_{intent}), the following relation is estabished from prior work (Lowe et al., 2020; Dasgupta et al., 2019)

B.3 The Role of P⁡(U)P(U)

The joint probability distribution P⁡(𝐔)P(\mathbf{U}) induces the statistical distributions in the demonstration dataset. We distinguish between these exogenous variables as follows:

1.

Environmental Stochasticity (ue​n​vu_{env}): Represents irreducible noise (e.g., friction, jitter, weather conditions). We assume ue​n​v⟂⟂ui​n​t​e​n​tu_{env}\perp\!\!\perp u_{intent}. This term dictates predictive variance (kernel thickness or uncertainty in the model’s output), which we do not explicitly deconfound. We assume ue​n​vu_{env} is captured by dataset variance and offer no solution for shifts in its distribution, focusing solely on structural invariance. Our position is that under a significant distribution shift of ue​n​vu_{env}, the world dynamics themselves have likely shifted so fundamentally that knowledge reuse from the previous model would yield diminishing returns.

2.

Strategic Confounding (ui​n​t​e​n​tiu^{i}_{intent}): Represents unobserved coordination signals that correlate with sts_{t} in expert data, creating backdoor paths (st←ui​n​t​e​n​ti→ati→st+1s_{t}\leftarrow u^{i}_{intent}\rightarrow a^{i}_{t}\rightarrow s_{t+1}) that cause associative models to mistake agent choice for physical law. We address this by leveraging policy variance (soft interventions) to satisfy the Sequential Backdoor Condition discovering true transition rules fsf_{s}.

To evaluate the causal impact of actions over a trajectory, we perform graph surgery of the causal decision graph 𝒢\mathcal{G} to create a manipulated graph 𝒢𝐚¯1:k−1,𝐚¯k+1:t\mathcal{G}_{\overline{\mathbf{a}}_{1:k-1},\underline{\mathbf{a}}_{k+1:t}} (Figure 9). This graph surgery isolates the forward-directed causal effect of the current action on the environment from backward-flowing statistical correlations.
